# Direction: concepts, glossary and accuracy notes

## Where direction comes from

- **Maitland Graves**, *The Art of Color and Design* (1941; 2nd ed. 1951), lists seven elements: **line, direction, shape, size, texture, value, colour**. Direction is its own element there, and many design courses, textbooks and teacher resources follow that list.
- Other lists (e.g. the common "line, shape, form, space, texture, value, colour") fold direction into **line**. Both are fine. Tell students that different sources group the elements differently, and direction is still being used whether or not it is on the list.
- In NSW syllabuses, direction is not listed on its own in every course. Use it as an element where the course or school scope lists it, and otherwise as part of line, composition and "the way the viewer's eye moves" (e.g. Visual Design and Visual Arts frames and conventions, and Graphics layout principles). Check the current syllabus wording before quoting it.

## The core directions and what they suggest

The meanings come from the body and gravity. They are tendencies that context can override, not fixed rules.

| Direction | Physical anchor | Usual associations | Typical uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Horizontal** | Lying down, the horizon, still water | Calm, rest, stability, peace, width, space, permanence | Landscapes, wellness and finance brands, luxury layouts, banners, calm websites |
| **Vertical** | Standing up, trees, columns, towers | Strength, alertness, dignity, formality, height, growth, aspiration, power | Architecture, corporate and prestige brands, portrait posters, elongating garments |
| **Diagonal / oblique** | Leaning, falling, running, a ramp | Movement, action, speed, energy, tension, instability, excitement, depth | Sport, action films, youth brands, sale graphics, dynamic photography, "Dutch angle" |
| **Curved / curvilinear** | Water, bodies, hills, a thrown ball | Flow, grace, gentleness, softness, continuity, rhythm | Organic, caring and playful brands; S-curves in landscapes and figures |
| **Circular / spiral** | Orbits, wheels, shells | Completion, unity, focus, endless motion, pulling inward | Logos, focal framing, spiral compositions |
| **Radial** | Sun rays, explosions, spokes | Energy spreading outward (or converging inward), emphasis on the centre | Burst graphics, comic "action lines", one-point perspective |

**Direction and mood together:** a strongly horizontal scene can feel lonely or bleak rather than calm, and a vertical layout can feel oppressive rather than dignified. Always read direction together with colour, value, scale and subject.

**Steep vs shallow diagonals:** a shallow diagonal suggests gentle movement or a slow climb. A steep diagonal (near 45° or more) suggests speed, danger or a fall. Rising lines (bottom-left to top-right, for left-to-right readers) tend to read as growth, progress or optimism. Falling lines (top-left to bottom-right) tend to read as decline or descent. Line charts and "arrow up" logos rely on this.

## Actual vs implied direction

- **Actual direction:** a line, edge or shape that is physically there: a road, a pole, a stripe, the edge of a building, a rotated text box.
- **Implied direction:** the eye connects things that are not joined. The main types are:
  - **Gaze:** we look where a figure looks. This is one of the strongest cues.
  - **Pointing and gesture:** hands, fingers, limbs, a character's movement.
  - **Arrows, chevrons and wedges:** signs we are taught to follow.
  - **Leading lines:** roads, rails, rivers, fences, shadows that run towards the subject.
  - **Repetition and sequence:** a row of repeated shapes forms a path (rhythm), and so do numbered steps and comic panels.
  - **Gradation:** shapes getting smaller, colours getting lighter, or type stepping down in size carry the eye along.
  - **Orientation of shapes:** a triangle "points", an oval "lies" or "stands", a leaning figure "falls".
  - **Motion cues:** blur, speed lines, trailing shapes, the side of a moving object with the "space ahead".
  - **Reading order:** text is followed in the reading direction of the viewer's language.

## Directional force and visual weight

- **Directional force:** the pull that a line, gaze or shape exerts along its direction. Rudolf Arnheim (*Art and Visual Perception*, 1954/1974) describes compositions as fields of these forces.
- **Visual weight:** how strongly an element attracts attention (size, contrast, colour, isolation, faces, text). Weight decides *where the eye lands*. Direction decides *where it goes next*. Students often confuse the two.
- **Balance with direction:** an element facing or moving into space needs room in front of it ("lead room", "nose room" or "looking room" in photography and film). A subject pressed against the edge it faces feels cramped or trapped, which is sometimes deliberate.

## Reading direction and reading patterns

- **Left-to-right cultures** (English and most European languages) tend to scan from the top-left and read rightward movement as "forward", "progress" or "the future". Research on the "spatial agency bias" supports this tendency, and it is weaker or reversed in right-to-left readers (Arabic, Hebrew). Traditional Japanese and Chinese text can run vertically, and manga reads right to left. Mention this when designing for multilingual audiences, and when flipping layouts for right-to-left languages.
- **Z pattern:** in sparse layouts (posters, ads, landing pages with little text) the eye tends to go top-left, across to top-right, diagonally down to bottom-left, then across to bottom-right. Designers put the logo or headline at the start and the call to action at the end.
- **F pattern:** on text-heavy web pages, eye-tracking studies by the Nielsen Norman Group (from 2006) showed readers scanning across the top, across again a little lower, then down the left side. It is a sign of *skimming*, not a layout to copy. Good headings, front-loaded words and bullet points work with it.
- **Gutenberg diagram:** for evenly weighted, dense layouts (associated with newspaper designer Edmund Arnold), attention starts at the top-left "primary optical area" and ends at the bottom-right "terminal area". The top-right and bottom-left get less attention.
- These patterns describe default habits. Strong emphasis, faces, colour and images override them.

## Direction and the principles of design

| Principle | How direction creates it |
|---|---|
| **Movement** | The eye path itself: implied and actual lines leading the viewer through the work in a planned order. |
| **Rhythm** | Repeating a direction (stripes, rows, columns) at regular or progressive intervals. |
| **Emphasis** | Converging lines (perspective, radial bursts, gazes) pointing at a focal point, or one opposing direction in a uniform field. |
| **Balance** | Directional forces pulling one way can be balanced by forces or weight on the other side. |
| **Unity / harmony** | One dominant direction shared by most elements. |
| **Contrast / variety** | Setting opposing directions against each other (horizontal vs vertical, straight vs curved, a single diagonal). |
| **Proportion / scale** | Vertical emphasis makes things look taller and narrower. Horizontal emphasis makes them look wider and lower (used in fashion and interiors). |

## Direction across subjects

- **Visual Arts and Visual Design:** compositional lines, the Dutch angle, the golden-spiral argument (treat it cautiously; the claims are often overstated), perspective orthogonals, gesture.
- **Graphics Technology:** layout grids, text rotation, arrows in instructions and wayfinding, leader lines on technical drawings, flow direction in diagrams and flowcharts, isometric axes at 30°.
- **Multimedia, Photography and Film:** leading lines, lead room, canted/Dutch angle for unease, camera movement (pan = horizontal, tilt = vertical), screen direction and the 180° rule (keeping a character moving the same way across shots), UI scroll and swipe direction.
- **Textiles:** vertical stripes and seams elongate the body, horizontal lines widen it (a tendency, and the evidence is mixed, e.g. the Helmholtz square illusion suggests horizontal stripes can look *narrower*). Other examples are bias cut (diagonal to the grain) for drape, pattern-piece direction, nap and print direction.
- **D&T and product design:** directional cues in form (a car's wedge shape "points forward"), affordances (a handle's direction shows how to hold it), and the grain direction of timber.

## Don't confuse

- **Direction vs line:** a line *has* direction, but direction also comes from edges, shapes, gazes and sequences with no drawn line.
- **Direction vs movement:** direction is an element (the orientation). Movement is a principle (the path the eye takes because of the directions and weights).
- **Actual vs implied direction:** physically there, or completed by the viewer's eye.
- **Directional force vs visual weight:** where the eye is pushed, vs how strongly something grabs attention.
- **Horizontal format vs horizontal direction:** a landscape-format page can still be full of vertical lines.

## Accuracy notes

- The associations (horizontal = calm, etc.) are **conventions based on experience, not laws**. Say "tends to suggest".
- The claim that vertical stripes always make you look slimmer is **not reliably supported**. Present it as contested.
- "People always read in a Z" is too strong. Reading patterns depend on the layout, the task and the culture.
- The golden spiral is often "found" by forcing the overlay. Don't present it as proof that a design works.
- NN/g's F-pattern research warns against designing *for* the F. It shows what happens when content is poorly structured.

## Glossary (student-friendly)

| Term | Plain definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | The way a line, shape or path points or travels | A road running into the distance |
| Horizontal | Side to side, parallel to the horizon | A calm sea horizon |
| Vertical | Straight up and down | A skyscraper |
| Diagonal / oblique | Slanting, neither horizontal nor vertical | A skier leaning into a turn |
| Curvilinear | Made of curves | A winding river |
| Radial | Spreading out from, or into, a centre | Sun rays, a spoked wheel |
| Actual line | A line that is physically drawn or present | The edge of a table |
| Implied line | A line the eye completes between separate things | A character looking at a door |
| Leading line | A line that guides the eye to the subject | Train tracks meeting at a station |
| Eye path | The route the viewer's eye takes through a work | Headline, then image, then logo |
| Directional force | The pull a line, gaze or shape exerts | An arrow pointing at a price |
| Lead room | Space left in front of a subject that faces or moves | A runner with space ahead |
| Dutch (canted) angle | A deliberately tilted frame | A horror film shot tilted 25° |
| Z pattern | Scanning top-left, top-right, diagonal, bottom | A sparse poster |
| F pattern | Scanning a text-heavy page in two bars and down the left | A news website |
