# Analysing and critiquing direction

## Checklist

Work through it, then write up only what matters for this work.

1. **Dominant direction.** Which orientation carries the most edges and the largest shapes? Estimate proportions roughly (e.g. "mostly vertical, about 60%").
2. **Contrasting direction.** Is there one element that goes against the rest? Is it the focal point? Should it be?
3. **Entry point.** Where does the eye land first, and why (size, contrast, face, colour, isolation)?
4. **Eye path.** List the order the eye travels: 1, 2, 3, 4. Name the cue that moves it each time (gaze, arrow, leading line, repetition, gradation, reading order).
5. **Exit or loop.** Does the path end on the most important item (call to action, logo, title), loop back into the work, or leak out of the frame?
6. **Reading pattern.** For text-led layouts: Z, F or Gutenberg? Does the placement of the headline, image and call to action suit it? Who is the audience, and what is their reading direction?
7. **Lead room and facing.** Do faces, figures, vehicles and arrows face *into* the layout, with space in front of them?
8. **Tilts.** Are angled elements clearly deliberate? Is any body text rotated so much that it is hard to read?
9. **Tangents and accidental lines.** Do edges touch or line up by accident, creating unwanted directional pulls (e.g. a horizon cutting through a head, a pole "growing" out of someone)?
10. **Fit to purpose.** Does the direction suit the brief, the brand and the mood?

## Common problems and fixes

| Problem | Why it hurts | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Subject looks or moves out of the frame | The eye follows it off the page | Flip the image, or move the subject to the other side so it faces inward |
| Arrow or leading line points at nothing, or at the edge | Wasted directional force | Point it at the key message or call to action |
| Too many competing diagonals | Chaos, no clear path | Keep one dominant diagonal and level the rest |
| Everything horizontal, nothing leads | Static, the eye doesn't know where to go | Add one vertical or diagonal accent at the focal point |
| Slight accidental tilt (1–3°) | Looks like an error | Straighten it, or commit to a clear angle |
| Rotated body text | Slow and tiring to read | Keep body text level and rotate only short display text |
| Call to action in a "dead" area (top-right or bottom-left of a dense layout) | Gets less attention | Move it to the end of the eye path (usually bottom-right for left-to-right readers) |
| Horizon line through the middle | Splits the work in two, and neither half dominates | Move the horizon to about one-third or two-thirds of the height |
| Stripes or pattern running the "wrong" way on a garment or product | Changes perceived proportion or looks unfinished | Check the grain and print direction, and match pattern at the seams |

## Model annotations

Use the pattern *identify, then describe, then explain the effect, then evaluate*. Label anything written in a student's voice as a model.

**Film poster (action):**
> The dominant direction is diagonal. The hero's body, the motorbike and the title are all angled at about 30°, rising from bottom-left to top-right. Because diagonals suggest instability and speed, the poster feels urgent and dangerous, which suits an action film. The rising angle also reads as forward momentum for a left-to-right audience. The only horizontal element is the release date strip at the bottom, which gives the eye a stable place to finish. This is effective, though the tagline is rotated too far to read quickly.

**Landscape photograph:**
> The horizon is a strong horizontal line placed low, at about one-third of the height, so the sky dominates and the image feels open and calm. A winding path (a curvilinear leading line) starts in the bottom-left corner and leads the eye up to the cabin, which is the focal point. The single vertical of the cabin's chimney contrasts with the horizontals and stops the eye there.

**Web landing page (Graphics / Multimedia):**
> The layout follows a Z pattern: logo top-left, navigation top-right, then a diagonal pull from the hero image, whose model gazes down to the right, to the "Start free trial" button at the bottom-right. The gaze is an implied line that delivers the viewer to the call to action. To improve it, the secondary button on the left could be removed, because it interrupts the diagonal.

**Garment (Textiles):**
> The dress uses vertical princess seams and a vertical stripe to emphasise height and create a long, elegant silhouette. The bias-cut skirt panel introduces a diagonal grain that lets the fabric drape and swing when the wearer moves, so direction affects both how the garment looks and how it behaves.

## Feedback to students

Be kind and specific: one strength, one fix, and why. For example:
> "Your runner faces right with plenty of space ahead, so the poster feels like it's moving forward. Great. Your sponsor logos sit in a diagonal line that pulls away from the title, though. Try lining them up horizontally along the bottom so the eye ends there."
