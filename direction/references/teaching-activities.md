# Teaching direction: activities and sequence

For NSW Stage 4–6 Visual Arts, Visual Design, Graphics Technology, Multimedia, Photographic and Digital Media, Textiles and D&T. Adapt each activity to the course and the stage. Each one lists the concept, materials, steps and an extension.

## Pick the right activity

1. Identify the **outcome type**:
   - *Know or understand* (vocabulary, meanings).
   - *Analyse or evaluate* (read a work).
   - *Apply or decide* (use direction for a brief).
   - *Make* (produce a design or photograph).
2. Choose an activity below, then add **retrieval** (hinge question, 3 quick-recall questions) and a **success criterion**, e.g. "I can explain how the dominant direction in a design creates its mood."
3. Differentiate:
   - **Support:** word bank (horizontal, vertical, diagonal, curved, radial, implied line, leading line, eye path, dominant, contrast), the body-pose anchor, and sentence stems: "The dominant direction is ___, shown by ___. This makes the viewer feel ___ because ___."
   - **Extension:** directional force and visual weight (Arnheim), the spatial agency bias and right-to-left layouts, the 180° rule, testing the vertical-stripes myth.

## Activities

### 1. Body directions (hook, Stage 4–5, 10 min)
- **Concept:** the meanings of the directions come from gravity and the body.
- **Steps:** Students lie flat, stand tall, then lean as if falling or running. Ask how each pose feels (rested, alert, off-balance). Record the words on the board under Horizontal, Vertical and Diagonal. Reveal that designers use exactly these feelings.
- **Extension:** Add curved (a relaxed slouch, a wave) and radial (star jump).

### 2. Three-line mood test (Stage 4–6, 15 min)
- **Concept:** direction alone communicates mood.
- **Materials:** paper divided into 6 boxes, or a Canva/Express template.
- **Steps:** Using only straight or curved lines (no pictures, no words), fill each box with a mood: calm, angry, proud, falling, joyful, sad. Swap with a partner, who guesses the moods and names the directions used.
- **Extension:** Repeat with a limit of three lines per box.

### 3. Tilt it (Stage 4–6, 15 min)
- **Concept:** the same image changes meaning when rotated.
- **Steps:** Take one ordinary photo (a friend, a building). Make three versions: level, a slight 3° tilt, a strong 25° Dutch angle. Discuss which looks like a mistake and which looks deliberate, and what mood the strong tilt creates.
- **Tool option:** Adobe `image_auto_straighten` to show the "fixed" version. Use Canva or Express rotate for the Dutch angle.

### 4. Eye-path tracing (Stage 5–6, 20 min)
- **Concept:** implied direction, eye path and reading patterns.
- **Materials:** 3–4 posters or ads (film posters work well), tracing paper or an annotation layer.
- **Steps:** Students number where their eye goes (1, 2, 3, 4) and draw arrows showing *what* moved it (gaze, arrow, leading line, repetition, reading order). Compare paths between students. Identify Z, F or Gutenberg patterns.
- **Extension:** Flip a poster horizontally and retrace it. Discuss what changed and why (reading direction).

### 5. Gaze and lead room (Stage 5–6, Multimedia, Photography, Visual Design, 20 min)
- **Concept:** gaze is an implied line, and subjects need space in front of them.
- **Steps:** In pairs, photograph a partner looking left, with space on the left of the frame, then with space on the right. Compare the two. Then place a text block where the subject looks.
- **Extension:** Film a short walk across the frame in two shots, obeying and then breaking the 180° rule. Discuss the confusion.

### 6. Leading lines photo hunt (Stage 4–6, 30–40 min)
- **Concept:** actual lines leading to a focal point.
- **Steps:** Find and photograph around the school: one strong horizontal, one vertical, one diagonal, one curved, one radial or converging set of lines, and one leading line that ends at a subject. Annotate each with the direction and its effect.
- **Tool option:** Unsplash search for comparison images ("leading lines", "spiral staircase", "long exposure road").

### 7. Fix the layout (Stage 5–6, Graphics, Multimedia, Visual Design, 25 min)
- **Concept:** critique and redesign for direction.
- **Materials:** a deliberately broken poster or web banner. The model looks off the page, an arrow points at nothing, the call to action sits top-right, and one text box is tilted 2°.
- **Steps:** Students list every direction problem using the checklist in `analysis-and-critique.md`, then fix it (in Canva on a copy, or by redrawing a sketch). They write a short justification using the annotation pattern.

### 8. Stripe direction test (Stage 5–6, Textiles, 20 min)
- **Concept:** direction and perceived proportion. It is also a lesson in testing claims.
- **Steps:** Put identical rectangles or croquis in vertical, horizontal and diagonal stripes. Survey the class on which looks tallest or widest. Compare the results with the common "vertical stripes slim you" claim and the Helmholtz illusion. Then discuss bias cut: cut two fabric strips, one on the straight grain and one on the bias, and compare the stretch and drape.

### 9. Direction in brand marks (Stage 5–6, Graphics, Visual Design, 20 min)
- **Concept:** direction in logos communicates values.
- **Steps:** Sort a set of logos by dominant direction: rising diagonal (growth, e.g. finance and sport), horizontal (stable), vertical (prestige), circular (unity, community). Discuss why each brand chose that direction. Students then sketch three logo thumbnails for a fictional brand, one with each direction, and justify the best one.

## Suggested sequence (about 4–5 lessons)

| # | Focus | Core activity |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | What direction is; the three core directions and moods | Body directions (1), then the three-line mood test (2) |
| 2 | Curved, radial and diagonal energy; tilts | Tilt it (3), then direction in brand marks (9) |
| 3 | Implied direction, eye path and reading patterns | Eye-path tracing (4), then gaze and lead room (5) |
| 4 | Direction in the real world | Leading lines photo hunt (6), or the stripe test (8) for Textiles |
| 5 | Apply and evaluate | Fix the layout (7), then a short written justification |

## Retrieval questions (with answers)

1. Which direction usually suggests calm and stability? *Horizontal.*
2. Name two ways to create an implied line. *Any two of: gaze, pointing, arrows, repetition, gradation, reading order, leading lines.*
3. Why should a subject usually face into the layout? *The viewer follows the gaze or movement. Facing out sends the eye off the page.*
4. What is the difference between direction and movement? *Direction is the orientation of elements (an element). Movement is the path the eye takes through the work (a principle).*
5. Hinge question: A poster for a meditation app uses a runner leaning at 40°, jagged diagonal text and a red burst. What is the main problem? *(a) too horizontal (b) the directions suggest energy and tension, not calm (c) there are no implied lines (d) it follows a Z pattern.* **Answer: b.**
