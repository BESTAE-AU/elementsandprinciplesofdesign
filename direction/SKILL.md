# Direction (element of design)

Direction is **the way something points or travels**: the orientation of a line, edge, shape or form, and the path the viewer's eye is pulled along. Every mark has direction, so it is always present. The design question is whether it is *controlled*.

Full definitions, meanings, sources and "don't confuse with" notes are in `references/concepts.md`. Read it before writing notes, a glossary or anything longer than a one-line definition. Analysis and critique method is in `references/analysis-and-critique.md`. Lessons and activities are in `references/teaching-activities.md`.

## Explain

1. **Find the level.** Stage 4: plain words and a body analogy ("lying down, standing up, falling over"). Stage 5: the correct term, the three core directions and one reason each matters. Stage 6 and teachers: actual vs implied direction, directional force, reading patterns, and links to the principles.
2. **Anchor to the body.** The meanings come from how we experience gravity:
   - **Horizontal** = lying down or the horizon: rest, calm, stability, space, width.
   - **Vertical** = standing up, trees, columns: strength, alertness, formality, height, growth, aspiration.
   - **Diagonal / oblique** = falling, running, leaning: movement, action, energy, tension, instability, depth.
   - **Curved / circular / radial** = water, orbits, bursts: flow, gentleness, rhythm, continuity, or energy spreading outward.
3. **Show, don't just tell.** Offer a tiny sketch, an ASCII diagram, or "tilt your phone 20° and look at the photo again".
4. **Separate the look-alike terms** (see `references/concepts.md`): *direction* vs *line*, *direction* vs *movement*, *actual* vs *implied* direction, *directional force* vs *visual weight*.
5. **Finish with a check:** one hinge question or 2–3 quick-recall questions, with answers.

## Analyse

1. **Get the work.** Use the image the person shares. If it lives in Canva, Adobe, Dropbox or Google Drive, pull it through that connector rather than asking for a screenshot.
2. **Find the dominant direction.** Squint, or imagine the work blurred. Which orientation carries the most edges and the biggest shapes: horizontal, vertical, diagonal or curved? Estimate it roughly ("mostly vertical, about 60%, with one strong diagonal").
3. **Trace the eye path.** Where does the eye land first (the point of emphasis)? What sends it on? Look for actual lines, and for implied lines: gaze, pointing, arrows, leading lines, repeated shapes, graded size or colour, the reading order of text. Where does it leave, or loop back?
4. **Check the reading pattern.** Text-led layouts: does it suit a Z pattern (sparse posters, ads), an F pattern (text-heavy web pages), or the Gutenberg diagram (even, dense layouts)? Remember the culture's reading direction (see `concepts.md`).
5. **Interpret.** Explain what mood or meaning the directions create and *how*: evidence first, then the effect.
6. **Evaluate and suggest.** What works, what fights (competing directions, an eye path that exits the frame, a subject looking or moving out of the page, accidental tangents, a tilt that looks like a mistake), and one or two specific fixes.

Use the checklist and model annotations in `references/analysis-and-critique.md`.

## Apply (for someone designing)

1. **Start from the brief's mood.** Calm, trustworthy or luxurious work usually leans horizontal. Authority, prestige, growth or height leans vertical. Sport, action, urgency, youth or disruption leans diagonal. Organic, playful, caring or continuous leans curved.
2. **Pick one dominant direction and one contrast.** Dominance gives unity. A single opposing direction gives emphasis, e.g. one diagonal in a calm horizontal layout.
3. **Plan the eye path before the details.** Point of emphasis first, then secondary information, then the call to action or logo. Point gazes, arrows and leading lines *into* the layout and towards the next item, never off the edge.
4. **Make tilts deliberate.** Tilt far enough to read as a decision (roughly 10–45°). A 1–3° tilt reads as a mistake. Keep text you need people to read fairly level.
5. **Check it.** Use the squint test, flip the design horizontally to see it fresh, and ask someone new where they looked first, second and third.

## Writing it up

- **Quick:** name the dominant direction, give the evidence (where it is), then the effect.
- **Folio, annotation or design justification:** *identify, then describe, then explain the effect on the viewer, then justify or evaluate*. Use the subject vocabulary: horizontal, vertical, diagonal/oblique, curvilinear, radial, actual/implied line, leading line, eye path, directional force, dominant, contrast, movement, rhythm, emphasis. When modelling student work, keep it in the student's voice and label it as a model.
- **Comparing two works:** use a table with these columns: dominant direction, contrasting direction, eye path, reading pattern, mood, and how direction supports the purpose.

## Use it in connected tools

- **Canva:** `read-design` or `export-design` to see a design. To demonstrate a fix, `copy-design` first, then `edit-design` on the copy (e.g. rotate a shape, move an arrow so it points inwards), then show the before and after. Use `comment-on-design` to leave specific direction feedback.
- **Adobe for creativity:** call `adobe_mandatory_init` first, and `create_visual_design_express_skill` before authoring any visual. Use `image_crop_and_resize` to re-crop a photo so a leading line or gaze runs into the frame. Use `image_auto_straighten` to fix an accidental tilt (don't use it on a deliberate Dutch angle).
- **Unsplash:** search for example photos by direction ("leading lines road", "dutch angle", "vertical skyscraper", "horizon calm sea", "spiral staircase") for teaching slides or mood boards.
- Ask before publishing, sharing or overwriting anything.
