# Typography — element of design

This skill gives Claude the knowledge to teach and analyse typography **as an element of design** — how type behaves visually, how it works with the other elements and principles, and how students should reason about it. The shared method — analysis progression, justification levels, Factors Affecting Design, experimentation, differentiation, feedback approach and language rules — is in the shared method files.

**Hand off to specialist skills when they fit and are available:**
- choosing, pairing or justifying typefaces → `typeface-selection-rationale`
- sizes, leading, type scales, paragraph or CSS styles → `type-system-builder`
- critiquing or marking the typography in a design → `typography-critique-audit`
- defining terms (kerning, x-height, point vs pixel) → `typography-fundamentals-explainer`
- typography lesson activities and videos → `typography-teaching-activities`

Use this skill for the element-of-design lens; combine it with those when the task needs both.

The core habit: type is both **image** and **language**. Move from **what the type looks like and how it's set** → **effect** (voice, hierarchy, reading comfort) → **why that matters here** → **how well it works and what it costs**.

## What typography is

Typography is the design and organisation of written language through typeface, style, size, spacing, alignment, hierarchy, composition and behaviour across media. It is both a visual element and a verbal communication system.

Keep the terms distinct:
- **Typeface vs font:** a typeface is the design family; a font is a specific style or digital file within it.
- **Kerning vs tracking vs leading:** kerning adjusts specific letter pairs; tracking adjusts spacing across a range; leading is the vertical space between lines.
- **Legibility vs readability:** legibility is recognising individual characters; readability is comfortably processing extended text.

## Variables

| Variable | Range | Typical effect | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Typeface / family** | serif, sans, slab, script, display, mono, variable | voice, recognition, function | Avoid personality stereotypes; check language support |
| **Weight** | thin → black | hierarchy, emphasis | Very light weights fail small |
| **Width** | condensed → extended | fit, texture | Condensed text can hurt readability |
| **Style** | roman, italic, oblique | emphasis, voice | Use emphasis sparingly and meaningfully |
| **Size** | display, body, caption, label | hierarchy, readability | Relate to viewing distance and device |
| **Case** | upper, lower, sentence, title | word shape, tone | All caps slows long passages |
| **Kerning / tracking** | tight ↔ loose | rhythm, recognition | Optically check logos and headlines |
| **Leading** | tight ↔ open | readability, density | Relate to size, line length and script |
| **Line length** | short ↔ long measure | reading comfort | Adapt for responsive layouts |
| **Alignment** | left, right, centred, justified | reading flow, texture | Justified text can create rivers |
| **Colour / value** | text-to-background contrast | hierarchy, accessibility | Meet contrast requirements |

## Types (classifications)

- **Serif** — with serifs; editorial, branding, some interfaces.
- **Sans serif** — without serifs; digital, signage, branding.
- **Slab serif** — heavy block serifs; display or text depending on design.
- **Script** — handwriting or calligraphy-based; display and identity, rarely long text.
- **Display** — designed for large, expressive use; headlines, campaigns.
- **Monospaced** — equal character widths; code, technical contexts.
- **Variable font** — one file with adjustable axes (weight, width); responsive systems.

## How typography works with the other elements

- **Line:** letters are built from strokes; stroke contrast is a line decision. Baselines, rules and underlines are lines, and text edges (ragged or justified) form implied lines.
- **Direction:** reading direction of the script, rotated or angled type, and the flow from line to line.
- **Shape:** letterforms and counters are shapes; a block of text forms a shape on the page.
- **Form:** 3D, extruded and embossed type; signage letters mounted on buildings.
- **Space:** letter-spacing, word spacing, leading, margins and paragraph spacing are all spatial decisions.
- **Size and scale:** type size drives hierarchy; display and text sizes may need different designs or optical sizes.
- **Colour:** coloured type loses legibility faster than black; hue alone shouldn't show hierarchy or links.
- **Value:** text-to-background value contrast is the main driver of legibility.
- **Texture:** from a distance, body text creates a grey "texture" on the page; distressed or textured type trades clarity for character.
- **Layout and composition:** type blocks are the main building material of most layouts — grids, columns and hierarchy.
- **Time and duration:** reading time limits captions, subtitles and kinetic type.

## How typography contributes to the principles

- **Hierarchy:** size, weight, colour, case and spacing rank headings, body and captions — best with more than one cue.
- **Contrast:** serif against sans, heavy against light, large against small.
- **Emphasis:** a single change (bold, colour, size) highlights a word; too many cancel out.
- **Unity and harmony:** a limited type system (one or two families) ties a document or brand together.
- **Alignment:** a strong left edge or baseline grid creates order.
- **Rhythm:** consistent leading and paragraph spacing set a reading rhythm.
- **Proximity:** headings closer to the text they introduce than to the text above.
- **Balance:** heavy display type balanced against images or white space.

## Typography across fields

- **Branding:** specify typography as a system — wordmark construction, primary and secondary typefaces, hierarchy, weights, spacing, case, numerals, language support, fallback fonts, licensing, motion behaviour and examples. Consistency is typographic behaviour, not just a repeated font.
- **Graphic and communication design:** hierarchy, readability and voice in posters, publications, packaging and infographics.
- **Film, video and animation:** titles, lower thirds, captions, subtitles and kinetic type must allow for safe areas, reading time, motion, contrast and platform cropping.
- **UI / UX:** responsive type scales, labels, forms, navigation and error messages need readable sizes, line lengths and hierarchy. Plan for font loading, fallbacks, localisation and user zoom.
- **Textiles and fashion:** type printed, embroidered, woven, appliquéd or cut; counters, stroke widths and spacing must suit the process.
- **Product and industrial design:** labels, control legends, warnings and packaging text must stay legible in real conditions and within production limits.
- **Interiors and architecture:** signage and environmental type depend on viewing distance, contrast, mounting, lighting and navigation.
- **Technical communication:** conventions for dimensions, labels and annotation; clarity and consistency come first.

## Production and reproduction

| Process | Typography risks |
|---|---|
| Print | Fine serifs, knockouts and small text need proofing; stock and ink spread affect clarity |
| Screen | Rendering and pixel density affect small text |
| Web | Licensing, loading, variable-font support and fallbacks |
| Embroidery / vinyl | Simplify fine counters and strokes; test minimum letter size |
| Motion | Reading time and compression affect title and caption legibility |

## Common misconceptions

| Misconception | Better understanding |
|---|---|
| "Typeface and font mean the same thing." | Typeface is the family; font is a specific style or file. |
| "Serif is more readable in print, sans on screen." | Readability depends on the specific typeface, size, spacing, medium and reader. |
| "Typography is just choosing a font." | It includes hierarchy, spacing, alignment, scale and system behaviour. |
| "A brand font creates brand consistency." | Consistency needs rules for hierarchy, weights, spacing and applications. |
| "Script fonts look elegant, so use them everywhere." | Script suits short display use; long text in script is hard to read. |

## Worked analysis (model for exemplars and annotations)

Scenario: a film festival poster. The festival name is set very large in a condensed, heavy sans-serif, all caps, tightly tracked, running the full width; dates and venue are in a light sans at a much smaller size, left-aligned in the lower corner.

- **Identify:** "The poster uses typography."
- **Describe:** "The name is a heavy, condensed, all-caps sans spanning the width with tight tracking; supporting details are in a light weight of a sans at roughly one-tenth the size, left-aligned bottom left."
- **Explain:** "The extreme size and weight contrast makes the name read first; the condensed width lets a long name fill the width at a large size."
- **Analyse:** "Typography works with hierarchy and space: two clear levels (name, details) rather than many, with the empty space between them separating the levels. The tight, heavy name forms a dark block that reads almost like an image, while the light details sit quietly in the corner."
- **Evaluate:** "Effective for recognition from a distance and as a strong graphic. However, the light-weight details at small size may be hard to read on a printed A3 version or a phone screen; a regular weight would keep the hierarchy while improving legibility."

Each level should add a link in the chain, not simply more words.

## Justification examples (Year 11 magazine spread)

- **Weak:** "I used this font because it looks cool."
- **Developing:** "I used a bold sans for headings so they stand out."
- **Strong:** "I used a bold sans for headings and a serif at 10 pt with 14 pt leading for body text, so headings are easy to scan and the long article is comfortable to read. That suits a school magazine that people browse, then read."
- **Sophisticated:** "I printed test pages at 9, 10 and 11 pt with 12, 14 and 16 pt leading. At 9 pt, older readers in my test group struggled; at 11 pt the article ran to a third page. 10/14 was comfortable and fitted two pages, so I chose it. I gave up a larger size for a layout that doesn't split the article."

## Feedback patterns to look for in student work

- **Too many typefaces or styles** — no clear system.
- **Hierarchy by size only,** or so many levels that none stands out.
- **Personality stereotypes** ("serif = old", "script = feminine") as the whole justification.
- **Long passages in caps, script or light weights.**
- **Poor contrast** — pale text, text over busy images.
- **Kerning problems** in logos and headlines.
- **Captions and titles on screen too briefly** to read.

## Activity ideas for teaching resources

- **Same words, five typefaces:** set one sentence in five faces; discuss how the voice changes.
- **Hierarchy with one family:** create three levels of hierarchy using only one typeface.
- **Squint test:** blur a page to see the grey "texture" and the hierarchy.
- **Kerning game:** manually kern a word, then compare with a model answer.
- **Distance test (Graphics):** print a sign at three sizes; test readable distance.
- **Caption timing (Multimedia):** subtitle a 20-second clip and test whether viewers can keep up.
