# Pattern — principle of design

This skill covers **pattern** as a principle of design. Principles describe relationships and organisational effects; elements (line, direction, shape, form, space, size and scale, time and duration, value, colour, texture, typography, layout and composition) are the variables that create them. So always explain *which elements* produce pattern and *how*, and never treat an element and a principle as the same thing.

The shared method (analysis progression, justification levels, Factors Affecting Design, experimentation, teaching resources, feedback and marking, and language rules) is in the shared method files. For depth on a particular element, use its `<element>-element-of-design` skill. For whole-process help, use the method skills (`design-analysis-progression`, `design-justification-writing`, `design-feedback-and-marking`, and so on).

## What pattern is

Pattern is the planned repetition of a motif or group of elements in a regular or predictable arrangement across a surface or space.

Keep it distinct:
- **Pattern vs repetition:** pattern is organised repetition of a motif; repetition can be looser.
- **Pattern vs texture:** pattern is repeated organisation; texture is surface quality (which can be patterned or irregular).
- **Pattern vs rhythm:** rhythm is the beat within the pattern.

## Types and variations

- **Half-drop repeat.**

- **Border / stripe.**

## Levers: what a designer changes to create pattern

- Motif design
- Repeat structure
- Scale
- Colourway
- Density and spacing
- Direction (one-way vs two-way)

## Common misconceptions

| Misconception | Better understanding |
|---|---|
| "Any repeated thing is a pattern." | Pattern needs a planned, recognisable arrangement. |
| "Pattern scale doesn't matter." | Scale changes how a pattern reads on a garment, wall or screen. |
| "Patterns are only decorative." | They can brand, camouflage, signal, and affect production yield. |

## Worked analysis (model for exemplars and annotations)

Scenario: A school uniform fabric with a small eucalyptus-leaf motif in a half-drop repeat, printed in two tones of green.

- **Identify:** "The design uses pattern."
- **Describe:** "One leaf motif, about 15 mm, in a half-drop repeat; two greens; non-directional."
- **Explain:** "The half-drop hides the grid so the repeat looks natural; the small scale reads as texture from a distance and leaves up close."
- **Analyse:** "Pattern works with scale and colour: small motif plus close tones makes it subtle, and non-directional layout lets pattern pieces be cut any way."
- **Evaluate:** "Effective for a uniform (subtle, local identity, efficient cutting). However, the lighter green may fade with repeated washing; a colourfastness test is needed before bulk ordering."

Each level should add a link in the chain, not simply more words.

## Justification examples

- **Weak:** "I made a pattern because it looks nice."
- **Developing:** "I repeated the leaf so it makes a pattern."
- **Strong:** "I used a half-drop repeat of a small leaf in two close greens, so it reads as subtle texture at a distance and a local motif up close, which suits a school uniform."
- **Sophisticated:** "I compared block, half-drop and tossed repeats; block showed obvious stripes and tossed was hard to print. Half-drop looked natural and printed cleanly. I kept the pattern non-directional to reduce fabric waste, accepting that the leaves can't all point up."

## Feedback patterns to look for in student work

- **Visible grid lines** from a poorly planned block repeat.
- **Scale not tested** on the actual product.
- **Directional motif** causing wasted fabric.
- **Seams** that break the pattern awkwardly.

## Activity ideas for teaching resources

- **Repeat types:** repeat one motif as block, half-drop, brick and mirror.
- **Scale test:** print the pattern at three scales on a garment template.
- **Pattern hunt:** photograph five patterns at school and name the repeat.
- **Seam matching (Textiles):** plan a seam so the pattern matches.
