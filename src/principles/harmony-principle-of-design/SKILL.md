# Harmony — Principle of Design

Treat Harmony as a **relational, organisational concept**: it describes how Elements of Design are
organised and perceived in relation to each other, not a physical object in the design. Explain
*how* specific Element variables create harmony, what effect that has, and whether the effect
suits the audience, purpose and context. Write in **Australian English**.

## How to reason about Harmony

**Elements of Design** (the variables manipulated): line, direction, shape, form, space, size and
scale, time and duration, value, colour, texture, typography, layout and composition. Name the
specific variable (e.g. "type weight", "interval between modules"), not just the category.

**Causal model:** Elements manipulated → primary Principle → secondary Principle(s) → perceptual
effect → communication / functional outcome → suitability for audience, purpose and context.

- Identify whether harmony is the **primary** Principle (most directly created by the decision)
  or a **secondary** effect of another Principle.
- Treat causal links as conditional, not automatic, and don't force a chain the evidence doesn't
  support. Separate cause from correlation.
- Use hedged, mechanism-based language ("tends to", "may be perceived as", "can direct attention"):
  perception varies with culture, reading direction, medium, device, viewing distance and
  accessibility needs.
- Surface trade-offs (possible advantage ↔ possible disadvantage) and treat failure as a matter of
  degree: absent, weak, inconsistent, excessive, inappropriate or deliberately disrupted.
- Recognise deliberate rule-breaking: ask what effect the decision creates and whether it suits the
  purpose, not whether a rule was followed.
- Keep accessibility and production conditions (print, screen, responsive, textile repeat,
  manufacturing, frame rate) in view throughout.

## Knowledge: Harmony

### Definition

Harmony is the compatible and visually coherent relationship between different elements or components.

Harmony concerns how differences coexist without producing unnecessary conflict.

### Core perceptual logic

Harmony may arise through:

- related colour
- compatible proportions
- consistent stylistic language
- related materials
- controlled contrast
- visual resonance
- repeated characteristics

### Why designers use it

Harmony can:

- make complex compositions feel considered
- support aesthetic coherence
- connect varied components
- create visual comfort
- reinforce mood or brand character

### Creation strategies

- analogous or related colour relationships
- compatible typeface pairing
- recurring form language
- material compatibility
- related proportions
- controlled image treatment
- consistent stylistic conventions
- moderated contrast

### Elements

Harmony commonly involves colour, value, shape, form, texture, typography, space and composition. Time-based harmony may also involve compatible timing, sound and motion behaviour.

### Relationships

- harmony can support unity
- contrast can exist within harmony
- variety can enrich harmony
- excessive harmony may weaken emphasis
- tension may deliberately interrupt harmony

### Harmony vs Unity

Unity concerns whole-system coherence. Harmony concerns compatibility between parts.

### Applications

In branding, harmony connects multiple brand assets without requiring identical execution.

In multimedia, visual and audio components may harmonise through pacing, tone, rhythm or mood.

In textiles, colours, materials, surfaces and silhouette may relate harmoniously.

In interiors, material palettes, lighting, furniture and spatial proportions can be compatible without being identical.

### Accessibility

Harmonious low-contrast schemes must not compromise legibility. Compatibility is not more important than functional differentiation.

### Trade-offs

- visual comfort ↔ insufficient distinction
- compatibility ↔ sameness
- subtlety ↔ reduced hierarchy
- coherence ↔ lower dramatic impact

### Failure modes

- incompatible components may create unintended conflict
- excessive harmony may become visually undifferentiated
- deliberate disharmony may create tension, urgency or disruption

### Misconception

**Misconception:** Harmony means avoiding contrast.  
**Correction:** Contrast can occur within a harmonious system when differences remain purposefully related.

## Distinguishing Harmony from related concepts

| Relationship | Distinction |
|---|---|
| Unity vs Harmony | Unity concerns whole-system coherence; harmony concerns compatibility between parts. |

## Causal chains and interactions involving Harmony

Use these as possible relationships to test against the evidence in the design, not as universal laws.

- **Harmony ↔ Contrast** — contrast can introduce differentiation without destroying harmony.
- **Unity** may be strengthened through repetition, alignment, harmony, consistent spacing, recurring forms and controlled variation.

## Weak → sophisticated responses

When scaffolding, generate a progression for harmony: **weak** (names the Principle), **developing** (describes a visible feature), **strong** (links specific Element variables to the Principle), **sophisticated** (explains the causal chain to a communication or functional outcome for the audience and context).

## Working protocols

### Analysing
Identify where harmony is evident → describe the Elements and relationships creating it →
explain the perceptual/functional effect → analyse how decisions combine and whether harmony
is primary or secondary → connect to communication, purpose, audience and context → evaluate
against explicit criteria → propose a refinement.

### Critiquing / feedback
State the intended outcome, the observable evidence, the Elements manipulated, the effect on
harmony and on communication or function, a trade-off where relevant, then a **specific**
change and the effect it should produce, and how to test it. Avoid vague advice like "improve the
harmony".

### Justifying a design decision
Design decision → Elements manipulated → primary Principle → secondary Principle(s) where relevant
→ perceptual/functional effect → communication outcome → purpose → audience/context →
suitability. For sophisticated work add evidence/research, an alternative considered, the trade-off
and an evaluation.

### Generating a concept or brief
Specify the intended outcome, how harmony will be created (which Elements and relationships),
related Principles, accessibility requirements, implementation constraints, system rules,
acceptable variation, trade-offs and testing criteria.

### Refining
Problem observed → evidence → variable changed → expected effect on harmony → expected
communication/function effect → test (at the intended size, medium or device). Change one
significant variable at a time where practical.

### Experimenting
Compare related and deliberately conflicting combinations.
Record: variable changed | variation | effect on harmony | secondary Principle | communication
effect | suitability | refinement.

### Teaching
Move learners through knowledge → recognition → description → explanation → analysis →
experimentation → application → justification → evaluation → refinement. Use non-leading
questions ("What effect does the level of harmony have?", not "How does the strong
harmony make it exciting?").

## Language

Prefer: establishes, contributes to, reinforces, disrupts, differentiates, groups, separates,
directs, emphasises, balances, creates cohesion, guides attention, may be perceived as, is
appropriate because. Avoid unsupported verdicts ("looks good", "is professional", "is boring",
"always works") unless criteria and context support them.

## Quality check

- Harmony treated as a relationship, with the specific Element variables named.
- Harmony distinguished from closely related Principles.
- Causal links evidence-based; primary vs secondary Principle identified where useful.
- Links made to communication, function, audience, purpose and context.
- Trade-offs, accessibility and implementation considered; deliberate disruption recognised.
- Feedback and refinements specific and testable; evaluation criteria explicit.
- Australian English throughout.
