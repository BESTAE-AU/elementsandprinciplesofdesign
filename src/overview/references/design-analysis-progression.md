
# Design analysis progression

This guide scaffolds analysis of any design using the NSW TAS command verbs in order. The detail for each element or principle lives in the `<element>-element-of-design` and `<principle>-principle-of-design` skills. This guide is about the **thinking process** that connects them. The shared method is in `method/analysis.md` and `method/checks-and-language.md`.

## The chain

**observable characteristic → perceptual or functional effect → what it communicates → purpose it serves → fit with audience and context → how well it works, and what it costs**

Move from evidence to interpretation, never the reverse. Every claim of effect should trace back to something you could point at.

## The verbs, and what each adds

| Verb | What it adds | Weak version | Stronger version |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Identify** | Names what is present, actual or implied | "There are lines." | "Four white road-marking lines lead from the bottom-left corner." |
| **Describe** | Precise, observable characteristics, with no interpretation | "The lines are big." | "They taper from about 8 mm to 1 mm and converge on the title." |
| **Explain** | Cause and effect, using *because* or *so* | "They look cool." | "Because they converge, they create perspective, so the eye travels to the title." |
| **Analyse** | Relationships with other elements and principles, and what would change if a variable changed | "It uses contrast too." | "The white-on-dark value contrast and the title's scale reinforce the path the lines create." |
| **→ Communication, purpose, audience** | What it may say, why it's there, and for whom | "It's good for runners." | "The diagonal suggests forward motion, which suits a fun-run audience seeing it briefly." |
| **Evaluate** | Judgement against a named criterion, with evidence and a limitation | "It works well." | "Effective at distance; however, the 1 mm tips may break up on a social tile." |

## Workflow

1. **Get the artefact.** Work from an image or description. If you're inventing one for teaching, describe it briefly first.
2. **Choose the focus.** A single element or principle, or the few that matter most. Don't list all 12 elements. Pick the ones doing the real work.
3. **Run the chain** for each, using the verbs in order.
4. **Connect across elements.** The strongest analysis shows how elements combine to create a principle, e.g. "value contrast and scale together create emphasis".
5. **Evaluate** against the brief or an explicit criterion, and name a trade-off or limitation.
6. **Check the language** (`method/checks-and-language.md`): conditional wording for emotional or cultural readings, and no "it works" without a criterion.

## The four lenses (Multimedia)

Use characteristics, features, properties and attributes precisely (`method/analysis.md`):
- **Characteristics:** what can be observed.
- **Features:** what it lets the design do.
- **Properties:** measurable or technical qualities.
- **Attributes:** relation to audience, purpose, context and meaning.

## Scaffolds for students

- **Sentence starters:**
  - "The designer has used ___ (characteristic) in ___ (location)."
  - "Because ___, the viewer ___."
  - "This works with ___ to create ___ (principle)."
  - "This may communicate ___ to ___ (audience)."
  - "This is effective / less effective because ___ (criterion); however, ___."
- **Two-colour highlight:** students highlight *what it is* in one colour and *what it does and why* in another. Weak responses are almost all the first colour.
- **Chain cards:** give students cards for each link and have them order and complete a chain for one feature.

## Common weaknesses

- Listing elements without effect.
- Effect claims with no evidence ("it makes it pop").
- Universal meanings ("red means danger").
- Jumping straight to opinion.
- Evaluation with no criterion.
- Confusing elements with principles.
