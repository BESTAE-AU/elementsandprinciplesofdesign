# Page and visual design principles

This skill doesn't do layout, but structure content so a later layout pass (by Britt, or by `greyscale-print-design-system`) can actually apply these well. Multimedia-learning and cognitive-load principles apply to print, not just screens.

## Core principles (Mayer's multimedia principles, applied to paper)

- **Coherence** — no decorative image or box that doesn't support understanding. If it's not doing a job, cut it.
- **Signalling** — use headings, arrows, numbering and typographic emphasis deliberately to guide attention, not decoratively.
- **Spatial contiguity** — put explanatory labels beside the feature they explain, not in a distant legend or a different page.
- **Avoid split attention** — students shouldn't have to flip between distant pages to integrate information that belongs together.
- **Avoid redundancy** — don't repeat identical prose in multiple boxes just because there's empty space to fill.
- **Segment** — break genuinely complex material into conceptual sections rather than one dense block.
- **Integrate representations** — place equation/diagram/graph and its explanation together when students need to coordinate them mentally.

## Practical heuristics (adjustable, not hard rules)

One obvious starting point per page; a clear visual hierarchy; generous margins; writing space that actually matches the requested response length; body text large enough for secondary readers (commonly 11–12pt or larger, bigger again for younger students or accessibility needs); avoid compressed line spacing, long italic passages, and ALL-CAPS body text; limit competing boxes; use icons consistently and sparingly; check the page still reads in grayscale (most of this printing is greyscale-only per the house design system); never rely on colour alone to carry meaning; avoid low-contrast pale instructional text.

Don't claim a single typeface — including a specific "dyslexia font" — is a universal fix for legibility; readability depends on several interacting design choices and the specific learner, not one silver-bullet font.

**White space is instructional infrastructure, not empty space to feel generous about** — but a page can legitimately be dense if the material is conceptually coherent and well organised. Don't chase whitespace for its own sake at the cost of splitting content that belongs together.
