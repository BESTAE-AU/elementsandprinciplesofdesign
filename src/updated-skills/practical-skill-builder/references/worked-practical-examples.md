# Worked practical examples

Short models to adapt. The full method is in `practical-skills-mode.md`. Never invent machine settings, tensions, temperatures, feeds or tolerances; the teacher supplies model-specific values.

## Planning a demonstration

Define the procedure, prerequisites, tools and materials, hazards, expected result and quality checkpoints. Arrange visibility, orientation, camera view and audio so students can see the essential action. Model one meaningful chunk, explain why, and show the check. Distinguish normal variation from a fault, and show common errors safely through samples or photos rather than hazardous demonstrations. Think aloud about decisions: "I'm checking grain direction because this panel must stay stable."

## Seam sample (Textiles)

1. Purpose: the seam must join the chosen fabric and withstand the intended movement.
2. Identify the actual machine, fabric and approved setup; students explain the hazards and controls.
3. Demonstrate alignment and stitching on a sample, making the key check visible.
4. Students practise on samples and inspect for consistency, secured ends and fabric behaviour.
5. Feedback names one change, such as alignment, setup or handling, based on the evidence.
6. Students repeat, compare the sample with the criterion and explain why the final method suits.

## Software procedure (Multimedia or Graphics)

State the intended result, show a few relevant tools, model one change and explain how to judge it. Students practise on a non-assessed asset, export and inspect the file, then repeat on a new asset with less support. A screenshot of tool use is weaker evidence than a correctly exported result plus an explanation of the decision.

## Troubleshooting chain

**Symptom → possible causes → safe check → one adjustment → test → judgement.**

Give novices a small table of visible symptoms and approved checks. Ask experienced students to distinguish competing causes and justify their diagnostic order. Don't encourage unsupervised repairs or adjustments outside authorised procedures.

## Readiness, access and evidence

- Competence on one machine or material doesn't authorise another. Readiness can include explaining hazards and showing a safe setup before operating.
- Access adjustments (adapted handles, sequence cards, seating, more time, a stable support) must preserve the intended competency and safe access.
- Assess process, result, self-checking, independence and the ability to explain decisions. Record the assistance given, because a finished artefact alone doesn't show independent performance.
- Link to design both ways: a technique expands design possibilities, and a design requirement gives a reason to learn a technique.
