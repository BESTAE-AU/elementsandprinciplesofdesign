# Literacy and language demands

Every subject has its own literacy, not just English — a historian's "evidence," a scientist's "evidence" and evidence in literary interpretation don't work the same way (Shanahan & Shanahan, 2008). Identify the actual language demands of a task before assuming students can just handle it because the disciplinary content has been taught.

## Vocabulary tiers

Distinguish three tiers when planning what to teach explicitly rather than assume:

- **Tier 1 — everyday vocabulary.** Common words most students already know.
- **Tier 2 — academic/general vocabulary.** Cross-subject words that carry precision in academic writing ("analyse," "significant," "justify," "consistent") — often the tier students most need explicit teaching in, since it's assumed rather than taught.
- **Tier 3 — technical/subject-specific vocabulary.** The discipline's own terms (mordant, kerf, bias, resolution, load-bearing) — usually already flagged and defined in content pages, but check it actually is.

## Other language demands to check for

Nominalisation (turning processes into dense nouns — "the reaction" vs "reacting"); complex noun groups; cause-and-effect language; comparison language; modality (certainty vs hedging); command verbs in instructions (the difference between "describe," "analyse," "evaluate," "justify"); text structure and genre conventions the task assumes students already know; discipline-specific discourse patterns (how a design justification is structured vs a scientific explanation vs a historical argument).

## Supports (preserve the disciplinary demand, remove the unnecessary language barrier)

Visuals; multilingual resources; oral rehearsal before writing; sentence stems; word families and morphology; model texts; worked sentences; paragraph frames; scaffolds that fade deliberately rather than staying put for the whole unit.

## Useful lenses, not mandatory templates

The Four Resources Model (Luke & Freebody) — students as code breaker, participant, user and analyst of a text — and the broader multiliteracies framework are worth having in mind when checking whether a task only ever asks students to decode and locate information, never to use a text for a real purpose or question whose interests it serves. Use them as a check on the *range* of literacy work a sequence asks for, not as a required four-part page template — treating them as a rigid sequence is a known way to misapply them.

## A grounding principle

Treat multilingualism, Aboriginal English, and other language varieties as resources for thinking and learning, not deficiencies to correct. Standard Australian English fluency is not evidence of superior intelligence or reasoning ability, and Aboriginal English is not "incorrect English" — it's a legitimate variety with its own grammar. Don't reduce conceptual complexity for EAL/D learners or students still developing academic English; teach the language explicitly instead, and keep the intellectual demand where it belongs.
