# Analysing texture

## Framework: identify → describe → explain → evaluate

1. **Identify**
   - Is each texture **actual** or **visual**? If visual, is it **simulated** or **invented**?
   - **Where** is it (background, focal point, type, border, product surface)?
   - Is it **dominant** or **supporting**? What **smooth** area does it contrast with?
2. **Describe**
   - Use 2–3 precise adjectives from the vocabulary bank in `glossary.md`.
   - State **how it was made**: medium, tool, technique, material, finish or software effect.
   - Note its **scale**, **density**, **direction** and **value range** (high or low contrast).
3. **Explain the effect.** Link each texture to a purpose, using "evidence → effect":
   - **Realism / believability:** makes objects look solid and real.
   - **Depth and space:** coarse, detailed texture seems close; fine or blurred texture seems far away (texture gradient).
   - **Emphasis / focal point:** a single textured area among smooth ones (or the reverse) attracts the eye.
   - **Contrast and hierarchy:** separates areas, layers or information.
   - **Mood and meaning:** rough and distressed = raw, rebellious, aged; soft and velvety = comfort, luxury; glossy and smooth = modern, clean, technical.
   - **Authenticity / era:** paper grain, film grain, halftone and letterpress suggest the handmade, vintage or analogue.
   - **Quality and brand:** tactile finishes signal premium; recycled textured stock signals eco values.
   - **Invitation to touch / interaction:** packaging, textiles, products, haptics.
4. **Evaluate**
   - Does the texture serve the brief, concept or audience?
   - Is it the right strength and scale for the output?
   - What would be lost if it were removed? That is the test of whether it's doing a job.
   - Suggest one or two specific fixes.

## Common problems (and fixes)

| Problem | Fix |
|---|---|
| Text sits on busy, high-contrast texture and is hard to read | Lower the texture opacity; add a scrim or solid panel behind the text; blur or darken that area; move the text. Check with `texture_tools.py text-over` |
| Too many competing textures, so nothing leads | Choose one hero texture; simplify or remove the rest; add smooth areas for rest |
| Texture everywhere at the same strength, so it reads as noise | Vary it: strong at the focal point, faint or absent elsewhere |
| Texture has no link to the concept ("grunge because it looks cool") | Pick a texture that carries meaning for the brief (e.g. hessian for a farm-to-table café) |
| Low-resolution texture looks pixelated at print size | Use a high-resolution source (300 ppi at final size) or a vector or procedural texture |
| Fine grain disappears on screen or on a photocopier | Increase scale or contrast of the texture; test the real output |
| Simulated texture looks fake (repeating tiles, wrong light direction) | Use seamless or larger textures; match the light direction of the rest of the image |
| Texture fights colour (busy texture with a busy palette) | Quieten one: textured image with a limited palette, or bold palette with smooth surfaces |

## Reference examples

Use these as anchors, or ask students to find their own. Always pair the name with *what the texture does*.

| Work / designer | Texture | Effect / why it's useful to discuss |
|---|---|---|
| Vincent van Gogh, *The Starry Night* (1889) | Actual (impasto) and invented visual texture in swirling strokes | Energy and emotion; the texture of the brushwork *is* the subject's movement |
| Albrecht Dürer, *Rhinoceros* (1515, woodcut) | Invented visual texture: hide as armour plates and scales | Texture used to make an unseen animal believable; realism vs invention |
| Max Ernst, *Histoire naturelle* (1926, frottage) | Visual texture from rubbings of wood, leaves and cloth | Found texture sparking imagination; a technique students can do |
| Meret Oppenheim, *Object* (1936) | Actual texture: fur-covered cup, saucer and spoon | Unsettling because the texture contradicts the object's function; texture creating meaning |
| Rosalie Gascoigne (Australian, 1917–1999) | Actual texture of weathered found materials: road signs, drink crates, linoleum | Texture carrying place, time and landscape; Australian example |
| Anselm Kiefer | Actual texture: straw, ash, lead, thick paint | Texture carrying history, loss and memory |
| David Carson, *Ray Gun* magazine (1992–95) | Distressed type, layered visual texture | Rebellion, youth culture; also the legibility debate |
| Apple iOS 6 → iOS 7 (2013) | Simulated leather, felt and paper textures replaced by flat design | Skeuomorphism vs flat design; how texture dates a design |
| Premium packaging (e.g. tea, cosmetics, spirits) | Emboss, foil, soft-touch lamination, textured stock | Tactile quality signals price and care |
| Denim | Twill weave (diagonal rib), fading and whiskering finishes | Texture from construction and finishing; worn texture as desirable |
| Timber furniture | Grain and figure, oiled vs lacquered finishes | Natural texture as the main aesthetic; finishing changes the feel |

**First Nations art:** if students analyse works by First Nations artists (e.g. Emily Kame Kngwarreye's layered dotting), frame the marks as the artist's cultural and personal practice, not a generic texture technique to copy. Name the artist and Country, and follow Creative Australia's *Protocols for using First Nations Cultural and Intellectual Property in the Arts*.

## Sentence stems

- *Identify:* "The designer has used ___ (actual / visual) texture in the ___."
- *Describe:* "The surface appears ___ and ___, created by ___."
- *Explain:* "This texture makes the viewer feel ___ because ___." / "The ___ texture contrasts with the smooth ___, drawing the eye to ___."
- *Evaluate:* "The texture is effective because ___. However, ___ could be improved by ___."
- *Justify (own work):* "I chose a ___ texture to communicate ___ to my target audience of ___. I kept it subtle behind the text so ___."

## Model annotation (labelled as a model)

> **Model (Stage 5, poster for a bakery):** The designer has placed a **simulated** kraft-paper texture across the whole background. It is **coarse and fibrous**, with flecks of darker brown, and the low value contrast keeps it subtle. The texture suggests something **handmade, natural and old-fashioned**, which suits a small artisan bakery. It also contrasts with the **smooth, flat** red logo, which helps the logo stand out as the focal point. The body text is harder to read over the darker flecks, so I would lighten the texture behind the text or put the text on a smooth cream panel.

> **Model (Stage 6, Visual Arts, Oppenheim's *Object*, 1936):** Oppenheim covers a teacup, saucer and spoon in gazelle fur, turning a smooth, hard, hygienic surface into one that is soft, animal and **tactile**. The **actual texture** directly contradicts the object's function: we imagine the fur against our lips, and the result is unsettling. Texture here isn't decoration. It is the idea itself, showing how Surrealists used familiar objects to disturb the viewer.
