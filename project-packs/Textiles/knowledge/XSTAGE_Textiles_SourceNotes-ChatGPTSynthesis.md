## Textiles: Materials and Properties

### Fibre → yarn → fabric → performance

Textile performance results from fibre properties, yarn construction, fabric structure, finishing and use conditions. Fibre identity alone does not determine suitability. Cotton jersey and cotton canvas share a fibre but differ substantially in stretch, drape and construction behaviour.

Natural fibres include plant and animal sources. Manufactured fibres include regenerated cellulosic and synthetic fibres. Blends combine fibres for particular properties; they may also complicate recycling. Continuous filaments and staple fibres influence yarn behaviour alongside twist and yarn size.

### Structure and properties

| Area | Variables | Likely relevance |
|---|---|---|
| Woven fabrics | Plain, twill, satin and derived structures | Stability, surface, wear and drape |
| Knitted fabrics | Loop structure, direction and density | Stretch, recovery, comfort and construction |
| Non-wovens | Fibre bonding or entanglement | Specialised stability, absorbency or filtration |
| Yarn | Fibre, twist, size, ply, texture | Strength, handle, surface and durability |
| Finishes | Mechanical or chemical treatments | Appearance, care, shrinkage, repellency and performance |
| Fabric properties | Mass, thickness, strength, stretch, recovery, absorbency, permeability | Suitability for intended use |

Stretch is extensibility; recovery is return after extension. Absorbency differs from wicking and water resistance. Breathability needs a defined meaning: air permeability and moisture-vapour transfer are not interchangeable. Drape concerns hanging behaviour, not softness alone.

Grain direction and bias affect shape and stretch. Nap and directional print affect layout, quantities and appearance. Colourfastness concerns resistance to specified exposure or transfer; wash, light and rubbing fastness can differ.

### Selection and testing

**End use → required performance → candidate fabric → evidence/test → construction implications → justified selection.**

Compare actual samples rather than generic fibre claims. Possible classroom trials include stretch/recovery, dimensional change after care, seam strength comparison, abrasion observation, drape, opacity, absorbency and print response. Keep sample size, direction and conditions consistent. Classroom trials demonstrate comparative evidence; do not label them as certified standard tests without the actual method and equipment.

Example: a costume sleeve must permit overhead movement. Test fabric stretch, pattern geometry and seam extensibility together. Stretch fabric alone cannot compensate for an unsuitable pattern or a seam that breaks under extension.

### Surface design and technology

Dyeing introduces colour into fibre/yarn/fabric; printing applies a pattern or image to selected areas. Embroidery adds stitched structure; appliqué attaches material shapes. Technique suitability depends on substrate, finish, stability, heat tolerance and care.

Digital textile technologies may include printing, cutting, embroidery and heat-transfer workflows. Smart textiles can incorporate sensing, conductive pathways or responsive materials. Claims of sustainability or innovation require evidence of benefit, durability and realistic use.

### Sustainability and pedagogy

Assess sourcing, production, dyeing, use, washing, durability, repair and end of life. Natural is not automatically sustainable, and recycled does not automatically mean recyclable.

Teach with labelled swatches, visible structure, handling and controlled tests. Support with a property bank and two candidates. Extend with conflicting requirements and comparison of finish effects. Assess **property → performance → end use → appropriateness**, including limitations and care consequences.

## Textiles: Construction and Manufacture

### Construction as a designed sequence

Textile manufacture transforms selected materials into a product through pattern preparation, cutting, joining, shaping, finishing and testing. The sequence affects access to seams, accuracy, finish and the possibility of correction.

**Design intent → fabric behaviour → construction method → equipment → sequence → sample → quality check.**

### Core construction language

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Seam | Join between material pieces |
| Seam allowance | Material between stitching and cut edge |
| Seam finish | Treatment controlling raw-edge behaviour or appearance |
| Notch/mark | Alignment or construction reference |
| Ease | Difference supporting fit, movement or assembly |
| Interfacing | Supporting layer modifying stability or structure |
| Lining | Internal layer contributing finish, comfort or function |
| Underlining | Layer treated with the outer fabric as a unit |
| Facing | Shaped finish for an edge/opening |
| Boning | Support controlling shape or collapse in appropriate structures |
| Piping | Covered cord or raised detail within a seam/edge |

Seam type, stitch type and seam finish are different decisions. Overlocking can join and/or finish depending on stitch configuration. It does not automatically suit every seam or replace all structural stitching.

### Construction approaches

Overlocked construction typically manages exposed allowances using a suitable overlocker configuration, with any required structural seams, lining and edge treatments planned separately.

Bagged-out construction joins layers and turns through a planned opening to enclose edges. Turning access, bulk, clipping, understitching, pressing and closing the opening must be considered. It is not a universal method that can be described accurately without the actual pattern and layer order.

For corsetry, distinguish fashion/costume structure from medical claims or unsupported extreme shaping. Fabric, reinforcement, boning placement, fastening, fit and wear comfort interact. Sample and fit-test under appropriate supervision.

### Planning and quality

Prepare fabric and check grain, nap, shrinkage assumptions and layout. Transfer marks accurately. Sample seams and decorative techniques using the actual material combination. Pressing during assembly supports accuracy; do not wait until completion.

Check seam consistency, alignment, dimensions, secure fastening, movement, raw-edge control, bulk and finish. Record errors and causes. Repeated unpicking can damage fabrics, so plan high-risk stages and test first.

### Equipment and WHS

Choose needles, thread and stitch settings from the material, operation, machine manual and sample results. Do not invent universal Baby Lock or Juki settings: needle configuration, differential feed, width, tension and length are model- and task-dependent.

Heat press and HTV processes require substrate and transfer specifications, time, temperature, pressure, cooling/peel instructions and a test sample. Manage hot surfaces and manufacturer-specific risks. A press suitable for HTV is not automatically appropriate for every interfacing process.

### Practical instruction and assessment

Demonstrate one meaningful stage, explain why, model checks and allow supervised practice. Use photographs showing correct orientation and identifiable fabric layers. Provide large unlined writing spaces or tables for student planning when required.

Support through labelled patterns and partially ordered sequences; extend through alternative finishes and quality comparisons. Assess accuracy, functional performance, finish, safe independence and evidence-informed process decisions, while distinguishing teacher assistance from student work.

## Fashion and Costume Design

### Fashion, costume and textile product design

Fashion design considers dress, identity, market, wear and production. Costume design supports character, performance, setting or concept. Textile product design can extend beyond garments to furnishings, accessories and functional products. Distinguish wearer, purchaser and audience: their needs may differ.

**Inspiration → concept → wearer/audience/end use → aesthetic and functional criteria → alternatives → fit/material trials → construction → evaluation.**

### Design variables

Silhouette is the overall outline. Proportion concerns relationships among parts. Line can direct attention or establish construction; colour, texture and pattern influence interpretation. Details include seams, openings, sleeves, collars, pockets, trims and fastenings. Each detail can serve aesthetic and functional purposes simultaneously.

Fit is suitability to body and intended silhouette; it is not necessarily tightness. Ease may support movement or a desired form. Anthropometric measurements are individual and task-dependent; avoid using one body size as a universal norm.

### Costume-specific requirements

Consider movement, visibility, dressing time, quick changes, attachment security, heat, weight, laundering, performance lighting and repeated use. A historically inspired garment need not claim historical accuracy without research. Stage lighting can alter colour and visibility; test when relevant.

Inspiration should be transformed through analysis rather than copied. Explain which visual or conceptual features were adapted and how they support the intended character or concept. Cultural references require authority, context and respect.

### Development and production

Use annotated alternatives, fabric trials, toiles or mock-ups, fitting records and comparison against criteria. A toile tests geometry and fit but may not predict the behaviour of a very different final fabric. Identify what the prototype can and cannot establish.

Production planning links pattern, fabric, construction and equipment. Consider seam accessibility, bulk, support, fastening and finishing early. Piping, boning, interfacing and decorative treatments may affect comfort or flexibility as well as appearance.

### Sustainability and ethics

Investigate durability, care, repair, adaptability, waste, sourcing and labour. Upcycling is meaningful when the resulting product is usable and avoids unnecessary additional impacts; novelty alone is insufficient. Inclusive design should consider size diversity, sensory preferences, mobility and dressing access without stereotypes.

### Assessment distinctions

Appropriate design assesses relevance to purpose and aesthetic/functional needs. Creativity concerns originality or meaningful adaptation. Manufacture proficiency concerns technical execution. Keep these criteria distinct to avoid double counting the same evidence.

A beautiful finish does not prove strong design reasoning; a well-justified concept can still have technical limitations. Assess each using observable evidence.

### Teaching and justification

Support with a wearer profile, small sample bank and annotated design template. Extend with conflicting performance needs, weighted comparisons and alternative construction systems.

Weak: “I chose satin because it looks fancy.” Stronger: “The selected satin's surface sheen reinforces the formal character under the intended lighting, while sample trials showed the required drape; its tendency to fray requires an enclosed finish.” Refer to the actual fabric rather than universal satin properties.
