## 1. Foundational concepts

Design is a process of understanding a situation, identifying a need or opportunity, establishing requirements, generating possibilities, gathering evidence, making decisions, testing, learning and refining.

A design situation describes the circumstances in which a need or opportunity exists. An identified need explains the gap between current conditions and a desirable outcome. A design opportunity may arise from frustration, inefficiency, exclusion, waste, technological change, demographic change, cultural change, environmental pressure, unmet aspiration, new materials or new manufacturing capability.

A design brief establishes the challenge, intended purpose, users and boundaries while preserving meaningful design decisions. Purpose and context should inform visual and technical development. A useful sequence is:

**Situation → need → purpose and context → brief.**

Requirements describe what a solution needs to achieve. Criteria provide a basis for judging success. Specifications communicate sufficiently precise requirements for development, production or verification. Constraints establish limits such as time, cost, safety, equipment and available materials.

**Need → requirement → criterion → method of testing.**

Design intent captures functional, experiential, communicative, aesthetic and emotional aims. Intention does not establish achieved effect: testing compares intent with actual outcomes.

## 2. Research for design

Primary research produces information directly through interviews, surveys, observation, measurements, user testing, material testing, product disassembly, experiments, photographs and field research.

Secondary research uses existing sources: books, academic literature, manufacturer specifications, databases, standards, industry publications, credible websites and case studies.

Research should reduce uncertainty and support a decision. Ask: **What decision will this information help make?** Research that cannot answer this may be irrelevant to the project.

The reasoning relationship is:

**Research finding → what it means → what should change.**

Example: users operate a device while wearing gloves. Fine fingertip controls may therefore be difficult to operate. Larger physical controls or alternative activation methods should be explored.

Research is not simply background information. Important findings should have identifiable design implications.

## 3. Ideation

Ideation deliberately produces possible responses before committing to a solution. Methods include brainstorming, mind mapping, thumbnail sketching, morphological exploration, mood boards, inspiration analysis, rapid sketching, scenario generation, material experimentation, physical manipulation, digital exploration, precedent recombination, analogies and divergent prompts.

Divergent thinking creates breadth. Ask what else could work; what changes if material, scale or interaction changes; what happens if the expected form is removed; or how the need could be met in an entirely different way.

Convergent thinking narrows possibilities through criteria, evidence, feasibility, testing, comparison and constraints. Healthy design alternates between divergence and convergence.

**Diverge → develop → compare → converge.**

## 4. Initial ideas, developed concepts and final designs

An initial idea is an exploratory possibility and need not resolve every detail.

A developed concept has undergone further investigation. It may include dimensions, materials, construction, interaction, visual choices, annotations, production feasibility and criteria alignment.

A final design is the currently selected solution intended for implementation. Production and testing may still require refinement; “final” does not mean incapable of change.

## 5. Meaningful alternatives

Alternatives should differ meaningfully, rather than repeat the same idea in three colours. They may vary in structure, form, mechanism, layout, material, interaction, manufacturing method, communication strategy, dimensions or feature set.

Alternatives enable comparison. Without them, selection may merely confirm the first idea.

## 6. Concept comparison and selection

Compare alternatives against relevant criteria using comparative annotations, advantages/disadvantages tables, decision matrices, weighted criteria matrices, prototype comparisons, user testing, pairwise comparison, performance testing or expert feedback.

Criteria may have different priorities. For a safety product, safety may outweigh decorative novelty. Weights should arise from the brief, user, end use, evidence and context, and should be justified.

Decision matrices are visible reasoning aids, not mathematically objective truth. Limitations include subjective scores, arbitrary weights, incomplete criteria and false precision. Unexpected results should prompt questioning.

**Alternatives → criteria → comparison → trade-off → selection.**

## 7. Justification

Justification explains why a decision is appropriate; it goes beyond description or preference.

| Level | Example |
|---|---|
| Description | I selected ripstop nylon. |
| Basic reason | I selected ripstop nylon because it is strong. |
| Stronger justification | Ripstop nylon was selected because its high strength-to-weight ratio supports a lightweight but durable product. It is more appropriate than heavier canvas for users who transport the product frequently. |

Reusable reasoning frameworks:

- **Decision → evidence → effect → purpose → user/audience → context → suitability.**
- **Decision → research → alternatives → trade-off → final selection.**
- **Feature → function → benefit → need.**
- **Property → performance → end use → appropriateness** for materials.
- **Visual choice → perceptual effect → communication effect → audience/purpose** for visual communication.

## 8. Prototyping

A prototype is a representation developed to investigate aspects of a potential solution. It may test form, dimensions, interaction, fit, movement, structure, layout, hierarchy, manufacturing, usability or performance. It need not perfectly resemble the final product.

Low-fidelity prototypes are quick, inexpensive exploratory representations: paper mock-ups, thumbnails, cardboard models, toiles and wireframes. They support early decisions about layout, form, interaction and dimensions.

Higher-fidelity prototypes more closely approximate the intended outcome and support detailed testing of usability, performance, material behaviour, visual finish and production.

Use the **lowest fidelity capable of answering the question being tested**. High fidelity too early can consume time and money, encourage attachment to weak ideas and discourage substantial revision.

## 9. Experimentation

Experiments become meaningful when they isolate or compare variables.

**Question → variable → trial → observation/data → interpretation → decision.**

Example questions:

- Which seam finish withstands repeated stress most effectively?
- Which typographic hierarchy enables the fastest information recognition?
- Which wheel alignment produces the lowest rolling resistance?
- Which interface layout helps users locate an action fastest?

## 10. Feedback

Feedback can come from users, peers, teachers, clients, experts, stakeholders, testing data and observation. Feedback is evidence, not an automatic instruction.

Consider relevance, expertise, representativeness, consistency and relationship to criteria. Users may identify a problem without identifying the best solution.

## 11. Iteration and refinement

Iteration repeatedly improves a solution through development and evaluation:

**Develop → test → evaluate → modify → retest.**

Meaningful iteration has a reason. Refinement is an evidence-informed improvement:

**Problem → evidence → probable cause → variable changed → expected effect → test → evaluation.**

Example: six users scroll instead of using navigation icons. Increase icon prominence and reposition them in the primary navigation zone. Retest whether users recognise navigation without prompting.

Iteration has diminishing returns. Further improvement must be weighed against time, cost and resources.

## 12. Ongoing and final evaluation

Evaluation occurs throughout development. Ask whether research changes direction, ideas meet criteria, testing reveals problems, the solution remains feasible, criteria need revision, consequences have appeared or refinement is needed.

**Decision → evaluation → adjustment.**

Final evaluation judges the realised solution against the brief, criteria, specifications, user needs, evidence, performance and implementation constraints. Identify strengths, limitations, unresolved issues and possible improvements.

Ask: **Effective for what, according to what evidence, for whom, under what conditions?**

| Approach | Reasoning framework |
|---|---|
| Criteria-based | Criterion → evidence → judgement → improvement |
| Comparative | Current performance → alternative → relative advantage → consequence |
| User-based | User need → interaction → observed experience → suitability |

Example: a garment must allow unrestricted shoulder movement. The wearer reports restriction during overhead reaching. The criterion is partially met. Modify armscye shape and sleeve volume, then retest.

## 13. Production planning

Production is part of design decision-making. Plan process sequence, tools, equipment, materials, time, skills, tolerances, costs, safety, dependencies and quality checkpoints.

**Design → production requirements**, while **production capability → feasible design**. The relationship is bidirectional.

Design for manufacture and assembly considers whether components can be produced accurately, suitable tools are available, parts can be assembled, tolerances are realistic, sequences are efficient, errors can be corrected, maintenance is possible and disassembly is feasible where required. Development may require simplification.

## 14. Quality within the process

Quality can be designed into a project rather than inspected only at completion.

**Materials inspection → dimensional checking → process control → assembly checking → finishing → functional test.**

Quality control uses inspection and testing to identify whether outputs meet requirements. Quality assurance uses systems and processes intended to prevent failures.

## 15. Characteristics, features, properties and attributes

These categories overlap in ordinary usage; their purpose here is to support analysis.

| Category | Guiding question | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Characteristics | What can be observed? | Compact, curved, textured, lightweight, symmetrical |
| Features | What has been included and what does it enable? | Adjustable strap, navigation menu, removable lining, autofocus |
| Properties | What inherent or measurable qualities influence performance? | Tensile strength, flexibility, absorbency, thermal conductivity, resolution, frame rate |
| Attributes | How does the design relate to audience, purpose, meaning, context or experience? | Professional, playful, accessible, culturally appropriate, reassuring |

**Characteristic/property → feature/behaviour → effect → attribute/user experience → suitability.**

## 16. Factors affecting design

Factors operate as decision lenses: appropriateness, needs, function/functionality, aesthetics, finance/cost, ergonomics, WHS/safety, quality, resources, environmental consequences, sustainability, obsolescence, life-cycle analysis, accessibility and ethics.

They influence **criteria → alternatives → selection → production → testing → evaluation → refinement**. Avoid mechanically attaching every factor to every decision; investigate materially relevant factors deeply. A separate knowledge base can expand each factor.

## 17. Trade-offs

| Tension | Possible relationship |
|---|---|
| Cost ↔ quality | Higher-specification materials may improve performance while increasing cost |
| Weight ↔ durability | Strength may require more material and mass |
| Complexity ↔ usability | More functionality may increase cognitive load |
| Novelty ↔ familiarity | Innovation may differentiate a solution while reducing intuitive use |
| Customisation ↔ production efficiency | Individualisation may reduce manufacturing efficiency |
| Precision ↔ production time | Tighter tolerances may require slower or specialised processes |
| Sustainability ↔ immediate cost | Lower-impact alternatives may involve different upfront costs |
| Aesthetics ↔ functionality | Appearance may interfere with performance requirements |
| Flexibility ↔ consistency | Adaptable systems may be harder to standardise |
| Automation ↔ control | Automation may improve efficiency while reducing direct control |

A trade-off does not necessarily require choosing one side completely. Seek an appropriate balance within context.

## 18. Decisions under uncertainty

Designers rarely possess complete information. Decisions can be exploratory, provisional or committed.

- Exploratory decisions investigate possibilities.
- Provisional decisions are currently preferred but subject to testing.
- Committed decisions are sufficiently supported to guide implementation.

This distinction prevents exploratory choices from becoming prematurely final and supports documentation of design evolution.

## 19. Traceability and documentation

A portfolio should make reasoning traceable:

**Need → research → criterion → idea → comparison → decision → prototype → test → refinement → final evaluation.**

Document thinking, investigation, alternatives, decisions, testing, change, production and evaluation. Ask of each page: **What part of the design process does this evidence demonstrate?**

Portfolio development encompasses the design situation, brief, research, success criteria, specifications, idea generation, concept development, tests, trials, production planning, project management, time and finance, ongoing and final evaluation, evidence collection, annotation, justification and reflection.

The portfolio is an evidence trail of development, rather than merely a polished retrospective publication.

## 20. Annotation

Annotation communicates design thinking rather than merely labels features.

| Level | Example |
|---|---|
| Weak | Zipper |
| Better | Concealed zipper |
| Stronger | A concealed zipper maintains a clean external silhouette while allowing the garment to open and close. |
| Sophisticated | A concealed centre-back zipper preserves an uninterrupted front silhouette and provides opening length for dressing. It is more suitable than a visible front fastening because the clean costume profile is a priority in the brief. |

## 21. Project management

Time, sequencing, dependencies, milestones, resources, procurement, skills, risk and contingencies influence design feasibility.

Example: a material's lead time exceeds the schedule. The original material becomes infeasible; alternatives must be evaluated. A new material may change the manufacturing method and require design modification.

## 23. Static and time-based design

Static design often concerns placement, scale, spatial relationships, composition, hierarchy and fixed interaction states.

Time-based design additionally concerns sequence, timing, pacing, duration, transitions, movement, temporal hierarchy, audio synchronisation, entry/exit and repeated exposure. Test the experience over time, not only individual frames.

## 24. Digital design

Digital processes involve versioning, non-destructive editing, rapid duplication, iterative exports, responsive layouts, software compatibility, platform specifications and file management.

Easy modification can support iteration or create unstructured experimentation. Maintain **change → purpose → evaluation**.

## 25. AI-assisted design

Generative AI may support ideation, alternatives, visualisation, wording, research summaries, pattern exploration and rapid variation.

Designers remain responsible for defining the problem, validating evidence, selecting criteria, recognising bias, checking feasibility, considering originality and ethics, evaluating appropriateness and making final decisions.

**AI generation ≠ design justification.** Explain why a generated alternative is suitable.

## 26. Accessibility

Consider accessibility during problem definition and criteria development:

**User diversity → accessibility requirements → criteria → alternatives → inclusive testing → refinement.**

Relevant variables include vision, hearing, mobility, dexterity, cognition, literacy, language, technology access and environment. These may affect form, controls, typography, contrast, interaction, navigation, captions, motion and physical access.

## 27. WHS and risk

Production safety concerns tools, machinery, materials, workflow, PPE, sequencing and workspace. Final-use safety concerns structural failure, sharp edges, entrapment, heat, electrical hazards, misuse and accessibility.

**Constraint → criterion → design feature → production control → test.**

Creative risk is distinct from unsafe risk. Exploration occurs within safe boundaries.

## 28. Sustainability

Sustainability influences problem framing, materials, quantities, production, energy, durability, repair, modularity, transport, packaging, disposal and redesign frequency.

An environmentally preferable decision at one stage may create impacts elsewhere. Consider lifecycle consequences and system relationships rather than a single final evaluation heading.

## 29. Culture, context and ethics

Ask whose needs are represented or excluded, what assumptions are embedded, which symbols are used, whether representation stereotypes, whether cultural knowledge is used appropriately, whether consent is relevant, whether users are manipulated, whether information is deceptive and whether privacy implications exist.

Appropriateness extends beyond technical performance.

## 30. Human-centred and related approaches

| Approach | Emphasis |
|---|---|
| User-centred design | Intended users and their interactions |
| Human-centred design | Human experiences, needs and contexts |
| Participatory design/co-design | Users or stakeholders actively contribute to development |
| Universal design | Usability for the widest practical range without unnecessary specialised adaptation |

These approaches overlap and may coexist; terminology varies between frameworks.

## 31. Linear and iterative models

Linear representations such as **investigate → design → produce → evaluate** help novice learning, planning, communication and assessment organisation, but can imply that earlier decisions never change.

Iterative representations such as **investigate ⇄ define ⇄ generate ⇄ prototype ⇄ test ⇄ refine** better reflect authentic development, but may confuse novices without structure.

Use stages as organisational landmarks rather than rigid one-way gates.

## 32. Common failure modes

| Failure | Consequence or correction |
|---|---|
| Starting with appearance before need/purpose | Rationale becomes retrospective |
| Solution fixation | Alternatives exist only to satisfy portfolio requirements |
| Decorative research | Connect findings to possible implications |
| Criteria written after selection | Derive criteria primarily from need, research and brief |
| Generic justification | Include evidence, effect, user, purpose and comparison |
| Testing without purpose | State the question the trial answers |
| Testing without refinement | Use evidence to inform decisions |
| Evaluation without evidence | Identify criterion, evidence, success and limitations |
| Portfolio completion mindset | Record decisions rather than simply complete pages |
| Excessive iteration | Weigh improvement against time, cost and resources |

## 33. Common misconceptions

| Misconception | Better interpretation |
|---|---|
| Design follows a fixed sequence | Stages organise an iterative process |
| The brief specifies exactly what to make | Briefs define challenge and boundaries |
| More ideas automatically means better design | Alternatives need meaningful variation and evaluation |
| Research is background information | Research reduces uncertainty and informs decisions |
| Innovation requires something completely new | Improvement or recombination can provide meaningful value |
| Sustainability is obvious | Compare consequences across a lifecycle |
| User preference determines the solution | Interpret preferences alongside safety, function and context |
| Testing proves a design is good | Testing generates evidence requiring interpretation |
| Evaluation belongs at the end | Evaluation operates throughout development |
| Final designs should never change | Production can reveal new information |
| Justification means opinion | Connect reasons and evidence to suitability |

## 34. Analysis progression

**Identify → describe → explain → analyse → apply → justify → evaluate → refine.**

- Identify: name the feature, requirement, property or decision.
- Describe: provide relevant characteristics.
- Explain: show how or why it works.
- Analyse: examine relationships and consequences.
- Apply: use understanding in the project.
- Justify: defend decisions using evidence and context.
- Evaluate: judge success against criteria.
- Refine: make an evidence-informed improvement.

Another progression is **observe → interpret → justify → decide**. For example, users grip an object with both hands; bilateral handling and stability matter; a wider symmetrical grip could support this; expand the grip surface, then verify the proposed dimensions through testing.

## 35. Pedagogical scaffolding

Scaffold movement from uncertainty to comparison to explaining appropriateness. Use selection, ranking, comparison, decision matrices, visual choices, guided questions, sentence starters and evidence prompts to reduce unnecessary writing while retaining genuine decisions.

Structure reasoning without prescribing every answer.

| Development | Possible task |
|---|---|
| Novice | Choose between provided alternatives and give a reason |
| Developing | Generate alternatives and compare using supplied criteria |
| Competent | Develop criteria from research and evaluate alternatives |
| Advanced | Prioritise criteria, identify trade-offs, challenge assumptions, test and justify refinement |

## 36. Practical skill pedagogy

Students explore a technique, observe capabilities and limitations, reconsider design possibilities, apply it and evaluate the result.

**Technical knowledge → expanded design possibilities.**

**Design intent → need for technical learning.**

## 37. Differentiation

Support can include teacher-selected sources, visual prompts, word banks, partly completed comparison tables, fewer alternatives, sentence starters, oral explanation, symbols and modelling.

Extension can include independent sourcing, competing constraints, weighted matrices, stakeholder conflict, ambiguous briefs, sustainability analysis, alternative production systems, optimisation and deeper justification.

Preserve the underlying learning about design decision-making.

## 38. Weak-to-strong examples

### Material choice

- Weak: “I chose cotton because it is good.”
- Developing: “I chose cotton because it is comfortable and easy to sew.”
- Strong: “Cotton was selected because softness, breathability and ease of construction support a comfortable school-use textile product and the available equipment.”
- Sophisticated: “Cotton woven fabric was selected after comparison with polyester because comfort, construction and workshop suitability were weighted more highly than moisture resistance. Sample-specific properties should be checked through specifications and practical trials.”

### Design development

- Weak: “I made the handle bigger.”
- Developing: “I made the handle bigger so it would be easier to hold.”
- Strong: “User trials showed insufficient clearance for a comfortable grip, so the internal height was increased.”
- Sophisticated: “Users with larger hands could not obtain clearance. Internal height was increased while retaining wall thickness. Retest the prototype because the larger opening may affect structural stiffness.”

## 39. Strong design questions

| Thinking | Questions |
|---|---|
| Identification | What is the need? Who is the user? What is the purpose? |
| Description | What characteristics and constraints are relevant? |
| Explanation | Why does the feature affect performance? How does the material meet requirements? |
| Analysis | How do variables interact? What secondary consequences could follow? |
| Justification | Why is this alternative more appropriate here? |
| Evaluation | To what extent is the criterion achieved? What evidence supports the judgement? |
| Refinement | What variable should change, what effect is expected and how will it be retested? |

## 40. Evidence

Claims may require measured data, test results, observed behaviour, user feedback, standards, material specifications, expert information, precedents, research, prototype comparison and production trials.

Evidence strength depends on the claim, relevance and method. Triangulating manufacturer data, practical sample tests and user feedback often supports stronger reasoning than unsupported preference.

## 41. Changing criteria and reframing problems

Criteria may change when investigation reveals misunderstood needs, infeasibility, unforeseen risk, new stakeholder requirements, changed resources or conflicting goals. Document and justify the change.

Problem framing can change the solution space. “Students forget equipment” might suggest a reminder poster. Investigation may show inconsistent storage and poor visibility; storage, organisation and routines become part of the problem.

## 42. Constraint-led innovation

Constraints such as no electricity, recycled materials only, one process, low budget, compact storage or limited assembly time can stimulate alternatives.

Ask: **Given this restriction, what becomes possible?**

Creativity generates valuable possibilities. Innovation applies or develops ideas to produce meaningful value or improvement. Novelty alone is insufficient.

**Novelty + relevance + feasibility/value → stronger innovation.**

## 43. Fixation and premature convergence

Fixation occurs when initial solutions restrict alternatives. Causes can include first-idea attachment, exemplar copying, early high-fidelity prototypes, restrictive references, templates and initial AI outputs.

Countermeasures include multiple alternatives, deliberate variation, analogy, delayed selection, changed constraints and comparison of fundamentally different approaches.

Premature convergence narrows ideas before adequate exploration. Signs include one detailed idea, superficial alternatives, weak comparison and retroactive justification.

**Diverge sufficiently → establish evidence → converge.**

## 44. Decision chains and feedback loops

A stretch-knit selection affects stretch-percentage investigation, pattern dimensions, seam choice, needle choice, machinery and testing. Capture downstream consequences.

- Research loop: **research → criteria → further research**.
- Prototype loop: **prototype → testing → modification → prototype**.
- Production loop: **production issue → adjustment → production retest**.
- Evaluation loop: **criterion → evidence → judgement → refinement → revised evidence**.

## 46. Design maturity and assessment

Maturity concerns relationships between evidence, criteria, decisions, testing and evaluation, rather than simply more detail. A shorter portfolio can demonstrate stronger reasoning than a longer one.

Assessment can consider problem understanding, research relevance, criteria, alternatives, creativity, technical reasoning, comparison, justification, experimentation, refinement, production planning, safety, evaluation and communication.

Avoid rewarding presentation alone when the intended construct is design thinking.

## 48. Authentic design education

Authentic uncertainty gives students opportunities to investigate, compare, decide and evaluate. Craftsmanship remains valuable alongside systems understanding, critical thinking, empathy, technological understanding, sustainability awareness, evidence-based reasoning and creative problem-solving.

A prescribed making activity can teach technique; additional decisions and investigations are needed when assessing design capability.

## 50. Levels of complexity

| Level | Process |
|---|---|
| Introductory | Need → idea → make → evaluate |
| Developing | Situation → research → criteria → alternatives → selection → production → evaluation |
| Strong | Situation → user investigation → criteria → divergent exploration → evidence-based comparison → development → prototyping → iterative testing → production → evaluation |
| Sophisticated | Framing/reframing → contextual research → stakeholders → conflicting/weighted criteria → alternatives → experimentation → systems consequences → iterative validation → implementation → evidence-based evaluation → continued refinement |

## 51. Strong and weak decisions

Strong decisions are relevant to purpose, informed by evidence, traceable, feasible, sensitive to constraints, aware of consequences, tested appropriately and open to refinement. They are defensible within context, rather than universally perfect.

Weak decisions rely on arbitrary preference, aesthetics alone, assumptions, copying, unanalysed convenience, retrospective rationalisation, irrelevant research or untested claims.

“Blue is calming” is insufficient without examining audience, culture, saturation, value, combinations, context and application.

## 52. Decision quality and outcome quality

A good process can produce an imperfect outcome because of production errors, unexpected material behaviour, limited resources or time. A visually successful outcome can arise through chance, copying or teacher intervention without demonstrating strong reasoning.

Assess process quality and artefact quality as related but distinguishable constructs.

## 53. Failure and reflection

Document failure through **what failed → why → evidence → learning → change**. A failed prototype with strong analysis can show more understanding than an untested successful-looking artefact.

Evaluation asks how successful the design was. Reflection asks what the designer learned about decisions, assumptions, process, technical capability and future practice. They overlap but remain distinguishable.

## 54. Supplementary development areas

The supplied knowledge is strongest in TAS project structure, criteria, portfolios, justification, assessment, evaluation, textiles, multimedia and practical production. Further development could include:

- Formal decision theory: multi-criteria analysis, weighting sensitivity, uncertainty, confidence and risk-adjusted choices.
- Systems design: components, feedback, unintended consequences, boundaries and emergent effects.
- Service design: journeys, blueprints, touchpoints and frontstage/backstage systems.
- Participatory design: co-creation, power, representation and conflicting needs.
- Verification versus validation: checking specification compliance versus meeting the intended need.

- Quantitative optimisation: objective functions, constraints, sensitivity and Pareto trade-offs.

These are supplementary areas, not replacements for existing Claude material.

## 57. Overarching conceptual model

Context, situation, need/opportunity and brief establish direction. Purpose, research and stakeholder understanding inform criteria, specifications and constraints. Divergent ideation produces alternatives; analysis, comparison and trade-offs support provisional selection. Development, experimentation and prototyping generate evidence through testing and feedback. Refinement informs production or implementation. Quality control and ongoing evaluation support final evaluation against needs, brief and criteria, followed by reflection and further improvement.

Feedback loops operate throughout; this model is not a rigid sequence.

## 58. Core reasoning principle

> A design decision becomes meaningful when there is a traceable reason why it is appropriate for a particular need, user, purpose and context.

**Understand → establish requirements → create possibilities → gather evidence → decide → test → learn → change → verify.**

The central question is:

> Why is this decision appropriate here?

It connects process, decision-making, factors affecting design, justification, evaluation, refinement, portfolios, practical production and assessment.
