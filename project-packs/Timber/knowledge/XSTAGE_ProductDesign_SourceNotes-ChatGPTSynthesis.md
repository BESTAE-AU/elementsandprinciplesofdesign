## Materials and Manufacturing

### Materials and processes as an interacting system

Material selection and manufacturing method must be developed together. Geometry, tolerances, equipment, surface finish, cost, waste, safety and end use influence feasibility.

**Need → performance requirements → material candidates → process candidates → trials → selection → manufacture → verification.**

### Material families

| Family | Variables to investigate |
|---|---|
| Timber | Species, grain, moisture, density, defects, stability, finish |
| Metals | Alloy, strength, ductility, corrosion, thermal behaviour, machinability |
| Polymers | Type, temperature response, toughness, weathering, joining |
| Textiles | Fibre, structure, stretch, strength, finish, care |
| Composites | Constituents, orientation, bonding, anisotropy, repair |
| Smart materials | Response, trigger, repeatability, lifecycle and real availability |

Strength, stiffness, hardness and toughness are different properties. A strong material may still bend substantially. Performance depends on geometry and loading as well as material data. Directional properties matter in timber, textiles and composites.

### Manufacturing categories

Subtractive processes remove material, such as cutting, drilling, routing or CNC machining. Additive processes build material, including many 3D printing methods. Forming changes shape through forces or heat. Joining connects components mechanically, adhesively or through material processes. Finishing modifies surfaces for protection, performance or appearance.

Digital fabrication is not automatically accurate, sustainable or appropriate. CAD dimensions must account for process behaviour. Laser kerf, tool radius, printer orientation, shrinkage and layer behaviour affect fit. Use test pieces before committing to final parts.

### Tolerances and assembly

A tolerance is an allowable variation from a specified value. Tighter tolerance can increase time and cost. Fit requires a deliberate relationship between mating parts; zero nominal clearance does not guarantee assembly. Build allowances from equipment capability and trials, not universal values.

Design for manufacture considers process access, setups and feasible features. Design for assembly considers part count, orientation, fastening, error prevention and maintenance. Design for disassembly supports repair and recovery where appropriate.

### Quality and safety

Quality assurance plans procedures to prevent defects; quality control checks outputs. Inspect incoming stock, dimensions, alignment, joins, surface and function at appropriate stages.

Use approved school procedures, supervision, machine guards, extraction and task-specific controls. Certain unknown, coated or composite materials may be unsuitable for heat or laser processes; check equipment guidance and material identification before use. This knowledge base is not machine authorisation.

### Sustainability and teaching

Compare material quantity, offcuts, energy, reject rate, durability, repair and disposal. Recycling availability is location- and material-specific. Innovation should identify a benefit compared with a feasible alternative.

Teach through sample joins, process comparisons and controlled dimensional trials. Support with a constrained material/process set. Extend by optimising for conflicting cost, weight, strength and repair criteria. Assess **property → process behaviour → performance → end-use suitability**, with evidence and acknowledged uncertainty.


### Emerging, innovative and sustainable technologies: investigation examples

These examples provide research directions rather than current purchasing recommendations or a claim that every material is available to schools.

| Technology | What students could investigate | Critical comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Additive manufacture | Geometry, infill, support, orientation and waste | Compare strength and total waste against another feasible method |
| Parametric design | Relationships controlled by dimensions/rules | Does variation improve fit or simply produce novelty? |
| Shape-memory materials | Shape response to a trigger | Trigger conditions, repeatability, safety and useful function |
| Phase-change materials | Storage/release of thermal energy | Working range, containment, durability and actual benefit |
| Conductive textiles | Circuits integrated with fabric | Washability, comfort, power, repair and e-waste |
| Recycled composites | Combining recovered materials | Bonding, performance, traceability and end-of-life recovery |
| Bio-based polymers | Feedstocks and processing | Bio-based does not automatically mean biodegradable |
| Mycelium-based materials | Growth, structure and proposed uses | Moisture, consistency, lifespan and manufacturing evidence |
| Digital textile printing | Short runs and image application | Ink, pretreatment, energy, reject rate and care |
| Repair/modular systems | Replaceable components and disassembly | Added complexity versus actual extended use |

For each investigation record the need, technical principle, established application, maturity, available evidence, limitations, lifecycle consequences and realistic classroom application. Separate proven performance from a supplier's marketing claim. A technology may be innovative in one context and established in another.

**Technology → capability → need → measurable benefit → trade-off → feasibility → evaluation.**

## Product and Industrial Design

### Product and industrial design

Product design develops objects or systems to meet needs. Industrial design commonly integrates usability, form, manufacture and market context; its scope can overlap engineering and service design. A visually appealing object must also be evaluated for its intended use.

### User and system investigation

Identify intended users, tasks, environments, frequency, maintenance and disposal. Observe actual behaviour rather than relying solely on stated preference. Distinguish the user's underlying need from a proposed feature.

**Need → requirement → criterion → concept → prototype → evidence → refinement.**

A request for a larger handle may reveal a grip-clearance problem. Compare opening size, shape, surface and location rather than assume only scale needs changing.

### Core design relationships

Form affects handling, manufacture and meaning. A mechanism converts or controls motion/force; it introduces load, tolerance and failure considerations. Material selection interacts with wall thickness, joints, surface, weight and durability. Ergonomics involves reach, posture, grip, force, feedback and movement over realistic use periods.

Anthropometric data describe populations, not one universal user. Consider ranges, adjustment and task-specific dimensions; do not adopt an average as automatically inclusive.

### Development tools

Use sketches to explore structure, CAD to resolve dimensions, low-fidelity models to test shape and prototypes to investigate specific functions. A cardboard model can test reach but cannot establish final load capacity. State the prototype's question and limitations.

Concept matrices help compare alternatives but depend on valid criteria and justified scores. Use non-negotiable requirements, such as relevant safety requirements, as thresholds rather than allowing decorative scores to compensate for failure.

### Manufacturing and lifecycle

Design for manufacture considers feasible processes, tooling, accuracy and waste. Design for assembly considers access, alignment, fastening and mistake prevention. Maintenance, repair, replaceable components, modularity and disassembly may preserve value. Modularity can add parts or complexity; assess the actual trade-off.

Circular design seeks to eliminate waste/pollution, retain products/materials in valuable use and support regenerative systems. See the [Ellen MacArthur Foundation](https://www.ellenmacarthurfoundation.org/circular-economy-principles). These principles do not prove that any specific product has low lifecycle impact.

### Testing and evaluation

Separate verification—meeting stated specifications—from validation—meeting the actual need. A perfectly dimensioned handle can still be uncomfortable. Use observed task performance, dimensional checks, user feedback and appropriate technical testing.

Consider foreseeable misuse and conditions beyond an ideal demonstration. Testing involving loads, mechanisms or electrical components must follow approved supervision and risk controls.

### Teaching

Support with a well-defined user and task, limited materials and modelled comparison. Extend through multiple stakeholders, uncertain data, repair systems and sensitivity of criterion weights.

Assess the evidence chain as well as the realised object. Require annotations explaining why features exist, not merely naming them. A useful decision record includes selection, trigger, evidence, alternative, effect, trade-off, criterion and planned verification.
