## Scaffolded Questioning

Questions should function as **instructional thinking tools**, not merely assessment questions.

### Recognition
- What Principle can you identify?
- Where is it evident?

### Description
- What Elements or relationships create it?
- How are the components arranged?

### Explanation
- What visual or functional effect does this organisation create?

### Analysis
- How do several Elements work together?
- Which Principle is primary?
- Does it influence another Principle?

### Application
- How could you manipulate the design to strengthen, reduce or redirect the Principle?

### Justification
- Why is this level or form appropriate for the user, audience, purpose and context?

### Evaluation
- How effectively does it support the design solution?
- What evidence supports that judgement?

### Refinement
- What should be changed?
- Which Element would you manipulate?
- What effect should the change produce?

Avoid leading questions.

Avoid:

> How does the strong contrast make the poster exciting?

Prefer:

> What effect does the level of contrast have on the poster?

## Common Misconceptions

Use the structure:

**Misconception → Correction → Better way to think about it**

#### Balance means symmetry
**Correction:** Symmetry is one method of balancing visual weight.  
**Better thinking:** Analyse how visual weight is distributed.

#### Contrast means opposite colours
**Correction:** Contrast may occur through value, scale, typography, texture, shape, direction, space, motion and other differences.  
**Better thinking:** Identify the variable that differs and the effect of that difference.

#### Hierarchy means a bigger heading
**Correction:** Scale is one hierarchy tool.  
**Better thinking:** Analyse the combined effects of scale, position, value, spacing, typography and contrast.

#### Unity means sameness
**Correction:** Unity requires coherence, not uniformity.  
**Better thinking:** Identify recurring relationships that connect varied components.

#### Pattern and repetition are the same
**Correction:** Repetition is recurrence; pattern is organised recurrence.  
**Better thinking:** Ask whether a recognisable system is formed.

#### Rhythm is repetition
**Correction:** Rhythm involves recurrence plus interval, sequence, timing or variation.  
**Better thinking:** Analyse cadence and progression.

#### Movement means animation
**Correction:** Movement may be implied in static work.  
**Better thinking:** Trace how attention progresses through the composition or sequence.

#### Proportion and scale are the same
**Correction:** Proportion concerns relationships between parts; scale concerns size relative to a reference or context.

#### Emphasis and hierarchy are the same
**Correction:** Emphasis creates prominence; hierarchy organises several levels of importance.

#### Alignment means everything must line up
**Correction:** Multiple alignment structures can coexist, and deliberate misalignment can be purposeful.

#### Harmony means no conflict
**Correction:** Controlled contrast can exist within harmony.

#### Tension is a mistake
**Correction:** Tension can be deliberately constructed for communication.

#### Variety always improves creativity
**Correction:** Variety must remain appropriate to purpose and system coherence.

## Educational Resource Generation

When generating:

- Canvas pages
- lessons
- workbooklets
- worksheets
- classroom activities
- portfolio scaffolds
- revision resources
- assessment support
- design-analysis activities
- exemplars

move learners through:

**knowledge → recognition → description → explanation → analysis → experimentation → application → justification → evaluation → refinement**

Do not move directly from a definition to independent production.

When useful, include:

- worked examples
- comparison tasks
- visual analysis
- controlled experimentation
- project application
- evaluation prompts
- reflection and refinement

## Principle-Specific Failure Modes

Claude should analyse degrees rather than binary success/failure.

For each Principle consider:

- absent
- weak
- inconsistent
- excessive
- inappropriate
- deliberately disrupted

Examples:

#### Hierarchy
Too little: information may be difficult to prioritise.  
Too much: secondary content may become inaccessible or overlooked.

#### Variety
Too little: insufficient differentiation.  
Too much: reduced unity and increased cognitive load.

#### Contrast
Too little: weak differentiation.  
Too much: fragmentation or competition.

#### Repetition
Too little: weak system consistency.  
Too much: monotony.

#### Balance
Reduced balance may feel unstable, but instability may be intentional.

#### Tension
Low tension may create stability; high tension may create urgency, drama or discomfort.
