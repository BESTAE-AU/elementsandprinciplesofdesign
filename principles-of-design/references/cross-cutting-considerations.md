## Characteristics, Features, Properties and Attributes

Claude should use this framework when it genuinely improves analysis.

### Characteristics

Observable qualities of how the Principle appears.

Examples:

- symmetrical
- irregular
- repeated
- high contrast
- tightly grouped
- strongly aligned
- progressively scaled

### Features

What the Principle enables the design to do.

Examples:

- directs attention
- groups related information
- establishes priority
- creates continuity
- differentiates controls
- supports recognition

### Properties

Relevant measurable, structural or technical conditions.

For abstract Principles, interpret properties appropriately.

Examples:

- spacing interval
- contrast ratio
- type scale
- grid dimensions
- repetition interval
- timing
- duration
- component size
- alignment axis
- modular ratio

Do not invent physical properties where none exist.

### Attributes

How the Principle relates to:

- audience
- purpose
- meaning
- communication
- context
- experience
- cultural interpretation

## Accessibility

Accessibility must be embedded throughout the design analysis.

Consider:

- readability
- legibility
- text contrast
- information hierarchy
- grouping
- colour vision differences
- low vision
- spacing
- cognitive load
- predictable structure
- visual clutter
- motion sensitivity
- reduced-motion preferences
- navigation
- touch targets
- comprehension

Examples:

- hierarchy can aid scanning
- proximity can clarify relationships
- insufficient contrast may reduce legibility
- excessive variety can increase cognitive load
- repeated interface conventions can improve predictability
- excessive motion may create accessibility barriers

Claude should avoid implying that accessibility is a stylistic preference.

## Production and Implementation

Principles must survive real production conditions.

Consider:

- print size
- paper
- ink
- screen resolution
- responsive layout
- aspect ratio
- packaging dimensions
- textile repeat
- embroidery limitations
- laser cutting
- manufacturing tolerances
- signage distance
- animation frame rate
- video format
- compression
- screen size
- lighting conditions

Examples:

- a hierarchy effective on an A3 poster may collapse on mobile
- fine pattern may moiré on screen
- subtle contrast may disappear in print
- repeated embroidery detail may become visually dense at small scale
- a balanced desktop layout may require complete restructuring on mobile

## Sustainability

Do not claim that a visual Principle is inherently sustainable.

Where meaningful, examine whether the design system affects:

- material use
- production complexity
- adaptability
- longevity
- modularity
- reusability
- redesign frequency
- printing
- digital efficiency
- obsolescence

For example, a modular visual system may enable reuse and adaptation, but this only contributes to sustainability if it actually reduces unnecessary production or redesign.

## Culture, Context and Ethics

Perceptions of design relationships may vary with:

- culture
- language
- reading direction
- visual literacy
- age
- medium
- social context
- audience expectations

Claude should consider:

- cultural symbolism
- culturally specific visual conventions
- stereotypes
- appropriation
- deceptive emphasis
- manipulative hierarchy
- misleading contrast
- visual dark patterns
- deliberate obscuring of information
- accessibility
- inclusivity

Do not treat culturally dependent meanings as universal.

## Factors Affecting Design

When relevant, connect Principles to the following Technology and Applied Sciences Factors Affecting Design.

| Factor Affecting Design | Principle-focused considerations |
|---|---|
| Appropriateness of the Design Solution | Does the use of the Principle suit the identified need, audience, context and environment? |
| Needs | Does the organisation respond to an actual user or communication need? |
| Function / Functionality | Does the Principle improve or interfere with intended function? |
| Aesthetics | What visual or sensory effect does the relationship create, and is it purposeful? |
| Finance / Cost | Does implementing the Principle increase production, development or maintenance cost? |
| Ergonomics | Do scale, grouping, proportion or hierarchy support comfortable and efficient use? |
| Work Health and Safety / Safety | Could the visual organisation obscure warnings, controls or safe operation? |
| Quality | Is the Principle implemented consistently and with suitable technical resolution or finish? |
| Resources | What materials, technologies, software, time and skills are required? |
| Short-Term and Long-Term Environmental Consequences | Does implementation create avoidable waste or production impact? |
| Sustainability | Can the system adapt, be reused or reduce unnecessary redesign? |
| Obsolescence | Is the solution overly dependent on short-lived visual trends or technologies? |
| Life Cycle Analysis | Where relevant, how does the design system affect production, use, maintenance and end-of-life? |
| Accessibility | Does the Principle support equitable perception, comprehension and interaction? |
| Ethics | Does the design organisation inform clearly or manipulate, obscure or exclude? |

Do not force irrelevant Factors into every response.

Questions should help decision-making rather than merely repeat definitions.

## Design Trade-Offs

Claude must surface trade-offs.

Do not treat Principles as automatically positive.

Use:

**possible advantage ↔ possible disadvantage**

Examples:

#### Unity
coherence and recognition ↔ insufficient differentiation

#### Harmony
compatibility and comfort ↔ lack of emphasis

#### Balance
stability ↔ predictability

#### Alignment
order ↔ rigidity

#### Proximity
grouping ↔ crowding

#### Proportion
clear relationships ↔ exaggerated dominance

#### Hierarchy
priority ↔ secondary information may be overlooked

#### Emphasis
focus ↔ competing or suppressed content

#### Contrast
differentiation ↔ fragmentation

#### Repetition
consistency ↔ monotony

#### Pattern
recognition ↔ visual density

#### Rhythm
flow ↔ repetitive predictability

#### Movement
guidance ↔ distraction

#### Tension
energy ↔ discomfort or instability

#### Variety
interest ↔ reduced unity

Trade-offs must be interpreted in relation to purpose and context.

## Intentional Rule-Breaking

Principles are not absolute rules.

Professional designers may deliberately:

- disrupt alignment
- break repetition
- create imbalance
- reduce harmony
- flatten hierarchy
- exaggerate contrast
- distort proportion
- create tension
- use irregular rhythm
- interrupt pattern
- introduce extreme variety

The key question is not:

> Did the designer follow the rule?

Instead ask:

> What effect does the organisational decision create, and is that effect appropriate for the intended purpose, audience, user and context?

Intentional departure can be valid when it is deliberate, controlled and justified.
