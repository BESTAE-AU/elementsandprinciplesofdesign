# Alignment

## Definition

Alignment is the deliberate positioning of elements in relation to shared edges, centres, axes, baselines, grids or spatial structures.

## Core logic

Alignment creates visible or implied relationships.

It can:

- organise information
- create continuity
- connect distant elements
- improve scanning
- reinforce hierarchy
- strengthen unity

## Types and forms

- edge alignment
- centre alignment
- baseline alignment
- grid alignment
- axis alignment
- modular alignment
- optical alignment
- deliberate misalignment

## Optical alignment

Mathematical alignment does not always appear visually aligned. Curves, pointed shapes, circular icons and letterforms may require optical adjustment.

## Elements

Alignment strongly affects typography, space, layout, line, shape, form and composition.

In motion, elements may align temporarily as part of a transition or sequence.

## Relationships

- alignment often supports unity
- alignment + proximity can establish grouping
- alignment helps clarify hierarchy
- deliberate misalignment may create emphasis, tension or movement

## Applications

Graphic design: text columns, grids, image systems.  
Branding: consistent templates and lock-ups.  
UI: component grids, labels, controls and responsive structure.  
Textiles: seam placement, motif alignment and repeat matching.  
Product design: controls, interfaces, joins and component relationships.  
Spatial design: sightlines, furniture, architecture and wayfinding.

## Accessibility

Consistent alignment can aid scanning and comprehension. Overly centred long text may reduce readability.

## Trade-offs

- order ↔ rigidity
- consistency ↔ reduced spontaneity
- precise alignment ↔ potential optical awkwardness
- disruption ↔ emphasis but possible confusion

## Misconception

**Misconception:** All elements should align to the same edge.  
**Correction:** Designs may use multiple intentional alignment systems within a coherent grid.
