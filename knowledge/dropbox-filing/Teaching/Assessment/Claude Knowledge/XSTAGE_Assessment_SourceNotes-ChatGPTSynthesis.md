# Assessment, rubrics, marking and feedback

> Source: ChatGPT knowledge collection (topic 26, 27, 28), imported and trimmed on 1 October 2026. Per-element boilerplate is removed because the element-of-design skills cover it. Treat as reference notes: check claims against current syllabus, policy and manufacturer documentation before use.

## Assessment Design

### Assessment as evidence of learning

Assessment gathers and interprets evidence for a purpose. Assessment for learning informs teaching and next steps; assessment as learning develops student monitoring and judgement; assessment of learning reports achievement. Formative describes use of evidence to improve learning, not simply a low-stakes task type.

**Outcomes → learning evidence → task → criteria → teaching opportunities → assessment → interpretation → response.**

Backward design starts with intended learning and acceptable evidence. Constructive alignment connects outcomes, learning activities and assessment. The assessment should make it possible to demonstrate the target knowledge and skills without unrelated barriers.

### Design quality

- Validity: whether the intended interpretation of results is supported by evidence.
- Reliability: consistency of judgement under relevant conditions.
- Equity: fair opportunities and interpretation, acknowledging access and context.
- Authenticity: meaningful application, rather than simply a realistic-looking task.
- Transparency: clear evidence requirements, standards and submission conditions.

A visually polished portfolio may be weak evidence of independent design reasoning. An oral explanation may reveal reasoning more directly if writing is not the construct. If technical procedure is the construct, a written description alone may not establish performance.

### Assessment notifications

State course, stage/year, task title, type, marks, weighting, due date, submission process, outcomes, instructions, evidence, criteria and any provided conditions. Separate required deliverables from illustrative examples. Check numerical totals and consistency between notification, rubric and LMS.

Do not invent school late-submission rules, official outcomes or AI permissions. Identify the governing school policy and syllabus version when needed.

### Cognitive demand

Command words change expected evidence. Identify requires recognition; explain requires relationships or reasons; analyse examines parts and interactions; justify supports a decision; evaluate judges using criteria and evidence. Do not ask for evaluation but reward only description.

For project assessment, distinguish concept, research, development, production and evaluation. Avoid double counting the same quality under multiple vague criteria. Allow students to demonstrate process through evidence collected during development, not only retrospective claims.

### Item design and accessibility

The Wayground Builder plugin specifies short multiple-choice/multi-select stems, generally capped at 30 words unless reading is the construct, parallel plausible distractors, no trick wording, text access for essential visuals and caption/transcript/summary provision for video. These are plugin guardrails, not universal assessment rules or proof of complete WCAG conformance.

Check whether alternative access accidentally supplies the answer. A data table can provide access to a graph, but a summary stating the assessed conclusion may change the construct. Seek an equivalent pathway where necessary.

### Moderation and integrity

Use anchor samples, independent marking of a sample, discussion of evidence and documented resolutions. Teach attribution and define permitted assistance. AI detection style impressions are not proof of authorship; use process evidence and school procedures.

Support students with success criteria, non-identical worked examples and staged checkpoints. Extend through deeper application and reasoning, not additional unrelated requirements. [NESA formative assessment guidance](https://curriculum.nsw.edu.au/assessment-and-reporting/formative-assessment) provides a current reference for using evidence during learning.


### Auditing a task before issue

Check five relationships: the syllabus demands what the task asks; instruction prepares students for that demand; deliverables make it observable; rubric criteria describe its quality; marks and weighting reflect its importance.

Then inspect arithmetic, dates, submission locations, required quantities, conflicting instructions and resource assumptions. If two platform-adapted videos are required, state whether the same footage may be repurposed and what distinct evidence each version must show. Do not leave this as a hidden marking expectation.

### Diagnostic versus summative inference

A diagnostic question may locate a misconception but not support a final grade. A final artefact may demonstrate production quality while failing to reveal research or independent decision-making. Combine evidence sources according to the construct rather than collecting everything indiscriminately.

An unfamiliar assessment format can create a barrier. Familiarise learners with the response form without supplying the graded answer. Worked examples should illustrate transferable reasoning and quality while leaving new content or decisions for the learner.

## Rubric and Marking Design

### Rubrics describe quality

A rubric relates criteria to levels of performance. A checklist records completion; it does not by itself describe quality. An analytic rubric separates criteria; a holistic rubric supports an overall judgement; a single-point rubric states an expected standard with space for evidence of strengths and development.

**Outcome → task evidence → criterion → quality dimensions → observable progression → moderation.**

### Descriptor construction

Each criterion should assess one coherent quality or a justified group of qualities. Keep dimensions consistent across levels: relevance, precision, development, evidence, independence or integration. Avoid changing what is being judged midway across the row.

Do not rely on “excellent”, “good” or “limited” without observable evidence. “Uses relevant testing evidence to justify a refinement” is more assessable than “shows good evaluation”. Completion counts may be useful requirements but do not automatically measure sophistication.

### Established school terminology

The user's preferred progression is **E elementary → D basic → C sound → B thorough → A extensive**. Use it as agreed terminology, not as an automatic official mapping to any numeric total. Confirm the assessment's mark scheme and applicable standards.

| Level label | Illustrative reasoning distinction |
|---|---|
| Elementary | Identifies a choice with little relevant support |
| Basic | Gives a simple relevant reason with limited explanation |
| Sound | Explains a suitable choice and relates it to purpose |
| Thorough | Justifies choices with developed evidence and contextual links |
| Extensive | Integrates evidence, alternatives, consequences and justified judgement |

This table illustrates progression; it is not a universal rubric to apply unchanged.

### Avoiding overlap

In Textiles, appropriate design concerns aesthetic/functional suitability; creativity concerns meaningful originality; manufacture concerns technical execution. Do not award the same decorative choice three times unless distinct evidence supports distinct constructs.

Presentation should carry only the weight justified by the outcome. Language accuracy should not dominate a practical criterion unless communication accuracy is explicitly assessed.

### Command words and advanced reasoning

Analyse relationships, justify appropriateness and evaluate success using evidence. Reflection considers learning; reflexivity examines assumptions, positionality and effects on others. Ethical reasoning can assess issue recognition, perspectives, principles, consequences, complexity and justified action rather than a predetermined opinion.

Argumentation can assess claim, evidence, reasoning, alternatives, counterargument and conclusion. The plugins identify Kuhn, Freire, Haynes, Cam and Rachels as conceptual references; consult actual texts before attributing a particular formal framework.

### Feedback and moderation

Provide marker notes on expected evidence, boundary decisions, assistance and missing components. Compare anonymised anchor samples and document disagreements. A total score should be traceable to criterion judgements; do not let an overall impression replace evidence.

Feedback banks should include criterion-specific strengths, a next action and how success can be checked. Student-friendly versions can use accessible success criteria without weakening the standard.

Audit alignment, bias, hidden expectations, language/cultural load, overlapping criteria, uneven progression, weighting and accessibility. Support assessment design rather than guarantee grades. For university contexts, use requested grade categories and the institution's supplied standards.


### Worked descriptor audit

Criterion: justification of equipment selection.

Weak level progression: “poor”, “fair”, “good”, “very good”, “excellent”. These terms do not identify what the marker should see.

Stronger dimensions: relevance to the intended technique; explanation of capability; connection to audience/purpose; evidence or comparison; awareness of limitation. Keep those dimensions stable across the scale while increasing precision, development and integration.

A sound response might explain why stable support enables the selected shot. A more developed response compares feasible supports, uses trial evidence and explains the portability–stability trade-off. The mark must follow the supplied rubric, not this general illustration alone.

### Borderline decisions

Identify which evidence meets the higher level and what is still at the lower level. Avoid averaging adjectives. Use the documented marking approach for mixed evidence and record the rationale. If the rubric cannot handle a common type of work, clarify it before marking all submissions rather than inventing a different rule for each student.

### Reflective and ethical criteria

Reward analysis of assumptions, evidence, effects on others and implications for practice. An emotional disclosure is not automatically a sophisticated reflection, and an unconventional ethical conclusion is not automatically weak if the reasoning is sound and the task permits it.

## Feedback and Evaluation

### Feedback, evaluation and reflection

Feedback supplies information used to improve learning or work. Evaluation judges quality or success against grounds. Reflection examines learning, decisions and future practice. They connect but should not be treated as interchangeable.

**Goal/criterion → evidence → judgement → next action → opportunity to act → revised evidence.**

Feedback is effective only when understandable and usable in context. More comments are not automatically more useful. Prioritise the changes most likely to improve the target learning.

### Feedback levels and focus

Comments can address a task, a process, self-monitoring or future transfer. Avoid replacing precise feedback with praise of fixed traits. “Your comparison uses two relevant test results” identifies evidence; “You are talented” does not tell a learner what to repeat or improve.

A useful comment identifies what is working, the evidence, the gap and a concrete next step. The user prefers improvement advice stating both what needs development and how to improve it. Where a current mark or level is authorised and evidenced, state it clearly using the correct terminology; otherwise identify the judgement as provisional.

### Example

Weak: “Add more detail.”

Stronger: “Your material selection gives a sound link to end use. Strengthen the justification by comparing the tested sample with one alternative, explaining the durability–weight trade-off and stating why the selected option best meets your criteria.”

Do not imply a guaranteed higher mark for making one change. Improvement depends on quality across the assessed criteria.

### Design evaluation

Use **criterion → evidence → judgement → limitation → improvement**. Include incomplete or contradictory evidence. Separate observed performance from intended effect.

Ongoing evaluation informs development; final evaluation summarises achieved performance and remaining limits. A preference statement such as “I like it” cannot establish suitability for a user or brief.

### Self and peer assessment

Teach the criteria using exemplars and modelled judgements before asking students to assess independently. Peer feedback requires a focused question and respectful evidence-based language. Students can identify a problem without knowing the best solution; the designer retains responsibility for interpretation.

Use comparison, highlighting evidence and revision plans rather than asking novices for an unsupported grade.

### Delivery and action

Choose written, oral, annotated visual or recorded feedback according to access and task. Provide captions/transcripts where appropriate. Build revision time into the sequence. Track whether a learner acted on feedback and whether the change improved performance.

For parent/student progress reports, distinguish submitted, missing, partial, unreadable and not yet assessed. Avoid translating missing access into a claim of non-completion.

### Teaching and assessment applications

Support with a limited criterion set, sentence stems and one priority. Extend through conflicting feedback, evidence quality and independent refinement plans. Assess use of feedback through changed work and reasoning, not merely a completed reflection sheet.

An AI-generated comment requires checking against the actual work and rubric. It must not invent evidence, claim to have inspected missing pages or substitute for the teacher's final judgement.


### Feedback triage

First resolve a misunderstanding that blocks the task. Then address an evidence gap or process weakness. Finally refine precision and presentation. This is a practical priority sequence, not a claim that aesthetics are unimportant.

Match advice to what the student can do next. A learner cannot act on “improve production quality” without a specific checkpoint, demonstration or trial. A useful target might be consistent seam allowance verified on a sample, corrected dialogue timing checked in playback, or a comparison using an actual test result.

### Checking the effect

Compare the revised work against the same criterion. Did the change improve the intended quality? Did it introduce another problem? What should be retained? Feedback loops can expose a weak criterion or an incorrect assumption, not only a student error.

### Parent-facing reports

Use clear, factual language: current evidence, achieved standard if assessed, remaining requirements and practical priorities. Separate teacher judgement from an automated draft. Avoid comments about motivation, intelligence or intent that cannot be substantiated. A report should explain the next action without requiring the parent to decipher internal rubric terminology.
