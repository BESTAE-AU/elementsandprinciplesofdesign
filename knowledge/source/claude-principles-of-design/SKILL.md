# Principles of Design

This skill is a design-thinking system, not a glossary or a rulebook. Treat the Principles as
**relational and organisational concepts**: they describe how Elements are organised, related,
differentiated, repeated, grouped, sequenced or perceived. The goal is to help designers and
learners explain *why* a decision works — what was changed, which Elements were manipulated, which
Principle resulted, how Principles interacted, what effect that produced, whether it suits the
audience and context, and how it could be tested and refined.

Write in **Australian English** (colour, organisation, emphasise, centre, judgement).

## 1. Elements vs Principles

Keep these distinct — confusing them is the most common conceptual error in design analysis.

**Elements** are the variables a designer manipulates (visual, spatial, material, temporal):
Line · Direction · Shape · Form · Space · Size and Scale · Time and Duration · Value · Colour ·
Texture · Typography · Layout and Composition.

**Principles** describe how Elements are organised and perceived in relation to each other:
Unity · Harmony · Balance · Alignment · Proximity · Proportion · Hierarchy · Emphasis · Contrast ·
Repetition · Pattern · Rhythm · Movement · Tension · Variety.

A Principle is never a physical object in the design.

- Prefer: *Repeated colour, typography and spacing conventions establish unity across the brand system.*
- Avoid: *Unity is an element in the poster.*

Name the **specific variable** manipulated (e.g. "type weight", "interval between modules",
"saturation of the accent"), not just the Element category.

## 2. Core reasoning model

**design intention → design choices → Elements manipulated → relationship between Elements →
Principle created → interaction with other Principles → perceptual effect → communication/function
→ audience or user experience → suitability → trade-offs → evaluation → refinement**

Adapt this to the Principle rather than applying it mechanically. First identify the intrinsic
nature of the Principle — the kind of relationship it concerns (similarity, difference, grouping,
visual weight, spacing, scale relationship, dominance, order, sequencing, recurrence, interval,
directional flow, stability/instability, variation, consistency, coherence).

## 3. Principle interaction and causal reasoning

Principles rarely operate alone. Reason through causal chains where the evidence supports them:

**Elements manipulated → Primary Principle → Secondary Principle(s) → perceptual effect →
communication or functional outcome**

- Value + size + typography → Contrast → Emphasis → Hierarchy
- Shape + colour + type repetition → Repetition → Rhythm + Unity → stronger brand consistency
- Space + alignment + proximity → Grouping → Hierarchy → easier information processing
- Asymmetric placement + opposing direction + scale difference → Tension → Movement + Emphasis
- Repeated timing + recurring motion → Repetition → Rhythm → Unity → coherent motion identity

The **primary** Principle is the one most directly established by the decision; **secondary**
Principles are strengthened, weakened, disrupted or created as a consequence. These chains are
conditional, not universal laws, and relationships often form a network rather than a line (e.g.
contrast can strengthen emphasis and hierarchy while, if overused, weakening unity). Don't force a
chain where the evidence doesn't support one, and don't require a secondary Principle when none is
meaningful.

**Causal reasoning rule:** whenever several Principles are identified, check whether their
relationship can be explained as **Element → Principle → Principle → Effect → Outcome** rather than
listed. Separate cause from correlation — repetition doesn't automatically produce rhythm (interval,
sequence and timing matter), and contrast doesn't automatically produce effective hierarchy (the
differences must match the intended levels of importance). Ask: what decision was made, which
Elements changed, which Principle is most directly established, what follows, and what evidence in
the design supports that reading? Apparently competing Principles (unity ↔ variety, balance ↔
tension, harmony ↔ contrast) often work together productively. In time-based work, analyse *when*
a Principle occurs as well as *where*.

For chain libraries (static, temporal, brand-system, UI/UX), networks, dependencies, positive and
negative interactions, and causal refinement/experimentation templates, read
`references/causal-reasoning.md`.

Don't stack terminology:

- Avoid: *The design uses contrast, hierarchy, emphasis, balance and unity.*
- Prefer: *The strong value and scale difference establishes contrast. This makes the headline the
  primary point of emphasis and places it at the top of the information hierarchy. Repeated left
  alignment then reconnects the contrasting levels into a unified structure.*

## 4. Perceptual foundations

Draw on perception concepts to explain *why* a Principle works: similarity, proximity, continuity,
closure, figure-ground, common region, connectedness, visual weight, salience, grouping, focal
attention, perceptual hierarchy, expectation, predictability, temporal sequencing.

These are tendencies, not laws. Perception shifts with culture, experience, visual literacy, reading
direction, medium, device, viewing distance, motion, environment and accessibility needs. So use
hedged, mechanism-based language — "tends to group", "may be perceived as", "is likely to appear
more dominant", "can direct attention" — rather than claiming universal responses.

## 5. The 15 Principles at a glance

| Principle | Core idea | Key distinction / common misconception |
|---|---|---|
| Unity | Parts perceived as belonging to a coherent whole | Coherent relationships, not uniformity |
| Harmony | Compatible relationships between different parts | Contrast can exist within harmony; unity = whole-system coherence |
| Balance | Distribution of visual, spatial or perceptual weight | Symmetry is one method, not a synonym |
| Alignment | Positioning to shared edges, centres, axes, baselines or grids | Multiple alignment systems can coexist; optical ≠ mathematical |
| Proximity | Using distance to communicate relationships | Intentional use of distance, not just "close together" |
| Proportion | Size relationships among parts of a whole | Scale = size relative to a reference; no ratio guarantees success |
| Hierarchy | Levels of importance, priority or sequence | Many variables beyond "bigger heading" |
| Emphasis | Intentional prominence of one element, area or moment | Hierarchy = several levels; emphasis = focal prominence |
| Contrast | Perceptible difference between conditions | Relational; many types beyond complementary colour; aesthetic ≠ functional contrast |
| Repetition | Recurrence of an element, feature or behaviour | Pattern = *organised* recurrence |
| Pattern | Organised system of recurrence | Not every repeated element forms a pattern |
| Rhythm | Cadence/flow from recurrence, interval, sequence, variation | More than repetition — interval and timing matter |
| Movement | Actual or implied progression of attention through space or time | Static work can imply movement; direction ≠ movement |
| Tension | Controlled instability, opposition or unresolved relationship | Can be deliberate; arises from expectation and deviation |
| Variety | Intentional difference within a design or system | Useful only when it serves purpose and system coherence |

For each Principle's full treatment, read `references/principles/<name>.md`.

## 6. Working protocols

Pick the protocol that matches the task. Each is expanded (with worked examples) in
`references/analysis-justification-feedback.md`, `references/experimentation.md` or
`references/teaching-resources.md`.

### Analysing a design
**identify → describe → explain → analyse → apply → justify → evaluate → refine.**
Identify the Principle and where it appears; describe the Elements, placement and relationships that
create it; explain the perceptual/functional effect; analyse how decisions combine and which
Principle is primary; connect to communication, purpose, audience and context; evaluate against
explicit criteria; propose an evidence-based refinement.

### Critiquing / giving feedback
1. Identify the intended outcome. 2. Describe observable evidence. 3. Name the Elements
manipulated. 4. Explain the Principle. 5. Explain the effect on communication or function.
6. Connect to audience, purpose and context. 7. Note a trade-off where relevant. 8. Recommend a
specific change. 9. Explain how that change should alter the Principle. 10. Encourage testing.

Feedback must be actionable — what to change, which Element, what effect is expected.
- Avoid: *Make it more balanced.*
- Prefer: *Reduce the visual weight in the upper right by decreasing the image scale or adding
  negative space around it. Test against the current version to see whether the composition feels
  more stable without weakening the intended emphasis.*

### Writing a design justification
**design decision → Elements manipulated → primary Principle → secondary Principle(s) where
relevant → perceptual/functional effect → communication outcome → purpose → audience/context →
suitability.** For sophisticated work: **decision → evidence/research → alternative → trade-off →
final selection → evaluation.**

### Generating a design concept or brief
Don't just name Principles. Specify the intended communication outcome, relevant Principles,
Elements to manipulate, spatial or temporal relationships, hierarchy, accessibility requirements,
implementation constraints, system rules, acceptable variation, trade-offs and testing criteria.

> Establish a three-level hierarchy using typographic scale, weight and spacing. Maintain unity
> through one type family and a repeated left-alignment grid. Use one high-contrast accent for
> emphasis rather than multiple competing colours. On mobile, collapse the two-column composition
> into a single sequence while retaining the same information order.

### Evaluating
Evaluation is criterion-referenced, not preference-based. Ask: effective for what? appropriate for
whom? in what context? compared with what alternative? based on what evidence? Judge at the intended
size, medium, device or production condition.

### Refining
**problem observed → evidence → variable changed → expected Principle effect → expected
communication/function effect → test.** Where practical, change one significant variable at a time
so cause and effect stay visible.

### Teaching and resource generation
For lessons, workbooklets, worksheets, Canvas pages, exemplars and assessment support, move learners
through **knowledge → recognition → description → explanation → analysis → experimentation →
application → justification → evaluation → refinement**. Don't jump from a definition straight to
independent production. Use non-leading questions ("What effect does the level of contrast have?",
not "How does the strong contrast make it exciting?"). See `references/teaching-resources.md` for
question banks, vocabulary, misconceptions, differentiation, and the weak → sophisticated exemplars
in `references/analysis-justification-feedback.md`.

## 7. Considerations to weave through (not bolt on)

- **Trade-offs** — no Principle is automatically positive. Frame as *possible advantage ↔ possible
  disadvantage* (e.g. unity: coherence ↔ reduced differentiation; contrast: differentiation ↔
  fragmentation) and interpret against purpose and context.
- **Failure modes as degrees** — absent, weak, inconsistent, excessive, inappropriate or deliberately
  disrupted, rather than pass/fail.
- **Intentional rule-breaking** — the question isn't "was the rule followed?" but "what effect does
  this decision create, and is it appropriate for the purpose, audience and context?"
- **Accessibility** — embedded throughout, never framed as a stylistic preference: legibility,
  functional contrast, colour-vision differences, hierarchy not relying on colour alone, cognitive
  load, touch targets, reduced-motion and vestibular sensitivity.
- **Appearance ≠ proven usability** — don't claim a choice improves usability, accessibility or
  function without explaining the mechanism or evidence.
- **Spatial vs temporal organisation** — in multimedia and motion, Principles can change over time
  (hierarchy shifts, tension builds and releases, rhythm depends on timing).
- **Discipline breadth** — apply beyond graphic design where relevant; branding is analysed as a
  whole identity system across touchpoints, not just the logo.
- **Implementation** — Principles must survive real production: print size, screen and responsive
  reflow, textile repeat, manufacturing tolerance, signage distance, frame rate, compression.
- **Culture and ethics** — interpretations are contextual, not universal; flag manipulative hierarchy,
  deceptive emphasis, dark patterns and obscured information.
- **Factors Affecting Design** (NESA TAS) and **Characteristics / Features / Properties /
  Attributes** — use when they genuinely sharpen the analysis; don't force irrelevant factors in.

Detail for all of these is in `references/cross-cutting-considerations.md`.

## 8. Language

Prefer verbs that describe mechanisms: establishes, contributes to, reinforces, disrupts,
differentiates, groups, separates, directs, emphasises, balances, creates visual weight, creates
cohesion, establishes hierarchy, guides attention, may communicate, may be perceived as, is
appropriate because.

Avoid unsupported verdicts — "looks good/bad", "is professional", "is boring", "is perfect",
"always works" — unless explicit criteria and context back them up. Don't treat preference as
evidence, and don't assume symmetry, harmony or unity are automatically superior.

## 9. Quality check before finalising substantial output

- Principles treated as relational concepts; Elements and specific variables named.
- Closely related Principles distinguished (e.g. hierarchy vs emphasis, repetition vs rhythm).
- Causal chains evidence-based, with primary/secondary Principles where meaningful.
- Links made to communication, function, audience, purpose and context.
- Trade-offs, accessibility and implementation considered; deliberate disruption recognised.
- Relevant disciplines and time-based aspects considered.
- Feedback and refinements specific and testable; evaluation criteria explicit.
- Experimentation encouraged; learners supported to justify decisions.
- Australian English throughout.
- The output explains *why* decisions work, not merely names them.

## 10. Final mental model

Design intention → Element variables selected → Elements manipulated → primary Principle
established → interaction with other Principles → perceptual response → communication / functional
effect → audience or user experience → suitability to purpose and context → trade-offs →
evaluation → refinement.

Treat the Principles of Design as a connected system for reasoning about design decisions, not as
15 disconnected definitions.
