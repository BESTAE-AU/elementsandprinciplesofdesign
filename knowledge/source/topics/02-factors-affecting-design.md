# Factors Affecting Design

## Purpose and provenance

A reference knowledge base for Claude, covering concepts, relationships, application and teaching. This is not an installable skill.

General subject knowledge synthesised for this collection; relevant plugin instructions and verified primary-source links are identified in the collection references.

## Design factors as decision lenses

Factors affecting design are considerations used to establish requirements, compare alternatives, justify selection and evaluate outcomes. They interact; they are not independent boxes to fill in. Relevance depends on user, purpose, context and project stage.

| Factor | Core question | Evidence or design response |
|---|---|---|
| Appropriateness | Is this suitable here? | Brief alignment, user/context testing, comparison |
| Needs | Which need is being addressed? | Observation, interviews, unmet requirements |
| Function/functionality | What must it do and how well? | Performance criteria, task completion, reliability |
| Aesthetics | How does appearance support experience or meaning? | Composition, material treatment, contextual audience response |
| Finance/cost | Is it affordable across its life? | Materials, labour, equipment, maintenance and replacement costs |
| Ergonomics | How does it fit people and their activities? | Reach, grip, posture, movement, anthropometric range |
| WHS/safety | What could cause harm in production or use? | Hazard identification, controls, supervised trials |
| Quality | Which standards of performance and finish matter? | Tolerances, durability, defect checks, process checkpoints |
| Resources | What is available and feasible? | Time, skills, equipment, materials, energy, information |
| Environmental consequences | What effects occur now and later? | Resource use, waste, emissions, transport, disposal |
| Sustainability | Can value be maintained responsibly over time? | Environmental, social and economic consequences |
| Obsolescence | What makes it stop being useful? | Wear, incompatibility, changing needs, repairability |
| Life-cycle analysis | Where do impacts arise across the system? | Defined boundaries, comparable functional units, lifecycle evidence |
| Accessibility | Who can perceive, understand and use it? | Diverse users, alternative cues, access testing |
| Ethics | Whose interests and rights are affected? | Consent, representation, labour, privacy, honest claims |

## Relationships and boundaries

Ergonomics concerns human fit and interaction; accessibility considers barriers across a broader diversity of abilities and circumstances. Safety and accessibility overlap but neither substitutes for the other. Quality is fitness for agreed requirements, not simply an expensive finish. Function is what is performed; functionality concerns capabilities and their usable operation.

Sustainability is broader than recyclability. Natural fibres, digital delivery and fewer colours are not automatically lower impact. Durability is valuable when use continues, but excessive material for an unnecessarily long life may introduce other costs. Obsolescence may be physical, technical, functional or perceived; distinguish these before proposing repair or replacement.

Formal lifecycle assessment requires explicit boundaries, data quality and comparable service delivered. Classroom lifecycle mapping can identify likely impacts but should not be presented as a complete quantified assessment.

## Applying the factors

**Context → relevant factor → requirement → alternative → evidence → trade-off → decision → test.**

Example: a school bag must be light, durable, comfortable and affordable. Compare fabrics, seam construction and strap systems using specimen tests and user trials. Explain whether durability, weight or cost was prioritised and why. Identify uncertainties rather than claiming one fabric is universally best.

WHS planning can use the hierarchy of controls; consult current school procedures and the machine manual. PPE is one control, not the complete risk-management strategy. See [Safe Work Australia](https://www.safeworkaustralia.gov.au/safety-topic/managing-health-and-safety/identify-assess-and-control-hazards/managing-risks).

## Teaching and assessment

Support learners with two contrasting alternatives and a small set of relevant criteria. Extend through conflicting stakeholder needs, lifecycle consequences and weighting sensitivity. Assess the reasoning linking factors to decisions; do not reward a long generic list detached from the project.

Common weak claim: “This is sustainable because it is recycled.” Stronger response specifies the material, process, lifespan, repair and disposal context, supported by available evidence. Where evidence is incomplete, explain the uncertainty and the next investigation required.

### Colour

#### Factors Affecting Design

| Factor Affecting Design | Questions and considerations for Colour |
|---|---|
| **Appropriateness of the Design Solution** | Does the use of colour suit the identified need, user, audience, context, environment and communication purpose? Would another treatment be more appropriate? |
| **Needs** | What genuine user, communication, functional or contextual need is the colour decision responding to? Is the need evidenced or assumed? |
| **Function / Functionality** | What must the design do, and how does colour support or obstruct that task? Distinguish primary from secondary functions. |
| **Aesthetics** | How does colour contribute to the intended visual/sensory character? Describe observable qualities before interpreting them. |
| **Finance / Cost** | Does the colour choice alter development time, material use, tooling, reproduction, licensing, maintenance or production cost? |
| **Ergonomics** | Does colour affect human interaction, comfort, reach, visibility, grip, cognition or ease of use? Only apply where meaningful. |
| **WHS / Safety** | Could the colour decision introduce production, use, maintenance or disposal hazards? Safety requirements override stylistic preference. |
| **Quality** | Will the colour choice reproduce, manufacture, wear and perform consistently at the required standard? |
| **Resources** | What software, tools, materials, skills, energy, time or specialist processes are required to realise the colour decision? |
| **Short- and Long-Term Environmental Consequences** | What immediate waste/energy/material impacts and longer-term impacts arise from producing and maintaining the colour choice? |
| **Sustainability** | Does the decision support durability, adaptability, repair, reuse, lower impact or responsible resource use without making superficial claims? |
| **Obsolescence** | Could the colour treatment become technically incompatible, hard to update, trend-dependent or prematurely dated? |
| **Life Cycle Analysis** | Where relevant, how does the decision affect extraction, manufacture, distribution, use, maintenance and end-of-life? |
| **Accessibility** | Can people with different sensory, physical, cognitive and technological needs perceive, understand or use the design? |
| **Ethics** | Could the colour treatment mislead, stereotype, manipulate, exclude, appropriate or misrepresent? Are cultural meanings handled responsibly? |

#### Design trade-offs

Claude must surface trade-offs explicitly. A strong design justification acknowledges what is gained and what may be compromised.

Examples for colour include:
- **simplicity ↔ expression** — simplification can improve recognition and reproduction but remove nuance;
- **visibility ↔ subtlety** — stronger treatment improves noticeability but may dominate the hierarchy;
- **consistency ↔ variety** — system consistency builds recognition but excessive uniformity can reduce flexibility;
- **novelty ↔ longevity** — an unusual treatment can increase distinctiveness but may date quickly or be harder to implement;
- **quality ↔ cost** — specialist materials/processes may improve finish but increase expense and production risk;
- **impact ↔ accessibility** — dramatic treatments can attract attention yet impair reading, comfort or navigation;
- **precision ↔ craft character** — controlled geometry can improve repeatability while irregularity may support a handmade identity;
- **cross-platform consistency ↔ medium optimisation** — exact replication can fail; optical adaptation may preserve the design intent more successfully.
