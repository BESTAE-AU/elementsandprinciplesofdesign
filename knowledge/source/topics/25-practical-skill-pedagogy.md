## Teaching practical and procedural skills

Practical competence combines knowing what to do, how to do it, why it works, when it is appropriate and how to recognise an error. Observation alone does not establish competence.

**Purpose → readiness → model → guided attempt → feedback → repeated practice → independent performance → transfer.**

## Planning a demonstration

Define the procedure, prerequisite knowledge, tools/materials, hazards, expected result and quality checkpoints. Arrange visibility, orientation, camera view and audio so learners can perceive the essential action. Avoid performing and speaking through too many simultaneous decisions.

Model a meaningful chunk, explain the rationale and show the check. Distinguish normal variation from a fault. Show common errors safely through samples or representations rather than creating hazardous demonstrations.

Think-alouds reveal decisions: “I am checking grain direction because this panel must remain stable.” Repeatability and clarity matter more than a polished performance that hides decision-making.

## Instructional structure

| Component | Useful content |
|---|---|
| Purpose | What the procedure achieves and where it is used |
| Prerequisites | Knowledge and skills required first |
| Preparation | Tools, materials, setup and approved controls |
| Steps | One clear action or coherent action group |
| Rationale | Why an action or sequence matters |
| Checkpoint | Observable evidence of correct progress |
| Troubleshooting | Symptom, possible cause, safe response |
| Completion | Inspection, clean-up, storage and reflection |

Photographs should show relevant orientation, hand position or layer order and include alternatives for essential visual information. Number procedures where order matters; do not use writing lines when the user has requested open response space.

## Practice and feedback

Guided practice permits correction before errors become habitual. Use samples before a final product when mistakes are costly. Reduce scaffolding as performance becomes reliable; return support when a new material or context adds complexity.

Feedback should identify the error, the next adjustment and the evidence of success. Do not overload a novice with every refinement at once. Promote student self-checking using clear checkpoints rather than relying only on teacher inspection.

## Safety and independence

Competence in one machine or material does not authorise another. Follow school procedures, manufacturer guidance and supervision requirements. A generated procedure must not invent tensions, temperatures, feeds or tolerances. Readiness can include correctly explaining hazards and showing safe setup before operation.

Accessibility may require adapted handles, visual sequence cards, seating, alternative demonstrations, increased time or another way to demonstrate knowledge. Adjustments must preserve the intended competency and safe access.

## Assessment and transfer

Assess process, result, self-checking, independence and ability to explain decisions. Record assistance provided. A successful artefact alone cannot reveal whether the learner performed the skill independently.

Support with short steps and immediate checks; extend by applying a technique to a new material, comparing methods and diagnosing faults. Connect practical learning with design: a technique expands possibilities, and a design requirement creates a reason to learn a technique.


## Example: teaching a seam sample

1. Establish the purpose: a seam must join the selected fabric and withstand the intended movement.
2. Identify the actual machine, fabric and approved setup. Students explain the relevant hazards and controls.
3. Demonstrate alignment and stitching on a sample, making the key check visible.
4. Students practise on samples and inspect consistency, secure ends and fabric behaviour.
5. Feedback identifies one change: alignment, suitable setup or handling according to the evidence.
6. Repeat and compare the sample with the criterion. Explain why the final method is appropriate.

The teacher supplies actual model-specific settings; this example deliberately does not invent them.

## Example: teaching a software procedure

Establish the intended result, show a small number of relevant tools, model one change and explain how to judge it. Provide guided practice using a non-assessed asset. Students export and inspect the file, then repeat the process on a new asset with less support. A screenshot of tool use alone is weaker evidence than a correctly exported result plus an explanation of the decision.

## Troubleshooting reasoning

**Symptom → possible causes → safe check → one adjustment → test → judgement.**

For novices, provide a small decision tree or table with visible symptoms and approved checks. For experienced learners, ask them to distinguish competing causes and justify the diagnostic sequence. Do not encourage unsupervised repairs or machine adjustments outside authorised procedures.
