# Rubric and Marking Design

## Purpose and provenance

A reference knowledge base for Claude, covering concepts, relationships, application and teaching. This is not an installable skill.

Marking Rubrics plugin instructions and the user’s established elementary/basic/sound/thorough/extensive terminology. All example descriptors are illustrative.

## Rubrics describe quality

A rubric relates criteria to levels of performance. A checklist records completion; it does not by itself describe quality. An analytic rubric separates criteria; a holistic rubric supports an overall judgement; a single-point rubric states an expected standard with space for evidence of strengths and development.

**Outcome → task evidence → criterion → quality dimensions → observable progression → moderation.**

## Descriptor construction

Each criterion should assess one coherent quality or a justified group of qualities. Keep dimensions consistent across levels: relevance, precision, development, evidence, independence or integration. Avoid changing what is being judged midway across the row.

Do not rely on “excellent”, “good” or “limited” without observable evidence. “Uses relevant testing evidence to justify a refinement” is more assessable than “shows good evaluation”. Completion counts may be useful requirements but do not automatically measure sophistication.

## Established school terminology

The user's preferred progression is **E elementary → D basic → C sound → B thorough → A extensive**. Use it as agreed terminology, not as an automatic official mapping to any numeric total. Confirm the assessment's mark scheme and applicable standards.

| Level label | Illustrative reasoning distinction |
|---|---|
| Elementary | Identifies a choice with little relevant support |
| Basic | Gives a simple relevant reason with limited explanation |
| Sound | Explains a suitable choice and relates it to purpose |
| Thorough | Justifies choices with developed evidence and contextual links |
| Extensive | Integrates evidence, alternatives, consequences and justified judgement |

This table illustrates progression; it is not a universal rubric to apply unchanged.

## Avoiding overlap

In Textiles, appropriate design concerns aesthetic/functional suitability; creativity concerns meaningful originality; manufacture concerns technical execution. Do not award the same decorative choice three times unless distinct evidence supports distinct constructs.

Presentation should carry only the weight justified by the outcome. Language accuracy should not dominate a practical criterion unless communication accuracy is explicitly assessed.

## Command words and advanced reasoning

Analyse relationships, justify appropriateness and evaluate success using evidence. Reflection considers learning; reflexivity examines assumptions, positionality and effects on others. Ethical reasoning can assess issue recognition, perspectives, principles, consequences, complexity and justified action rather than a predetermined opinion.

Argumentation can assess claim, evidence, reasoning, alternatives, counterargument and conclusion. The plugins identify Kuhn, Freire, Haynes, Cam and Rachels as conceptual references; consult actual texts before attributing a particular formal framework.

## Feedback and moderation

Provide marker notes on expected evidence, boundary decisions, assistance and missing components. Compare anonymised anchor samples and document disagreements. A total score should be traceable to criterion judgements; do not let an overall impression replace evidence.

Feedback banks should include criterion-specific strengths, a next action and how success can be checked. Student-friendly versions can use accessible success criteria without weakening the standard.

Audit alignment, bias, hidden expectations, language/cultural load, overlapping criteria, uneven progression, weighting and accessibility. Support assessment design rather than guarantee grades. For university contexts, use requested grade categories and the institution's supplied standards.


## Worked descriptor audit

Criterion: justification of equipment selection.

Weak level progression: “poor”, “fair”, “good”, “very good”, “excellent”. These terms do not identify what the marker should see.

Stronger dimensions: relevance to the intended technique; explanation of capability; connection to audience/purpose; evidence or comparison; awareness of limitation. Keep those dimensions stable across the scale while increasing precision, development and integration.

A sound response might explain why stable support enables the selected shot. A more developed response compares feasible supports, uses trial evidence and explains the portability–stability trade-off. The mark must follow the supplied rubric, not this general illustration alone.

## Borderline decisions

Identify which evidence meets the higher level and what is still at the lower level. Avoid averaging adjectives. Use the documented marking approach for mixed evidence and record the rationale. If the rubric cannot handle a common type of work, clarify it before marking all submissions rather than inventing a different rule for each student.

## Reflective and ethical criteria

Reward analysis of assumptions, evidence, effects on others and implications for practice. An emotional disclosure is not automatically a sophisticated reflection, and an unconventional ethical conclusion is not automatically weak if the reasoning is sound and the task permits it.
