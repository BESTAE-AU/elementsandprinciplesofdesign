## Visual literacy and analysis

Visual literacy is the capacity to interpret, question and create visual meaning. Art analysis examines observable qualities and relationships, contextual evidence and interpretations. A confident interpretation is not the same as a substantiated one.

**Observe → describe → relate → interpret → contextualise → evaluate.**

Begin with what is visible: material, scale, colour, line, shape, form, space, texture, composition, symbols and relationships. Distinguish observation from inference. “The figure is isolated at the edge” is an observation; “the figure experiences exclusion” is an interpretation that needs support.

## Evidence sources

| Evidence | Contribution | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Artwork | Visible relationships and material effects | Reproduction may alter scale, colour or texture |
| Artist statement | Intention and process | Does not determine every audience reading |
| Historical context | Conditions, events and conventions | Avoid reducing work to one event |
| Critical writing | Interpretive arguments | Evaluate the writer's evidence and position |
| Audience reception | Different experiences and meanings | One response is not universal |
| Exhibition context | Placement and relationships | Meaning may differ in other settings |

Formal analysis explores how visual decisions work together. Iconographic inquiry investigates imagery and symbols using context. Critical analysis can examine power, representation, exclusions and assumptions. Comparative analysis identifies meaningful similarities and differences rather than listing unrelated details.

## Reasoning and writing

**Claim → visual evidence → explanation → contextual support → implication.**

Weak: “The painting is sad because it is blue.” Stronger: “The restricted cool palette, downturned posture and isolated placement contribute to a subdued reading; the interpretation should be considered alongside the work's title and context.” Colour association alone is insufficient.

Use verbs such as establishes, contrasts, directs, fragments, obscures, frames, reinforces and questions. Specify where evidence occurs and how it supports the claim. Discuss ambiguity when several readings are plausible.

Evaluation asks how effectively an artwork establishes a particular relationship or communicates an idea, according to stated grounds. It should not simply rank artworks by personal taste.

## Access and ethics

Use high-quality reproductions with creator, title, date, medium and source where known. Do not invent metadata. Provide image descriptions that support access; distinguish descriptive alternatives from interpretive model answers.

Respect culturally restricted knowledge, representation and reproduction permissions. Cultural symbolism requires appropriate sources and authority. Do not ask Aboriginal students to explain an entire culture or assume an artwork belongs to one generic Indigenous style.

## Teaching progression

Support close looking through a short observation table and vocabulary. Develop comparison with one shared question. Extend through competing interpretations, critical literature and positionality: how the viewer's knowledge and assumptions influence reading.

Assess precision of evidence, explanation of relationships, contextual accuracy and justified interpretation. A long artist biography with little engagement with the artwork is not strong analysis.

### Colour

#### Design analysis framework

Use this progression:

1. **Identify** — What use of colour is present?
2. **Describe** — What observable characteristics can be named precisely?
3. **Explain** — What visual, perceptual or functional effect does the choice create?
4. **Analyse** — How does it interact with other elements, principles and design decisions?
5. **Connect to communication** — What might the treatment communicate, signal or help the audience understand?
6. **Connect to purpose** — How does it support the intended function or communication goal?
7. **Connect to user/audience/context** — Why may it be appropriate here?
8. **Evaluate** — How effectively does it meet the brief, criteria and constraints? What evidence supports the judgement?

##### Worked progression

**Weak identification:** “The designer used colour.”

**Description:** “The design uses a consistent, high-contrast colour treatment with clear variation between primary and secondary content.”

**Explanation:** “This variation establishes a more visible hierarchy, so the primary message is encountered before supporting information.”

**Analysis:** “The colour treatment works with scale, spacing and value contrast to separate the primary message from secondary content and guide scanning.”

**Evaluation:** “The decision is effective for the intended context because it supports rapid recognition while remaining reproducible at the required scale. However, the smallest application should still be tested because the lower-priority treatment may lose clarity when reduced.”

Claude should replace generic wording with the **actual observable characteristic** in the work being discussed.
