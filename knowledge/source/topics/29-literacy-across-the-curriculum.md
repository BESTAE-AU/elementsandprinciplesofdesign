## Literacy is disciplinary

Literacy includes reading, writing, speaking, listening, viewing and representing. Different subjects use particular texts, vocabulary and evidence conventions. Students may understand a practical process but need explicit support to communicate it in a design justification or critical analysis.

**Task → language demand → model → guided rehearsal → independent use → feedback.**

## Vocabulary

Tier 1 commonly refers to everyday words; Tier 2 to broadly useful academic words; Tier 3 to specialised terms. These categories depend on learner and context. Teach pronunciation, meaning, examples, non-examples and use in a relevant sentence.

Words such as form, value, pattern, property, analyse and evaluate have specialised meanings. Technical vocabulary should be applied through relationships, not memorised only as a list.

## Text purposes

| Text | Main organising logic |
|---|---|
| Procedure | Goal, preparation, ordered actions, checks |
| Explanation | How/why, causes and relationships |
| Report | Classification and relevant information |
| Argument | Claim, evidence, reasoning and response to alternatives |
| Discussion | Perspectives, evidence and considered judgement |
| Evaluation | Criterion, evidence, judgement and implication |
| Reflection | Experience, analysis, learning and future practice |

Model how the discipline uses evidence. A design justification links choice and properties to performance and end use. Art analysis connects observable visual evidence to interpretation and context.

## Sentence and paragraph reasoning

Use cause/effect language—because, therefore, contributes to, results in—carefully. Comparison requires shared grounds; “stronger” needs a compared object and condition. Evaluation requires evidence and a criterion rather than an adjective alone.

Sentence stems can expose reasoning: “The trial indicated…, so… was changed to… because…”. Fade stems as independence develops. Avoid forcing every response into a formula when it obscures meaning.

## Reading and multimodal texts

Preview purpose, vocabulary and structure. Model identifying claims, technical conditions and relevant data. Distinguish credible sources from persuasive presentation. Reading diagrams, patterns, charts, interfaces and storyboards is part of disciplinary literacy.

Visuals require interpretation; they are not automatically accessible or easier. Provide descriptions and explicit links between image and text.

## Language diversity

Separate English-language demands from subject understanding for EAL/D learners. Support oral rehearsal, bilingual glossaries, visuals, explicit vocabulary and alternative response modes where appropriate. Translanguaging can use a learner's language resources to support meaning; decisions should reflect context and student preference.

Aboriginal English and other varieties are legitimate language practices, not deficient versions of English. Teach the register required for an assessment explicitly while respecting identity and home language. Avoid assuming language background from appearance or surname.

## Teaching and assessment

Support with worked examples, word banks and partly completed comparison tables. Extend with synthesis, counterarguments and audience adaptation. Diagnose whether difficulty concerns knowledge, comprehension, language or task interpretation.

Assess language only to the extent justified by the construct. Consult [ACARA general capabilities](https://www.australiancurriculum.edu.au/f-10-curriculum/general-capabilities) alongside the applicable NSW syllabus; capability guidance is not a substitute for course outcomes.


## Literacy in practical subjects

Students read patterns, technical drawings, care labels, software instructions, machine manuals and assessment briefs. These texts may combine symbols, conditional steps, diagrams and specialist vocabulary. Teach how to navigate the actual text rather than assume general reading ability transfers automatically.

### Procedure versus explanation

A procedure states what to do in order. An explanation establishes why it works. A student can copy steps without understanding the mechanism. Ask for a relevant rationale at selected checkpoints, while keeping operational instructions short enough to use safely.

### Design justification

“I chose…” identifies a decision. “Because…” must introduce a relevant reason. The explanation then needs the effect, purpose and comparison where required. Model how vague words such as strong, clear, suitable or professional become precise through evidence and context.

### Multimodal argument

An annotated prototype can combine labels, measured dimensions, photographs, arrows and prose. Each part should have an identifiable contribution. The image may show a trial, but a caption must explain its result and implication if that is the learning evidence.

## Assessing the language demand

Identify specialist vocabulary, sentence complexity, source reading, response genre and expected audience. Support those demands explicitly. Do not remove the disciplinary concept simply because a learner needs accessible language; instead provide an appropriate route into it.
