# Numeracy Across the Curriculum

## Purpose and provenance

A reference knowledge base for Claude, covering concepts, relationships, application and teaching. This is not an installable skill.

Numeracy Education plugin instructions, the user’s TAS applications and general mathematical knowledge. Numerical examples are illustrative and mathematically checked.

## Numeracy beyond calculation

Numeracy includes interpreting, representing, estimating, measuring, spatial reasoning, problem-solving, communicating, using data and applying mathematics in context. Students do not automatically transfer mathematics to TAS tasks; make the relationship explicit.

**Context → relevant information → representation → strategy → calculation → reasonableness → explanation.**

## TAS applications

| Area | Application | Common check |
|---|---|---|
| Measurement | Dimensions, fabric lengths, tolerances | Units, precision and tool resolution |
| Ratio | Aspect ratio, mixtures, scaled layouts | Relationship rather than total amount |
| Scale | Drawings and models | Consistent linear scale and units |
| Area | Fabric, sheet stock, surface coverage | Layout/waste beyond nominal area |
| Volume | Capacity and material estimates | Cubic units and real geometry |
| Percentage | Waste, discounts, progress | Correct base/denominator |
| Costing | Quantities, unit price, contingency | Separate rates, totals and assumptions |
| Data | Testing results and user responses | Sample, variation, missing data |

Ratios describe relationships. A 16:9 image can have many resolutions. Uniformly doubling linear dimensions multiplies area by four and volume by eight; this can matter when estimating material and mass, but actual manufacturing geometry may differ.

## Worked example

A panel is 240 mm wide in a 1:2 model. The corresponding full-size width is 480 mm. Explain the scale relationship before calculating. If the project needs an area estimate, calculate from full-size dimensions rather than doubling model area.

For material costing, **quantity × unit price = material cost**, then add relevant waste, delivery or other identified costs. State assumptions; contingency is a planning allowance, not a measured expense. Do not give personalised financial product advice from a classroom budget exercise.

## Data literacy and ethical reasoning

Ask who collected data, which users are represented, what is missing, how measurements were made and whether the conclusion follows. Mean and median answer different questions; a percentage without sample size can mislead. Charts need accurate scales and labels. Correlation alone does not establish causation.

Teach strategy comparison and “How do you know?” questioning. Error analysis can reveal understanding without framing mistakes as inability.

## Inclusive numeracy

Use concrete, representational and abstract forms according to the concept. Provide worked examples, diagrams, manipulatives, unit prompts, bilingual vocabulary, oral reasoning and accessible tools. Separate mathematical understanding from English-language difficulty.

Reduce avoidable pressure and provide low-stakes practice for anxious learners. Do not diagnose dyscalculia or mental health conditions; observe barriers, provide support and collaborate through established school processes. Extend gifted learners through generalisation, proof, open problems and multiple strategies rather than more routine items.

## Assessment

Assess interpretation, strategy, representation, accuracy, checking, reasoning and application. A correct answer without units or justification may be incomplete evidence; an arithmetic error can coexist with sound strategy.

The [ACARA capability framework](https://www.australiancurriculum.edu.au/help/general-capabilities) locates numeracy across learning areas. Use actual curriculum requirements for formal mapping.


## Ratio, costing and test-data examples

### Aspect ratio

A 1920 × 1080 frame reduces to 16:9 because both dimensions can be divided by 120. A 1080 × 1920 frame reduces to 9:16. Changing orientation changes composition; it is not simply a resolution improvement. Ask students to identify what would be cropped or repositioned.

### Material allowance

If an estimated requirement is 2.4 m and the task specifies a 10% allowance, the allowance is 0.24 m and the planned total is 2.64 m. This arithmetic does not establish that 10% is appropriate: pattern layout, width, nap, defects and cutting method may require another estimate.

### Cost comparison

Compare materials using the same quantity and unit. A lower price per metre may not mean a cheaper project when usable width, waste, delivery and durability differ. State the assumptions before recommending a choice.

### Test results

Record units, sample conditions and repeats. A single unusually high result may be a measurement error or genuine variation. Calculate a relevant summary but retain the original observations. Ask what the evidence supports and what it cannot establish about users or real-world performance.

## Mathematical dialogue

How did you represent the problem? Which information was unnecessary? Why does that operation fit? Is the unit correct? Can another method confirm the result? Would doubling the dimension double the area? These questions develop reasoning and error detection rather than speed alone.
