# Texture — element of design

This skill gives Claude the texture-specific knowledge for teaching, analysing and designing with texture. The shared method — analysis progression, justification levels, Factors Affecting Design, experimentation, differentiation, feedback approach and language rules — is in `references/design-method.md`. Read the relevant section of that file when a task calls for it.

The core habit: always say whether texture is **actual** (can be felt) or **visual** (only seen). Move from **what the surface is like** → **effect** (touch, grip, light, character, noise) → **why that matters here** → **how well it works and what it costs**.

## What texture is

Texture is the tactile or visual character of a surface. **Actual texture** can be physically felt; **visual (implied) texture** represents surface qualities through image, mark, pattern, light, value or digital simulation.

Keep it distinct from its neighbours:
- **Texture vs material:** material is what something is made of; texture is a surface quality arising from the material, the process or a simulation.
- **Texture vs pattern:** pattern is repeated organisation; texture may be patterned but can also be irregular.
- **Texture vs finish:** gloss, reflection and translucency are optical surface properties that change how texture is perceived.

## Variables

| Variable | Range | Typical effect | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tactility** | smooth, rough, coarse, fine, soft, hard | touch, comfort, grip | Relate to the real material and user |
| **Regularity** | uniform ↔ irregular, ordered ↔ random | consistency vs organic character | Affects cleaning and repeatability |
| **Scale** | micro ↔ macro | legibility and tactile effect | Texture vanishes or dominates when scaled |
| **Density** | sparse ↔ dense | surface activity, perceived mass | High density can become visual noise |
| **Relief / depth** | flat, raised, embossed, recessed | shadow, grip, feedback | Adds tooling, material and cost |
| **Finish** | matte, satin, gloss, reflective | light, fingerprints, perceived quality | Gloss can cause glare |
| **Transparency** | opaque, translucent, transparent | layering, depth | Not all materials reproduce consistently |
| **Digital grain / noise** | fine ↔ coarse | visual surface, no tactile change | Don't use noise instead of real hierarchy |

## Types

- **Actual / tactile texture** — physical surface that can be felt; textiles, products, interiors, timber.
- **Visual / implied texture** — the look of texture without tactile change; graphics, photography, illustration.
- **Natural texture** — associated with natural materials: wood grain, stone, fibres, leaves.
- **Manufactured texture** — engineered surfaces: knurling, embossing, sandblasting, coatings.
- **Digital texture** — raster, vector or 3D surface simulation: overlays, brushes, texture maps.
- **Material texture** — inherent to the material or process: weave, grain, layer lines.

## How texture works with the other elements

- **Line:** dense repeated lines become texture (hatching, pinstripe, corduroy); edge roughness reads as texture.
- **Direction:** directional textures (grain, brushed metal, nap) add a quiet vector across a surface.
- **Shape:** a texture fill can define a shape; a textured edge changes its character.
- **Form:** light reveals texture on form; gloss shows curvature while matte hides it.
- **Space:** detailed foreground texture against smoother background texture builds depth.
- **Size and scale:** texture scale must suit viewing distance — fine texture disappears far away or when reduced.
- **Colour:** texture changes perceived colour (a rough surface looks darker and less saturated than a smooth one of the same pigment).
- **Value:** texture is revealed by value — highlights and shadows across the surface; flat lighting flattens texture.
- **Typography:** type on heavy texture loses legibility; textured or distressed type trades clarity for character.
- **Layout and composition:** contrast between textured and plain areas creates hierarchy and focus.
- **Time and duration:** animated grain, moving fabric, texture compressed or smeared in fast motion.

## How texture contributes to the principles

- **Contrast:** rough against smooth, matte against gloss (e.g. a spot-gloss logo on matte packaging).
- **Emphasis:** the one textured area in a plain design draws the eye (or hand).
- **Unity and harmony:** a consistent material palette or grain overlay ties a series together.
- **Variety:** controlled mixing of textures adds interest without chaos.
- **Rhythm, repetition and pattern:** repeated textures (weaves, knurling, tiles) create rhythm.
- **Balance:** heavily textured areas carry visual weight.
- **Hierarchy:** plain areas often carry key information, textured areas support mood.

## Texture across fields

- **Branding:** texture can be a brand asset through paper stock, grain, pattern overlays, emboss/deboss, foil, varnish, tactile packaging, photo treatment and digital texture. Define when texture is core, supporting or not allowed, so it stays coherent rather than decorative noise.
- **Graphic and communication design:** paper choice, print finishes, texture overlays and illustrated texture. Check legibility of anything placed on texture.
- **Photography, film and animation:** lighting angle reveals texture; 3D uses texture, normal, bump and displacement maps, shaders and materials. Digital grain can support a look but fights compression and legibility.
- **UI / UX:** visual texture shouldn't reduce readability or suggest false affordances. Physical interfaces use texture for grip, orientation and control differentiation (e.g. tactile dots). Consider sensory sensitivity.
- **Textiles and fashion:** texture is fundamental — fibre, yarn, weave, knit, felt, pile, quilting, embroidery, appliqué, pleating and finishing. Distinguish inherent structural texture from applied decorative texture; evaluate comfort, durability and care.
- **Product and industrial design:** texture affects grip, slip, cleaning, scratch visibility, wear, perceived quality and tactile feedback. A finish can be functional and aesthetic at once.
- **Interiors and architecture:** material palettes, acoustic panels, flooring and wall finishes shape sensory experience. Consider glare, slip resistance, maintenance and sensory load.
- **Technical communication:** hatching and material symbols represent materials in drawings — they are conventions, not actual texture.

## Production and reproduction

| Process | Texture risks |
|---|---|
| Emboss / deboss | Minimum depth, stock thickness and tooling limit results |
| Foil / varnish | Registration and substrate limit fine detail |
| 3D printing | Layer height creates actual texture; orientation changes surface quality |
| Textile processes | Stitch density, pile, yarn and finishing determine texture |
| Digital imagery | Compression and scaling destroy fine texture or create artefacts |
| Timber / metal finishing | Sanding grit, sealer and coating change feel and look |

## Common misconceptions

| Misconception | Better understanding |
|---|---|
| "Texture is only something you can touch." | Visual texture exists without tactile change. Say which. |
| "Rough textures always feel rustic." | Meaning depends on material, context and finishing. |
| "More texture = more interest." | Texture can overload and reduce readability. |
| "Texture and pattern are the same." | Pattern is repeated organisation; texture can be irregular. |
| "A grain overlay makes it look professional." | It can support a style, but it can't fix weak hierarchy. |

## Worked analysis (model for exemplars and annotations)

Scenario: a chocolate bar wrapper printed on uncoated kraft paper, with the brand name as a raised emboss and a gloss spot varnish only on the cocoa pod illustration.

- **Identify:** "The wrapper uses texture."
- **Describe:** "Uncoated kraft stock with visible fibres; the brand name embossed about 0.5 mm; gloss varnish only on the cocoa pod, surrounded by matte kraft."
- **Explain:** "The kraft fibres give an actual, slightly rough feel; the emboss makes the name catch light and be felt; the gloss pod reflects light and stands out from the matte surface."
- **Analyse:** "Texture works with contrast and emphasis: matte against gloss draws the eye to the pod, and the raised name is both seen and touched, so the brand is reinforced through two senses. In this context the kraft stock may suggest craft or natural sourcing."
- **Evaluate:** "Effective for a premium supermarket product where people pick it up and handle it. However, emboss plus spot varnish adds two extra production steps and tooling cost, and kraft stock dulls the printed colours. For a lower price point, a printed visual texture could approximate the feel at lower cost but lose the tactile cue."

Each level should add a link in the chain, not simply more words.

## Justification examples (Year 10 Textiles cushion)

- **Weak:** "I used velvet because it's soft and nice."
- **Developing:** "I used velvet so the cushion feels soft and cosy."
- **Strong:** "I used a cotton velvet front and a plain canvas back. The velvet's pile gives a soft touch for the side people lean on, while the canvas is harder-wearing on the side that sits against the couch. That suits a family lounge cushion that will be used every day."
- **Sophisticated:** "I compared cotton velvet, polyester velvet and corduroy after 20 rubs with a scouring pad and one wash. The polyester pilled; the corduroy kept its texture but felt stiffer. Cotton velvet crushed slightly but recovered with steaming, so I chose it and added a care label. It costs more than polyester, but it lasts longer in daily use."

## Feedback patterns to look for in student work

- **Actual and visual texture not distinguished** in written analysis.
- **Texture as decoration only** — no link to touch, grip, durability or care.
- **Text placed on busy texture** with poor legibility.
- **Overlays used to hide weak composition.**
- **Finish chosen without testing** real material (screen mock-up only).
- **Care and durability ignored** in textiles and products.

## Activity ideas for teaching resources

- **Texture rubbings:** collect ten rubbings; classify each as natural or manufactured and describe it precisely.
- **Feel and describe:** a blind touch test with sample materials; build a shared vocabulary.
- **Actual vs visual:** photograph an actual texture, then draw it; discuss what changes.
- **Finish sample board (D&T/Timber):** sand to three grits and apply two finishes; compare feel and look.
- **Swatch testing (Textiles):** rub-test, wash-test and photograph three fabrics.
- **Overlay experiment (Multimedia):** apply three grain strengths to one poster; test legibility at thumbnail size.
