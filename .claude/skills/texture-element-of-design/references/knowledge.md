# Texture: knowledge

The definition and variables are in `../SKILL.md`.

## Types

- **Actual / tactile texture** — physical surface that can be felt; textiles, products, interiors, timber.
- **Visual / implied texture** — the look of texture without tactile change; graphics, photography, illustration.
- **Natural texture** — associated with natural materials: wood grain, stone, fibres, leaves.
- **Manufactured texture** — engineered surfaces: knurling, embossing, sandblasting, coatings.
- **Digital texture** — raster, vector or 3D surface simulation: overlays, brushes, texture maps.
- **Material texture** — inherent to the material or process: weave, grain, layer lines.

## How texture works with the other elements

- **Line:** dense repeated lines become texture (hatching, pinstripe, corduroy); edge roughness reads as texture.
- **Direction:** directional textures (grain, brushed metal, nap) add a quiet vector across a surface.
- **Shape:** a texture fill can define a shape; a textured edge changes its character.
- **Form:** light reveals texture on form; gloss shows curvature while matte hides it.
- **Space:** detailed foreground texture against smoother background texture builds depth.
- **Size and scale:** texture scale must suit viewing distance — fine texture disappears far away or when reduced.
- **Colour:** texture changes perceived colour (a rough surface looks darker and less saturated than a smooth one of the same pigment).
- **Value:** texture is revealed by value — highlights and shadows across the surface; flat lighting flattens texture.
- **Typography:** type on heavy texture loses legibility; textured or distressed type trades clarity for character.
- **Layout and composition:** contrast between textured and plain areas creates hierarchy and focus.
- **Time and duration:** animated grain, moving fabric, texture compressed or smeared in fast motion.

## How texture contributes to the principles

- **Contrast:** rough against smooth, matte against gloss (e.g. a spot-gloss logo on matte packaging).
- **Emphasis:** the one textured area in a plain design draws the eye (or hand).
- **Unity and harmony:** a consistent material palette or grain overlay ties a series together.
- **Variety:** controlled mixing of textures adds interest without chaos.
- **Rhythm, repetition and pattern:** repeated textures (weaves, knurling, tiles) create rhythm.
- **Balance:** heavily textured areas carry visual weight.
- **Hierarchy:** plain areas often carry key information, textured areas support mood.

## Texture across fields

- **Branding:** texture can be a brand asset through paper stock, grain, pattern overlays, emboss/deboss, foil, varnish, tactile packaging, photo treatment and digital texture. Define when texture is core, supporting or not allowed, so it stays coherent rather than decorative noise.
- **Graphic and communication design:** paper choice, print finishes, texture overlays and illustrated texture. Check legibility of anything placed on texture.
- **Photography, film and animation:** lighting angle reveals texture; 3D uses texture, normal, bump and displacement maps, shaders and materials. Digital grain can support a look but fights compression and legibility.
- **UI / UX:** visual texture shouldn't reduce readability or suggest false affordances. Physical interfaces use texture for grip, orientation and control differentiation (e.g. tactile dots). Consider sensory sensitivity.
- **Textiles and fashion:** texture is fundamental — fibre, yarn, weave, knit, felt, pile, quilting, embroidery, appliqué, pleating and finishing. Distinguish inherent structural texture from applied decorative texture; evaluate comfort, durability and care.
- **Product and industrial design:** texture affects grip, slip, cleaning, scratch visibility, wear, perceived quality and tactile feedback. A finish can be functional and aesthetic at once.
- **Interiors and architecture:** material palettes, acoustic panels, flooring and wall finishes shape sensory experience. Consider glare, slip resistance, maintenance and sensory load.
- **Technical communication:** hatching and material symbols represent materials in drawings — they are conventions, not actual texture.

## Production and reproduction

| Process | Texture risks |
|---|---|
| Emboss / deboss | Minimum depth, stock thickness and tooling limit results |
| Foil / varnish | Registration and substrate limit fine detail |
| 3D printing | Layer height creates actual texture; orientation changes surface quality |
| Textile processes | Stitch density, pile, yarn and finishing determine texture |
| Digital imagery | Compression and scaling destroy fine texture or create artefacts |
| Timber / metal finishing | Sanding grit, sealer and coating change feel and look |

## Common misconceptions

| Misconception | Better understanding |
|---|---|
| "Texture is only something you can touch." | Visual texture exists without tactile change. Say which. |
| "Rough textures always feel rustic." | Meaning depends on material, context and finishing. |
| "More texture = more interest." | Texture can overload and reduce readability. |
| "Texture and pattern are the same." | Pattern is repeated organisation; texture can be irregular. |
| "A grain overlay makes it look professional." | It can support a style, but it can't fix weak hierarchy. |
