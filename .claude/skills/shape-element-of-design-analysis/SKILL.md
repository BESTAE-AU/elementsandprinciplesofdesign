# Shape: analysis and model answers

This skill handles **analysis and model answers** for shape as an element of design. Everything known about shape (definition, variables, types, relationships with the other elements and the principles, fields, production, misconceptions and worked examples) is in `references/element-knowledge.md`. The shared method is in `references/design-method.md`. Read the sections the workflow points to.

Related skills: `shape-element-of-design-teaching`, `shape-element-of-design-feedback`, `shape-element-of-design-practice`; `shape-element-of-design` for general questions about shape; the `*-principle-of-design` skills for principles.

Apply the language rules in `references/design-method.md` §14: Australian English, describe before interpreting, conditional language for emotional or cultural associations, and name the criterion whenever something "works".

## Workflow

1. **Fix the artefact.** If the user gives an image or description, work from it. If you're inventing one (for an exemplar), describe it briefly first so students can picture it, with the specific shape characteristics the answers will cite.
2. **Work through the progression** (method §2): identify → describe → explain → analyse (connect to communication, purpose, audience) → evaluate against a named criterion, including a limitation or trade-off.
3. **For levelled exemplars** (e.g. developing / sound / high-range), make each level add a link in the reasoning chain, not just more words. Respect any word limits. Under each, add a short note on what lifts it to the next level.
4. **Analyse relationships.** Use the relationships with other elements and principles below to go beyond the element on its own.
5. **Language:** conditional wording for emotional or cultural readings, observation before interpretation, and no "it works" without a criterion.

## Worked analysis (model for exemplars and annotations)

Scenario: a logo for a native plant nursery. A leaf shape is formed entirely from the negative space between two rounded hands facing each other. The mark is one flat colour.

- **Identify:** "The logo uses shape."
- **Describe:** "Two rounded, organic hand shapes face each other; the gap between them forms a pointed leaf. There is no outline — the shapes are defined by one flat colour against the background."
- **Explain:** "Because the leaf is formed from negative space, the viewer sees hands first and then 'finds' the leaf, which links care (hands) with plants (leaf) in one mark."
- **Analyse:** "Shape works with value contrast — the flat mark against a light ground makes the figure-ground reading clear — and with simplicity: two main shapes keep it recognisable. Rounded edges on the hands contrast with the leaf's pointed tip, which draws the eye to the leaf."
- **Evaluate:** "Effective as a memorable, meaningful mark at signage and website sizes. However, the leaf's narrow tip is a thin negative gap that may close up when embroidered on staff polo shirts or printed on small plant tags; a small-size version with a wider gap should be tested."

Each level should add a link in the chain, not simply more words.

## Justification examples (Year 11 app icon set)

- **Weak:** "I used rounded shapes because they look nice."
- **Developing:** "I used rounded squares for all icons so they look friendly and match."
- **Strong:** "I set every icon inside a rounded square with the same 8 px corner radius, so the set reads as one family and matches the rounded buttons in the app. That suits a study app for younger teens who need to recognise icons quickly."
- **Sophisticated:** "I tested sharp-cornered, 8 px and fully circular containers at 24 px. Circles cropped the detail in the calendar and calculator icons; sharp corners clashed with the app's buttons. The 8 px radius kept detail and matched the UI, so I set it as the rule. I gave up the extra distinctiveness of circles for clearer icons."

## How shape works with the other elements

- **Line:** a closed line creates an outline shape; line weight changes perceived shape size. Shapes can also be implied by a series of lines stopping at a common edge.
- **Direction:** pointed, wedge and chevron shapes point; asymmetric shapes appear to face a way. Arrangement of shapes along a path creates direction.
- **Form:** shape is the 2D reading of form. Add value, overlap and perspective and a shape starts to read as volume; a garment silhouette is a shape reading of a 3D form.
- **Space:** figure-ground — the negative space between and inside shapes is itself a shape. Well-known logos hide a second shape in the negative space.
- **Size and scale:** detail inside a shape disappears as it shrinks; small versions of a mark often need simplified shapes and opened-up gaps.
- **Colour:** colour can define a shape without an outline; two shapes of equal area can feel unequal if one is more saturated.
- **Value:** value contrast is usually what separates a shape from its ground; low contrast weakens recognition.
- **Texture:** texture fill can define a shape (hatching, pattern) and edge texture changes its character.
- **Typography:** letterforms are shapes; counters (the enclosed spaces in a, e, o) are negative shapes that close up in embroidery or small print.
- **Layout and composition:** containers, cards and panels are shapes that group content; the overall arrangement of shapes sets balance and hierarchy.
- **Time and duration:** in motion, shapes morph, mask and reveal; recognition must survive the transition.

## How shape contributes to the principles

- **Unity and harmony:** a consistent shape language (same corner radius, same geometry) makes an icon set or brand feel like one family.
- **Contrast and variety:** a single organic shape among geometric ones, or one sharp shape among rounded ones, stands out.
- **Emphasis:** an unusual or larger shape becomes the focal point.
- **Balance:** a few large shapes can balance many small ones; asymmetric balance depends on shape weight and position.
- **Repetition, pattern and rhythm:** repeated motifs build pattern; varied spacing or size builds rhythm.
- **Proportion:** internal proportions of a shape, and the ratio between shapes.
- **Proximity and alignment:** shapes near each other group; shared edges align.
- **Hierarchy:** container shapes (cards, panels, badges) signal levels of information.
