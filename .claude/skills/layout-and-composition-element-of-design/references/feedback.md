# Layout and composition: feedback and marking

Use this file when a teacher shares student work or a justification and wants feedback, a mark or "what gets them to the top band" on layout and composition.

## Workflow

1. **Read the work against its brief.** Note the audience, purpose and every touchpoint or output named (cups, embroidery, a phone screen, signage…). Reproduction problems usually hide there.
2. **Start from what the student actually did.** Quote their words or point to the part of the design, and name the layout and composition variable involved (Variables in `SKILL.md`).
3. **Check for the patterns below** and the misconceptions in `knowledge.md`.
4. **Check production and accessibility.** Use the production table in `knowledge.md` and `method/checks-and-language.md`.
5. **Place the reasoning on the justification ladder** (weak → developing → strong → sophisticated; `method/justification.md`). Say which link in the chain is missing.
6. **If marking:** use the rubric supplied. If none is supplied, use `marking-rubric-builder` (if available) to generate NESA-aligned criteria first, mark against them and say they were generated. Without that skill, give feedback without a grade (`method/feedback.md`).
7. **Write it up:**
   - **What's working:** specific, not generic.
   - **What's holding it back:** 2–4 points, each quoting the student.
   - **Next steps:** one or two, phrased as tests or design decisions, in priority order.
   - **Model structure** (optional): a fill-in-the-blanks version of a top-level justification, with a note not to copy the numbers.
   - **Teacher notes:** current level, gap to the top, and a conferencing question.

Keep the tone warm, honest and specific.


## Feedback patterns to look for in student work

- **No clear first read** — several elements competing at the same weight.
- **Near-miss alignment** — items almost but not quite aligned.
- **Everything centred** by default with no reason.
- **Even spacing everywhere,** so nothing groups.
- **Rule of thirds applied mechanically** without purpose.
- **Key content in unsafe areas** (trim, gutter, title-safe, folds).
- **Desktop layout squeezed** onto mobile instead of reflowed.

## Justification examples (Year 10 event poster)

- **Weak:** "I put things where they looked good."
- **Developing:** "I put the title at the top so people read it first."
- **Strong:** "I used a 3-column grid, placed the band photo across two columns and the title above it, and grouped the date, time and venue in the third column. That makes the band and title read first and keeps the practical information in one place for students checking details."
- **Sophisticated:** "I compared a centred symmetrical layout with an asymmetrical 3-column layout in a 5-second test with six students. On the centred version, three couldn't find the venue; on the asymmetrical version, all six could. I kept the asymmetrical layout, though the photo had to be smaller to fit the grid — I traded some visual impact for faster information."
