# Layout and composition — element of design

This skill gives Claude the knowledge for teaching, analysing and designing layout and composition — how all the other elements are organised into relationships, hierarchy and a reading or viewing path. The shared method — analysis progression, justification levels, Factors Affecting Design, experimentation, differentiation, feedback approach and language rules — is in `references/design-method.md`.

For exact InDesign margins, grids and object styles use `indesign-parameters`; for print workbooklet layout use `workbooklet-style-system` or `greyscale-print-design-system` — when available.

The core habit: analyse the **system**, not just where things are. Move from **placement, alignment, grouping, weight and order** → **effect** (hierarchy, balance, flow) → **why that matters here** → **how well it works and what it costs**.

## What layout and composition are

Layout and composition are the deliberate organisation, positioning and relationship of visual elements within a space or sequence. **Layout** often means the structured arrangement of content for communication (pages, screens); **composition** more broadly means the visual relationships that create hierarchy, balance, flow and unity (images, frames, artworks).

Keep the terms distinct:
- **Grid vs visible lines:** a grid is an underlying system; it rarely shows in the final design.
- **Balance vs symmetry:** asymmetrical designs can be balanced through unequal visual weights.
- **Hierarchy vs reading order:** hierarchy is perceived importance; reading order is the path actually taken. They should agree but don't always.
- **Element vs principles:** layout uses the principles (balance, alignment, proximity…) rather than replacing them.

## Variables

| Variable | Range | Typical effect | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Placement** | position in the frame, page or screen | emphasis, relationships | Edges, crops and responsive reflow |
| **Alignment** | shared edges, axes, baselines | order, connection | Near-misses look accidental |
| **Proximity / grouping** | near ↔ far | structure | Back up critical groups with other cues |
| **Grid** | columns, rows, modules, baseline | consistency and flexibility | The grid should serve content, not dominate |
| **Margins / gutters** | edge and internal space | density, separation | Adapt for binding and device |
| **Visual weight** | relative prominence | balance, focal point | Weight comes from size, value, colour, texture, image and position |
| **Hierarchy** | primary, secondary, tertiary | organises attention | Must match content priorities |
| **Flow / reading order** | path through content | comprehension | Language and platform conventions |
| **Framing / cropping** | what's included or excluded | focus, relationships | Avoid accidental amputations and lost meaning |
| **Layering / overlap** | foreground–background | depth, grouping | Can reduce legibility |
| **Density / white space** | amount and distribution | cognitive load, pacing | Neither dense nor sparse is inherently better |
| **Repetition / rhythm** | recurring structures | consistency, progression | Variation should signal something |

## Types (compositional structures)

- **Symmetrical** — balanced around an axis; formal or stable arrangements in some contexts.
- **Asymmetrical** — balance through unequal weights; dynamic, flexible layouts.
- **Central** — main emphasis near the centre; portraits, symbols, single messages.
- **Diagonal / triangular** — organised along diagonals or a triangle; photography, posters.
- **Radial** — arranged round a centre; infographics, identity devices.
- **Grid-based / modular** — columns and modules; editorial, UI, catalogues.
- **Rule of thirds** — a heuristic dividing the frame into thirds; optional, not universal.
- **Z / F scanning patterns** — observed tendencies in some left-to-right reading contexts; not universal.

## How layout and composition work with the other elements

Layout organises every other element, so each relationship is about **arrangement**:
- **Line:** grids and alignments are structural (usually implied) lines; visible rules and leading lines shape the reading path.
- **Direction:** gaze, perspective and sequence set the path; layout decides where that path leads.
- **Shape:** containers, cards and panels group content; the overall silhouette of the arrangement matters.
- **Form:** in 3D and product layout, arrangement of forms and controls in space.
- **Space:** space is the raw material of layout — margins, gutters and grouping gaps.
- **Size and scale:** relative scale sets the hierarchy; one dominant element anchors the composition.
- **Colour:** colour distribution and proportion balance the page and link related items.
- **Value:** the big pattern of light and dark masses structures the composition before detail is seen (squint test).
- **Texture:** textured and plain areas create contrast and focus.
- **Typography:** type blocks and hierarchy are the main content of most layouts.
- **Time and duration:** in motion and interactive work, composition changes over time — what enters, where, and in what order.

## How layout and composition contribute to the principles

Layout is where the principles become visible:
- **Balance:** symmetrical or asymmetrical distribution of visual weight.
- **Hierarchy and emphasis:** placement, scale and isolation create a clear first, second and third read.
- **Alignment:** a strong grid creates order and connection across the page.
- **Proximity:** related items grouped, unrelated items separated.
- **Unity:** a consistent grid and spacing system across pages, screens or a campaign.
- **Contrast:** dense against open, large against small, image against text.
- **Rhythm and repetition:** recurring structures across spreads or slides, with variation to signal change.
- **Movement:** the planned path of the eye through the composition.

## Layout and composition across fields

- **Branding:** brand systems need compositional rules — logo placement, margins, grid logic, image crops, graphic-device positions, text-to-image ratios and campaign templates. Consistent composition can be as recognisable as colour or type.
- **Graphic and communication design:** posters, publications, packaging, infographics and slides. Consider hierarchy, grouping, legibility, reading order, emphasis, consistency, scannability and efficiency.
- **Photography, film and animation:** framing, headroom, lead room, depth, blocking, focal points and multiple-subject balance. Motion graphics compose over time and across aspect ratios.
- **UI / UX:** responsive layouts must reflow, reorder and resize while keeping reading order, accessibility and component relationships. A desktop grid shouldn't just be squeezed onto mobile.
- **Textiles and fashion:** placement prints, motifs, embroidery, trims and panels compose the garment surface; placement must respect seams, darts, body movement and pattern matching.
- **Product and industrial design:** control placement, information panels, labels and graphics form functional layouts; reach, sequence and visibility often outweigh symmetry.
- **Interiors and architecture:** furniture, circulation, focal zones, lighting and architectural features compose space; evaluate use patterns, access and wayfinding.
- **Technical communication:** drawing sheets and diagrams need disciplined hierarchy, alignment, annotation placement and reading order; expressive composition must not compromise accuracy.

| Process | Layout risks |
|---|---|
| Print | Bleed, trim, binding, folds and imposition affect the final layout |
| Responsive digital | Breakpoints, reflow and dynamic content change composition |
| Video | Title-safe and action-safe areas and platform crops |
| Packaging | Dielines, folds, seams and panels fragment the visible layout |
| Textiles | Seams and cutting layouts break placement continuity |

## Common misconceptions

| Misconception | Better understanding |
|---|---|
| "Balanced means symmetrical." | Asymmetrical layouts balance through unequal visual weights. |
| "The rule of thirds is a rule." | It's a heuristic that helps sometimes; use it when it serves the design. |
| "A grid must be visible." | Grids usually structure content invisibly. |
| "Layout is just where things go." | It's hierarchy, spacing, grouping, flow and relationships — a system. |
| "Fill the space so it looks finished." | Crowding flattens hierarchy; space is part of the composition. |

## Worked analysis (model for exemplars and annotations)

Scenario: a double-page magazine spread on a local skate park. A full-bleed photo of a skater mid-air fills the left page and crosses the gutter by 40 mm; the right page has a large headline top-left, a short standfirst, and body text in two columns on a 6-column grid, with a pull quote spanning two columns.

- **Identify:** "The spread uses layout and composition."
- **Describe:** "Full-bleed photo on the left page crossing 40 mm over the gutter; on the right, a headline top-left, a standfirst, two columns of body on a 6-column grid, and a pull quote spanning two columns."
- **Explain:** "The large photo is the first read and gives energy; the headline, aligned to the grid, is the second read; the columns and pull quote organise the text into manageable blocks."
- **Analyse:** "Composition works with scale, alignment and direction: the skater faces right, into the page, so their implied direction leads the eye to the headline. The photo crossing the gutter joins the two pages into one composition, and the consistent grid keeps the text side orderly against the photo's energy — a deliberate asymmetrical balance."
- **Evaluate:** "Effective for a lifestyle magazine where readers are drawn in by the image and then read. However, the 40 mm crossover risks losing part of the skater's hand in the binding; the image should be checked against the printer's gutter allowance, or shifted so no key detail falls in the fold."

Each level should add a link in the chain, not simply more words.

## Justification examples (Year 10 event poster)

- **Weak:** "I put things where they looked good."
- **Developing:** "I put the title at the top so people read it first."
- **Strong:** "I used a 3-column grid, placed the band photo across two columns and the title above it, and grouped the date, time and venue in the third column. That makes the band and title read first and keeps the practical information in one place for students checking details."
- **Sophisticated:** "I compared a centred symmetrical layout with an asymmetrical 3-column layout in a 5-second test with six students. On the centred version, three couldn't find the venue; on the asymmetrical version, all six could. I kept the asymmetrical layout, though the photo had to be smaller to fit the grid — I traded some visual impact for faster information."

## Feedback patterns to look for in student work

- **No clear first read** — several elements competing at the same weight.
- **Near-miss alignment** — items almost but not quite aligned.
- **Everything centred** by default with no reason.
- **Even spacing everywhere,** so nothing groups.
- **Rule of thirds applied mechanically** without purpose.
- **Key content in unsafe areas** (trim, gutter, title-safe, folds).
- **Desktop layout squeezed** onto mobile instead of reflowed.

## Activity ideas for teaching resources

- **Squint test:** blur three layouts and identify the first, second and third reads.
- **Rearrange the same content:** give everyone the same elements; compare symmetrical, asymmetrical and grid layouts.
- **Grid overlay:** draw the hidden grid over a magazine page or website.
- **Crop experiment:** crop one photo three ways; discuss how the focus and meaning change.
- **Dieline layout (Graphics):** lay out packaging artwork on a dieline and check what's visible when folded.
- **Responsive reflow (Multimedia/UI):** redesign a desktop page for mobile, keeping the reading order.
