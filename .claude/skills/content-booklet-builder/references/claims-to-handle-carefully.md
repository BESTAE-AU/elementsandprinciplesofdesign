# Claims to handle carefully

Education is full of confident folk claims that outrun the evidence in both directions — some too pessimistic about students and screens, some too sweeping about what "just works." Consult this table whenever a workbooklet's planning summary, teacher notes, or rationale touches one of these topics, so language stays calibrated rather than sliding into a slogan.

Two entries below (UDL meta-analysis, mobile-phone-ban review) are referenced by finding only — full author details weren't available when this was written. Don't present them as complete citations; verify before Britt quotes them formally anywhere.

| Claim | Evidence judgement | Better language |
|---|---|---|
| "Students can't concentrate anymore." | Exaggerated/unsupported as a generational claim. | Students may be learning in environments with frequent opportunities for distraction and task-switching. Attention also varies with task design, knowledge, motivation, sleep, wellbeing and context. |
| "Screens destroy attention." | Unsupported as a universal causal claim. | Notifications, multitasking, scrolling, poorly designed interfaces and off-task applications can interfere with focused learning. The problem is not the existence of a screen in itself. |
| "Paper is always better." | False. | Effects depend on learner, text and task. Print can advantage some sustained informational reading; digital environments may advantage accessibility, vocabulary and multimedia. A 2024 review of children/adolescents found "no difference" was the most common result, followed by a print advantage in comprehension (Hare et al., 2024). A 2026 network meta-analysis narrows this further: the digital disadvantage shows up mainly when the reader has to scroll — when the full text is navigable without scrolling, reliable medium differences largely disappear (Clinton-Lisell & Litzinger, 2026). Useful in practice: if a digital text can be paginated or fit to one screen, the print/digital choice can turn on other factors (annotation, accessibility, cost) rather than assuming print for comprehension's sake. |
| "Handwriting is always better than typing." | Too broad. | For lecture note-taking in higher education, a recent meta-analysis suggests a modest achievement advantage for handwritten-and-reviewed notes, while typing produces substantially more notes (Flanigan et al., 2024). That doesn't establish handwriting superiority for every learner or task. |
| "Technology increases engagement." | Sometimes, but engagement ≠ learning. | A technology may raise behavioural or situational engagement without improving knowledge or transfer. Evaluate learning, not just participation. |
| "Retrieval practice always works." | Strong general evidence, false as a universal. | Retrieval usually improves retention when students retrieve relevant material successfully enough, receive appropriate correction where needed, and revisit it across time (Agarwal et al., 2021). |
| "Explicit teaching is always preferable to inquiry." | False dichotomy. | Novices usually need substantial guidance — unguided discovery performs poorly, while well-guided inquiry can be highly effective, particularly for conceptual and scientific learning (Alfieri et al., 2011). |
| "Higher-order thinking should replace knowledge learning." | Misconceived. | Higher-order thinking works *with* knowledge. Students need sufficiently organised domain knowledge to analyse, evaluate, compare, argue and solve unfamiliar problems. |
| "Phones should simply be banned because they ruin learning." | Evidence on policy effect is still developing. | Phones can create real opportunities for distraction; restriction may help, but implementation, exceptions, school culture and digital learning goals all matter (a recent systematic review of school phone bans describes benefits and implementation complexity rather than a single universal effect — author details unverified, check before citing). |
| "AI will make students lazy/stupid." | Emerging, overly deterministic. | Generative AI can support learning or substitute for it depending on task and guardrails. In a large secondary-maths field experiment, unrestricted AI assistance improved *assisted* performance but reduced later *unaided* performance; a guided-tutor design largely fixed that (Bastani et al., 2025). |
| "AI improves learning." | Also too broad. | Experimental meta-analyses currently show positive average effects, but much of the evidence is higher-education, short-duration and heterogeneous (Deng et al., 2025). Evaluating unaided learning and long-term retention still matters. |
| "Students are digital natives and can multitask." | Unsupported. | Familiarity with devices doesn't imply information literacy or an ability to process competing cognitive tasks simultaneously (Kirschner & De Bruyckere, 2017). |
| "Teach to visual/auditory/kinaesthetic learning styles." | Unsupported. | Learner variability is real; matching teaching to supposed learning-style categories is not an evidence-based form of differentiation (Pashler et al., 2009). |
| "Practice makes perfect." | Too simplistic for practical-skill design. | Practice has to be accurate enough, purposeful and appropriately challenging — repeating an error doesn't fade it, it entrenches it. |
| "It takes 10,000 hours of practice to build expertise." | False as a universal rule. | Deliberate practice explains a meaningful but incomplete share of performance differences, and how much varies substantially by domain (Macnamara et al., 2014) — treat it as one contributing factor, not a formula. |
| "A polished finished product proves the student has mastered the skill." | Incomplete. | A strong-looking outcome can be produced with heavy teacher support; process, safety and how independently it was achieved matter as much as the object itself — see `practical-skills-mode.md`'s process/outcome/independence distinction. |

## Hard rule

No generational-decline rhetoric, technological determinism, neuromyths, learning-styles matching, "goldfish attention span" claims, or categorical statements where the underlying evidence is actually conditional. When in doubt, name the moderators (age, task, prior knowledge, design quality) instead of asserting a flat effect.
