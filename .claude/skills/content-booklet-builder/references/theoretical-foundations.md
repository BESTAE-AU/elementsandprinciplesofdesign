# Theoretical foundations

Not every educational theory here is equally established. Hold traditions to an internal evidence hierarchy rather than treating a design pattern and a slogan as the same kind of claim, and never turn a broad research finding into an invented numerical rule ("every page must contain exactly 250 words" does not follow from "working memory is limited").

**A — Strong:** replicated effects, classroom evidence and/or high-quality meta-analysis. Spacing, retrieval, worked examples for novices.
**B — Generally supported:** good evidence with meaningful moderators. Guided inquiry, feedback, self-explanation, interleaving.
**C — Promising / context-dependent:** encouraging evidence, but implementation or research quality varies. UDL intervention effects, many digital-learning claims.
**D — Emerging:** too new for firm prescriptions. Most GenAI/cognitive-offloading evidence currently sits here, despite some striking recent experiments.
**E — Do not endorse:** learning-styles matching, digital-native assumptions, fixed generational attention-collapse claims. See `claims-to-handle-carefully.md`.

These labels are an internal safeguard, not something to print on a worksheet — don't surface "Tier A" language to students or in a casual planning note unless Britt specifically wants the rationale spelled out.

## By tradition

**Cognitive Load Theory** (Sweller, Paas, van Merriënboer, Kalyuga) — [A/B] Working memory is limited; prior knowledge changes what's difficult; extraneous load can be reduced; support that helps novices can become redundant (even harmful) for experts (expertise-reversal effect). Segment complex material, integrate related information spatially, eliminate decorative clutter, pre-teach prerequisites, use worked examples, and fade support as expertise grows. Don't read CLT as "make everything easy" — remove unnecessary difficulty, not meaningful challenge.

**Explicit instruction and modelling** (Rosenshine; Engelmann; Stockard et al.) — [A] Clear modelling, guided practice, checking and cumulative review have substantial support, particularly during initial acquisition (Stockard et al., 2018). Direct Instruction as a named curricular model is not the same claim as "explicit teaching in general" — don't conflate them. Teach unfamiliar knowledge and procedures clearly before expecting unsupported performance.

**Worked examples** (Sweller, Renkl, van Gog) — [A] Especially strong for novices learning procedures and structured problem solving; a 2023 maths meta-analysis found a medium average benefit (*g* = .48; Barbieri et al., 2023). Use worked example → completion problem → independent problem, with fading rather than one example followed immediately by a large exercise set. Not maths-only — model analytical paragraphs, source-analysis, scientific calculations, claim–evidence–reasoning, geographical data interpretation, design-process exemplars, annotated artworks, sentence construction. Explain *why* decisions were made, not only what steps were performed — that's what makes the modelling worth more than a demonstrated procedure.

**Retrieval practice** (Roediger, Karpicke, Agarwal) — [A] Generally improves later retention versus restudy, including in classroom research; a systematic classroom review found medium-or-large benefits in 57% of included experiments (Agarwal et al., 2021). Go beyond definitions — retrieve facts, vocabulary, processes, diagrams, formulas, examples, explanations, conceptual relationships. Copying from the preceding paragraph is not retrieval; a student should sometimes have to close the source or turn the page. Let students correct their own responses after attempting recall. Keep it mostly low-stakes.

**Spacing / distributed practice** (Cepeda; Kang) — [A] Re-exposure separated in time generally improves durable retention; a 2025 classroom meta-analysis found a moderate advantage for distributed over massed practice (*d* = .54; Mawson & Kang, 2025). Deliberately revisit earlier learning across a booklet rather than concentrating all revision immediately before summative assessment. Track when an idea was introduced and last revisited (a running "knowledge ledger") — this is one of the biggest advantages an AI-assisted design process has over page-by-page manual planning.

**Interleaving** (Rohrer; Brunmair & Richter) — [B] Useful particularly when students need to discriminate between similar problem or category types, but not universally superior — meta-analytic average *g* = .42 with strong variation by material (Brunmair & Richter, 2019). Establish component knowledge first, then increasingly mix problem types once students can perform each individually. Random scrambling from lesson one just confuses novices.

**Generative learning** (Fiorella & Mayer, 2015; Fiorella, 2023) — [B] Reading or copying content doesn't by itself produce understanding — explaining, drawing, mapping, predicting, teaching an idea, and linking to prior knowledge can promote sense-making, though effectiveness depends on prior knowledge, task and support.

**Self-explanation** (Chi; Bisra et al.) — [A/B] Prompting learners to explain relationships and reasoning improves learning; a meta-analysis reported *g* = .55 overall (Bisra et al., 2018). "Why does this step work?", "What changed here?", "How does this evidence support the claim?" tend to beat generic "reflect on this" prompts.

**Critical thinking as argument** (Kuhn; Abrami et al.) — [B] Critical thinking is teachable but not content-free — reasoning depends substantially on domain knowledge, disciplinary conventions and evidence. Don't build "higher-order" questions on material students haven't actually learned yet; build knowledge and reasoning together. Progression: claim → evidence → how the evidence supports the claim → assumptions → alternative explanations → counterarguments → evaluating competing evidence → revised conclusion → justified final position.

**Formative assessment** (Black & Wiliam; Wiliam) — [B] Positive but more modest and heterogeneous than slogans suggest — a 2024 K–12 meta-analysis reported *g* = .25 (Yao et al., 2024). A question is formative only when the evidence it produces is actually used to decide the next step: reteach, correction, alternative example, extension or review.

**Feedback / feedforward** (Hattie & Timperley; Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie) — [A/B] Not automatically effective — a large meta-analysis found *d* = .48 overall with substantial heterogeneity tied to information content (Wisniewski et al., 2020). Good feedback answers: where am I going? how am I going? where to next? — and creates an actual opportunity to act on it. Avoid vague filler ("good work", "try harder", "add more detail").

**Metacognition / self-regulated learning** (Zimmerman; Winne; Donker et al.) — [B] Planning, monitoring, strategy selection and reflection can be taught, but effects differ substantially across domains (Donker et al., 2014). Use specific prompts, not generic emotional reflection: What can you now explain without notes? Which part still needs support? What error did you make and why? Which strategy helped?

**Constructive alignment / backward design** (Biggs; Wiggins & McTighe) — [framework, not a causal-effect claim] Planning frameworks: outcomes, activities and assessment should target the same knowledge and performance. Generate materials backward from curriculum intention and evidence of learning, not forward from "activities that sound engaging."

**Disciplinary literacy** (Shanahan & Shanahan; Moje; Luke) — [B] Reading, writing, vocabulary and evidence work differently across disciplines — literacy is an access issue across the whole curriculum, not the English department's job alone. Subject-specific reading/vocabulary/representation/argument structures beat one generic literacy template.

**UDL and inclusive pedagogy** (CAST; Graham; Florian; Rose & Meyer) — [C] The core principle — reduce unnecessary barriers while preserving meaningful challenge — is sound. The intervention literature is less mature than retrieval or spacing research; a 2023 meta-analysis reported a moderate combined achievement effect with notable study limitations (author details unverified — see `claims-to-handle-carefully.md`). Build accessibility into the core booklet rather than designing for an imagined "average student" and patching afterward.

**Social model of disability / reasonable adjustment** — Barriers arise from the interaction between learner and environment; inclusion means adapting curriculum, pedagogy, assessment and access — not just co-locating students in the same room. Ask "what barrier is this design creating?" before asking how to "fix" a student.

## Assessment purposes

Distinguish three purposes and weight a workbooklet toward the first two:

- **Assessment *for* learning** — evidence teachers and students use to identify current understanding and decide next steps. Should dominate a workbooklet.
- **Assessment *as* learning** — students monitoring, evaluating and regulating their own learning.
- **Assessment *of* learning** — evidence used to judge achievement at a point in time. Selected tasks only.

All assessment should be valid, purposeful, aligned, transparent, accessible, fair, sufficiently challenging, capable of revealing misconceptions, useful for feedback, and appropriate to the actual construct being assessed — don't accidentally assess literacy or tech skill when that's not the point.
