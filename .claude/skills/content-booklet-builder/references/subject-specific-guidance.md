# Subject-specific disciplinary thinking

There is no single worksheet recipe — "analyse" means something different in Textiles, Visual Arts, History and Maths. Load a discipline's schema before generating anything for it. Britt's own subjects are marked; the rest are here for covering a colleague's class or cross-KLA collaboration.

## Britt's subjects, mapped

- **Textiles Technology / Textiles and Design** — draws on both families below. Design-process rigour (briefs, constraints, prototyping, testing, iteration) for construction and technical work; observation, annotation and critique for design development and visual/contextual analysis. Printed material should scaffold actual designing and making, not turn Textiles into a written-theory subject.
- **Multimedia** — Technologies family, plus the digital-composition/authentic-publication considerations from the core educational position — this is a subject where screens legitimately earn their place often (multimedia composition, collaborative publication).
- **Graphics Technology, Design and Technology, Timber Technology** — Technologies family.
- **Visual Arts** — Creative Arts / Arts family.

## Technologies

Design briefs; criteria; ideation; research; constraints; technical knowledge; labelled/planned designs; prototyping; production; testing; evaluation; feedback; iteration. Printed material should scaffold actual designing and making, not displace it.

## Arts (Creative Arts / Visual Arts)

Observation; disciplinary vocabulary; exemplars; artist or practitioner models; contextual knowledge; experimentation; creative decisions; design development; analysis; critique; reflection. A booklet can structure noticing and planning but shouldn't displace the actual artistic production.

## English

Close reading, vocabulary, syntax, genre, literary interpretation, rhetoric, evidence, authorial choices, writing craft. Model annotation and analytical writing explicitly; teach language explicitly; distinguish knowledge *of* a text from interpretation *of* it. Avoid endless generic comprehension questions.

## Mathematics

Conceptual understanding, procedural fluency, representations, worked examples, mathematical language, variation, strategy selection, reasoning/justification, error diagnosis. Vary what matters mathematically across a practice set, not just the surface numbers.

## Science

Prior conceptions, causal models, scientific vocabulary, representations, graphs, measurement, variables, evidence, explanation, disciplinary inquiry. Printed material should prepare for and consolidate practical work, not replace it. Guided inquiry (not unguided discovery) is particularly valuable here.

## History / Humanities

Chronology, context, causation, continuity and change, significance, perspective, evidence, provenance, contestability, argument. Questions should make students reason *with* sources, not just extract facts from them.

## Geography

Spatial reasoning, scale, place, interconnection, data, maps, fieldwork, interpretation of geographic representations. Include genuine maps and datasets, not prose *about* geography.

## Languages

Comprehensible input, vocabulary retrieval, morphology, grammar, sentence patterns, receptive-then-productive progression, increasingly free communication. Audio/pronunciation is an obvious case where digital tools add value paper can't.

## Health and Physical Education

Scenarios, models, anatomical diagrams, health evidence, practical-performance reflection, decision-making frameworks, analysis of claims and misinformation. Physical performance, interaction and embodied practice need experiences beyond the page.
