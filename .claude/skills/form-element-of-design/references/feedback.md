# Form: feedback and marking

Use this file when a teacher shares student work or a justification and wants feedback, a mark or "what gets them to the top band" on form.

## Workflow

1. **Read the work against its brief.** Note the audience, purpose and every touchpoint or output named (cups, embroidery, a phone screen, signage…). Reproduction problems usually hide there.
2. **Start from what the student actually did.** Quote their words or point to the part of the design, and name the form variable involved (Variables in `SKILL.md`).
3. **Check for the patterns below** and the misconceptions in `knowledge.md`.
4. **Check production and accessibility.** Use the production table in `knowledge.md` and `method/checks-and-language.md`.
5. **Place the reasoning on the justification ladder** (weak → developing → strong → sophisticated; `method/justification.md`). Say which link in the chain is missing.
6. **If marking:** use the rubric supplied. If none is supplied, use `marking-rubric-builder` (if available) to generate NESA-aligned criteria first, mark against them and say they were generated. Without that skill, give feedback without a grade (`method/feedback.md`).
7. **Write it up:**
   - **What's working:** specific, not generic.
   - **What's holding it back:** 2–4 points, each quoting the student.
   - **Next steps:** one or two, phrased as tests or design decisions, in priority order.
   - **Model structure** (optional): a fill-in-the-blanks version of a top-level justification, with a note not to copy the numbers.
   - **Teacher notes:** current level, gap to the top, and a conferencing question.

Keep the tone warm, honest and specific.


## Feedback patterns to look for in student work

- **Form described only by looks** — no link to use, strength or manufacture.
- **Shape and form confused** in written analysis.
- **Render trusted over prototype** — no physical test of grip, balance or fit.
- **Sharp edges** left where hands or bodies contact the product.
- **Forms that can't be made** with the student's available process (overhangs, undercuts, impossible radii).
- **Body-value language** when discussing garment volume.

## Justification examples (Year 9 timber phone stand)

- **Weak:** "I made it curvy because it looks cool."
- **Developing:** "I curved the back so it looks smooth and modern."
- **Strong:** "I shaped the back support as a 70° curved slope with a 5 mm lip, so a phone rests at a readable angle without sliding. The rounded edges make it comfortable to pick up and safer on a desk used by younger siblings."
- **Sophisticated:** "I tested 60°, 70° and 80° angles with three phone sizes. At 60° the screen reflected the ceiling light; at 80° larger phones tipped forward. 70° worked for all three, so I used it. Cutting the curve on the bandsaw took longer than a straight slope, but the curve removed a sharp edge and used less timber overall."
