# Practical skill / how-to mode — full detail

Full grounding for Practical Skill Mode (see SKILL.md for the condensed, always-loaded version). This is the mode that matters most for Britt's own subjects — Textiles construction, Timber Technology joints and machine use, Graphics/CAD, Design and Technology prototyping — since these are fundamentally "being able to do," not "knowing about."

## Two linked engines

Treat Knowledge Learning Mode (the main workbooklet architecture) and Practical Skill Mode as complementary, not competing — a single workbooklet can switch between them page to page, depending on what's actually being taught at that point. A unit on constructing a garment might use Knowledge Mode for fibre properties and Practical Mode for the seam techniques.

## Knowing how vs being able to do

A student who can correctly list the steps for using a coping saw, sewing a French seam, or operating a laser cutter has not thereby demonstrated they can safely and accurately do it. Distinguish:

| Knowledge | Student demonstrates |
|---|---|
| **Declarative** | "I know what this is and why it matters." |
| **Procedural** | "I know how to perform the process." |
| **Conditional / adaptive** | "I know when, why and how to modify the process when conditions change." |

A workbooklet should support all three, but practical performance itself is the evidence of skill — written recall alone is inadequate when the NESA outcome verb is *perform, construct, operate, demonstrate, make* or *create*.

## Procedural literacy is a separate goal from procedural performance

A student can perform a skill and still be unable to precisely explain or write it up — and vice versa: a student can recite a procedure accurately without being able to execute it. Where the outcome calls for it (a written method, a design-process journal entry, peer-teaching a technique, a recipe or process write-up), treat producing and interpreting procedural text as its own explicit teaching target rather than assuming students absorb it by osmosis from doing the task.

Procedural texts are a genre with real structure — goal/purpose, equipment or materials, safety, preparation, sequenced method, decision points ("if X, do Y"), quality checkpoints, troubleshooting, evaluation of the finished result (Martin & Rose, 2008) — and their own vocabulary demands distinct from general literacy: technical nouns (*mordant, kerf, bias, aperture*), precise action verbs (*align, clamp, fold, calibrate* — not just "do"), sequence language (*before, once, subsequently*), conditional language (*if the mixture separates…*), causal language (*because, so that, otherwise*), measurement/manner language (*approximately 5mm, perpendicular, evenly*), and safety language (*must, must not, ensure*). This is where `references/literacy-and-language.md`'s Tier 3 (technical/subject-specific) vocabulary shows up most concretely — teach it explicitly rather than assuming it.

Where writing or explaining a procedure is itself part of the outcome, assess the two separately: performance evidence for "can they do it," a piece of procedural writing/explanation for "can they communicate it accurately." Don't let a well-written method stand in as evidence of competent performance, or the reverse.

## Classify the skill before writing instructions

Pedagogy differs by skill type — don't use identical treatment for all of them:

| Skill type | Examples (Britt's context) | Emphasise |
|---|---|---|
| **Fine-motor / manual** | Hand-stitching, machine sewing, pattern cutting, joinery, food prep | Positioning, tool handling, visual demonstration, precision, product quality |
| **Scientific / technical procedure** | Dyeing/chemical processes, circuit building (Multimedia/D&T electronics) | Sequence, equipment, measurement, safety, accuracy, error prevention |
| **Digital procedural** | CAD, Photoshop/Illustrator, video editing, spreadsheet/coding tasks | Screen demonstration, sequence, decision points, practice without instructions on screen |
| **Complex occupational / design skill** | Garment construction, timber project builds, multimedia productions | Whole-task performance, component skills only where needed, planning, troubleshooting, quality control |
| **Creative practical** | Design development, visual composition | Technique plus interpretation, variation, experimentation, quality judgement |
| **Culturally governed practice** | A fibre/weaving technique, food knowledge, or making practice tied to a specific culture or Country — most likely to come up in Textiles | Knowledge authority and consent, not just technique — see below before treating it as an ordinary skill to sequence |

Some practical skills carry cultural authority that technique-focused instruction can't substitute for. A weaving or fibre technique specific to an Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander community, for instance, isn't just a set of hand movements to demonstrate — before generating a generic how-to for it, ask who has authority to teach it, whether it's appropriate for general classroom use, and whose Country or community it belongs to. This is the same ICIP and local-consultation safeguard as `references/inclusive-design.md`, applied to hands-on/making content specifically rather than only written or visual material. Where that authority sits outside the classroom, flag it to Britt for community consultation rather than generating a generic version — don't reason that because a technique is technically describable, it's therefore fine to teach it as a standard practical skill.

An external attentional focus (cueing the *effect* of a movement — "keep the fabric flat as it feeds through" rather than "hold your left hand at 30 degrees") has reasonably strong support for motor-skill tasks specifically (Chua et al., 2021) — use it for fine-motor/physical skills, not for a CAD walkthrough where it doesn't apply.

## The practical-skill sequence

Not rigid — these are instructional functions to consider, in roughly this order:

**Know the purpose → check prerequisites and safety → observe → notice → rehearse → perform (with support) → check quality → receive feedback → repeat and refine → perform independently → vary / transfer → reflect.**

For novices: *see it → understand it → do it with support → get feedback → do it again.* For students building competence: *do it with fewer prompts → diagnose errors → adapt the skill → perform independently.* This is cognitive apprenticeship (Collins et al., 1989) — modelling, coaching, scaffolding, then articulation, reflection and increasing independence — and reflects Vygotsky's point that learners can perform beyond their independent level with the right guidance.

## Demonstration is not optional for most of these skills

A printed page should say, plainly, when a skill needs it: *this procedure is difficult to learn from written directions alone — demonstrate it first; use the workbooklet as a stable reference during performance, not a replacement for watching and doing.* Observational learning has real support for motor-skill acquisition (a PE systematic review of 18 studies found strong evidence it helps; Han et al., 2022), but observation is not equivalent to doing — physical practice produces the stronger direct learning effect. Pair demonstration with **observation cues** so students know what to actually watch for, since they don't extract this automatically from a complex demonstration:

> While watching, notice: where the blade starts; how the material is secured; the direction of pressure; what changes as the cut finishes.

For a skill where timing, rhythm, force or a changing spatial relationship matters more than any single static position (a machine feed rate, a stitch rhythm, a swing), a recorded demonstration that can be paused, slowed and replayed is a clearer affordance than either a live demonstration (gone once it's over) or a sequence of static photos (missing the movement between them) — one of the more clear-cut cases where digital genuinely earns its place over print, per the core educational position's affordance test.

## Writing an actual instruction step

Go beyond a bare numbered list. For each significant step, consider four components — **Action** (what do I do), **Cue** (what should I notice), **Reason** (why does this matter), **Check** (how do I know I've done it correctly):

| Stage | Action | Cue | Why | Check |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Example (microscope) | Begin with lowest-power objective | Specimen should appear within a broad field | Low power makes locating the specimen easier | Specimen is visible and centred |

This starts building a mental model of the procedure, not just a compliance checklist.

Where it's genuinely ambiguous, mark whether a step is **non-negotiable** (safety, or a sequence dependency where doing it out of order breaks the result), **standard procedure** (a reliable default for novices — not the only way, but the one to teach first), **technique choice** (several valid methods exist — say so, rather than implying there's one correct way), or **expert judgement** (depends on reading the specific conditions). Students eventually benefit from being able to tell these apart — "which parts of this were compulsory, and which were technique choices?" is itself a useful question once they have a secure base.

## Troubleshooting, explicitly

Competence isn't just reproducing the normal sequence. Build a troubleshooting table once students have a secure reference point:

| Problem | Possible cause | What will you change? |
|---|---|---|
| Seam is puckering | ___ | ___ |
| Joint is not square | ___ | ___ |

This develops adaptive expertise — integrating routine procedure with non-routine judgement (van Merriënboer et al., 2002). Where it helps a student or Britt diagnose *what kind* of intervention is needed, distinguish the error type before deciding how to respond: a **knowledge error** (didn't know what to do — needs re-teaching), a **decision error** (knew the options, chose wrong one — needs practice selecting, not just executing), an **execution error** (chose correctly, performed it inaccurately — needs more guided practice and feedback), or a **monitoring error** (didn't notice something had gone wrong — needs explicit training in what to check and when, not another run-through of the procedure).

## Feedback and retry

Build a cycle: **attempt 1 → specific, actionable feedback → adjustment → attempt 2** — not "attempt → teacher ticks the page → activity finished." Feedback should name what worked and one or two specific next adjustments ("your cut followed the line accurately; on the next attempt, reduce pressure in the final 10mm to prevent breakout"), and always be followed by another opportunity to perform. Don't apply a rigid fixed feedback-frequency schedule ("lots of feedback early, then taper on a formula") — the evidence for that specific "guidance hypothesis" is weaker than textbooks sometimes suggest (McKay et al., 2022); fade support based on the learner, task and risk, not a formula.

## Fade scaffolding across attempts

A strong practical sequence: **first attempt** — full instructions + diagrams + demonstration; **second attempt** — abbreviated steps + key visual cues; **third attempt** — short performance checklist only; **fourth attempt** — no procedural instructions; **transfer task** — changed context or material requiring adaptation. The purpose of scaffolding is independence, not permanent dependence on the workbooklet.

## Separate performance from learning

A student performing correctly while continuously coached hasn't necessarily learned the skill yet. Where it matters, distinguish acquisition (can they do it now, with support?), retention (can they do it later, unprompted?) and transfer (can they do it under changed conditions?).

## Process quality vs outcome quality vs independence

Distinguish criteria for *how* the student worked, *what* they produced, and *how much support it took* — three different things, and a strong-looking finished product can quietly hide a lot of teacher intervention:

- **Process** (example: woodwork) — secures material correctly; selects appropriate tool; uses controlled technique; follows safety procedure.
- **Outcome** — accurate dimension; square edge; clean finish.
- **Independence** — how much prompting, correction or teacher setup it took to get there. Two students can produce an identical finished piece while one required eight prompts and a corrected setup and the other caught and fixed their own error unprompted — that's a real difference in practical competence, not a rounding error, so it's worth recording (a simple "prompts needed" tally alongside the finished piece is often enough) rather than only marking the object.

Where safety is involved, treat it as **non-compensatory** in any judgement of overall competence — strong craftsmanship doesn't offset an unsafe procedure, and a rubric or checklist shouldn't average the two together into a single score that lets one cancel the other out.

## Assess through performance, and record the process

If the outcome verb is perform/construct/operate/demonstrate/create, written recall alone is inadequate evidence — NESA assessment guidance explicitly recognises practical activities, demonstrations and teacher observation as legitimate evidence. A practical workbooklet can double as the record of the learning process itself: prerequisite knowledge and safety check (student), first practice and feedback (teacher observation), second attempt and quality check (teacher/student), independent performance (teacher), reflection (student) — rather than pretending the writing is the skill.

## Whole-task vs fragmented practice (4C/ID)

For complex practical skills (a full garment, a timber project, a multimedia production), don't necessarily fragment into dozens of isolated micro-skills before students meet the whole task — identify the authentic whole performance first, break out components only where targeted practice is genuinely needed (part-task practice for a recurring difficult element), then reconnect components to the complete task (van Merriënboer et al., 2002). Provide procedural "how-to" information just-in-time, at the point of use, rather than requiring a large instruction sheet to be memorised up front — this is a cognitive-load concern as much as a motivational one.

## Introduce variation carefully

Once basic competence is developing, vary conditions (different material, changed dimensions, time pressure, an unfamiliar fault to diagnose) so students select and adapt the procedure rather than only repeat it. But don't mechanically maximise variation/randomness for school-age novices — the evidence for high contextual interference is stronger in lab settings and adults than in applied, younger-learner settings (Czyż et al., 2024). Establish basic competence first, then introduce purposeful variation.

## Accessibility applies fully here — the demand doesn't get reduced, the barrier does

Long, complex or poorly sequenced instructions are themselves a barrier (Graham & Tancredi's Accessible Pedagogies). The operating principle: **don't reduce the complexity of the learning because the instructions are complex — reduce unnecessary complexity in the instructions so students can engage with the real complexity of the task.** A student with language-processing difficulties may need shorter procedural sentences, numbered stages, diagrams, pre-taught key vocabulary, more processing time, demonstration, the written sequence kept available during performance, a chance to rehearse orally/physically first, and repeated practice — none of which changes the expected skill.

## Safety — a hard rule, not a design preference

Never invent a safety procedure. For anything involving machinery, tools, chemicals, heat, or physical risk, defer to school procedures, curriculum authority guidance, manufacturer instructions, SDS/technical documentation and trained-teacher supervision — flag to Britt that she needs to verify or supply the actual procedure rather than generating one. Visually distinguish **SAFETY** (mandatory) from **TIP** (improves performance) so they don't read the same on the page. Never imply that reading the instructions is sufficient authorisation to proceed independently — for higher-risk activities, build in an explicit teacher-check point:

> **STOP — TEACHER CHECK.** Before turning on the machine, show your teacher: work is secured / guard is correctly positioned / settings are correct / exclusion zone is clear. Teacher initials: ______

## Practical page template

What you're learning (skill outcome) → Why this matters (authentic purpose) → Equipment/materials → Safety first (non-negotiable) → Watch (demonstration) → Look for (2–5 critical cues) → How to (concise sequenced instructions, Action/Cue/Reason/Check where it matters) → Visual (diagram/photo where it genuinely helps) → Quality check (what success looks like) → Common mistakes → Practise (first attempt) → Feedback → Try again → Independent attempt (scaffold removed) → Challenge (adaptation/transfer) → Reflect.
