# Time and duration: analysis and model answers

This skill handles **analysis and model answers** for time and duration as an element of design. Everything known about time and duration (definition, variables, types, relationships with the other elements and the principles, fields, production, misconceptions and worked examples) is in `references/element-knowledge.md`. The shared method is in `references/design-method.md`. Read the sections the workflow points to.

Related skills: `time-and-duration-element-of-design-teaching`, `time-and-duration-element-of-design-feedback`, `time-and-duration-element-of-design-practice`; `time-and-duration-element-of-design` for general questions about time and duration; the `*-principle-of-design` skills for principles.

Apply the language rules in `references/design-method.md` §14: Australian English, describe before interpreting, conditional language for emotional or cultural associations, and name the criterion whenever something "works".

## Workflow

1. **Fix the artefact.** If the user gives an image or description, work from it. If you're inventing one (for an exemplar), describe it briefly first so students can picture it, with the specific time and duration characteristics the answers will cite.
2. **Work through the progression** (method §2): identify → describe → explain → analyse (connect to communication, purpose, audience) → evaluate against a named criterion, including a limitation or trade-off.
3. **For levelled exemplars** (e.g. developing / sound / high-range), make each level add a link in the reasoning chain, not just more words. Respect any word limits. Under each, add a short note on what lifts it to the next level.
4. **Analyse relationships.** Use the relationships with other elements and principles below to go beyond the element on its own.
5. **Language:** conditional wording for emotional or cultural readings, observation before interpretation, and no "it works" without a criterion.

## Worked analysis (model for exemplars and annotations)

Scenario: a 30-second school open-day promo. It opens with rapid 0.5-second cuts of students in workshops, then holds for 4 seconds on a slow push-in of a student looking up from a sewing machine, then shows the date and time as kinetic type for 3 seconds before a 2-second end card.

- **Identify:** "The video uses time."
- **Describe:** "The first 12 seconds contain about 24 cuts of 0.5 s each; then a 4-second single shot with a slow push-in; then 3 seconds of animated date/time text; then a 2-second end card."
- **Explain:** "The fast montage builds energy and shows variety; the sudden long hold slows the pace so the viewer settles on one student, creating emphasis before the key information."
- **Analyse:** "Time works with contrast and hierarchy: the change from fast to slow makes the held shot feel important, and placing the date straight after the hold means it arrives when attention is highest. The push-in adds movement without breaking the stillness."
- **Evaluate:** "Effective for an energetic, emotional hook on social media. However, 3 seconds for animated date, time and address text is likely too short for parents to read — especially as the text is still moving. Holding the finished text for at least 2 more seconds, or putting the details on the end card, would fix this at the cost of 2 seconds of montage."

Each level should add a link in the chain, not simply more words.

## Justification examples (Year 10 logo animation)

- **Weak:** "I made the animation fast because it looks cool."
- **Developing:** "I made the logo animate in quickly so it feels energetic."
- **Strong:** "I animated the logo in over 0.8 s with an ease-out, so it arrives quickly and settles gently, then held it for 2 s so viewers can read the name. That suits a 6-second social ad for a skate shop where people scroll fast."
- **Sophisticated:** "I compared 0.4 s, 0.8 s and 1.5 s entries with five classmates. At 0.4 s two didn't notice the logo arrive; at 1.5 s it felt slow and ate into the hold. 0.8 s with an ease-out was noticed by all five and left 2 s to read. I also made a static version for users with reduced-motion settings. I gave up some drama for readability."

## How time and duration work with the other elements

- **Line:** lines draw on, trace and reveal; the speed of a line's growth sets pace.
- **Direction:** entry side, travel path, exit and consistent screen direction across cuts.
- **Shape:** shapes morph, mask and transition; recognition must survive the change.
- **Form:** turntables, camera moves round 3D forms, products that unfold.
- **Space:** motion needs room — entry and exit space; pauses are temporal "space" that let content breathe.
- **Size and scale:** zooms, scale-ups and shot-size changes over time.
- **Colour:** colour changes through a sequence (grade shifts, state colours); rapid flashing is an accessibility risk.
- **Value:** fades to and from black or white; light changes across a scene.
- **Texture:** grain and noise animate; compression can smear texture in fast motion.
- **Typography:** reading time is the hard constraint on titles, captions and kinetic type.
- **Layout and composition:** composition changes over time — hierarchy shifts as elements enter and leave.

## How time and duration contribute to the principles

- **Rhythm:** edit and animation rhythm — regular cuts, beats, repeated intervals.
- **Emphasis:** a hold, a slow-motion moment or a pause before a reveal.
- **Hierarchy:** order of appearance ranks information (what arrives first reads first).
- **Contrast:** a sudden change of pace (fast montage to a long still shot).
- **Unity:** consistent durations and easing across a motion system feel like one brand.
- **Movement:** timing and easing make movement read as heavy, light, mechanical or natural.
- **Tension:** delay, withholding and extended duration build suspense.
- **Balance:** alternating fast and slow sections balance a sequence.
