# Shape — element of design

This skill gives Claude the shape-specific knowledge for teaching, analysing and designing with shape. The shared method — analysis progression, justification levels, Factors Affecting Design, experimentation, differentiation, feedback approach and language rules — is in `references/design-method.md`. Read the relevant section of that file when a task calls for it.

The core habit: treat every shape as a decision. Move from **what the shape is** (geometry, edges, closure, figure-ground) → **what it does** → **why that matters here** → **how well it works and what it costs**.

## What shape is

Shape is a two-dimensional area defined by an edge, boundary, contrast, colour, value, texture or negative space. It can be geometric, organic, abstract, representational or symbolic, and can act as image, container, symbol, interface component, motif or pattern piece.

Keep it distinct from its neighbours:
- **Shape vs form:** shape is flat area; form is three-dimensional volume (actual or implied).
- **Shape vs contour:** a contour is the boundary of a shape or form, not the area itself.
- **Shape vs silhouette:** a silhouette is the recognisable outer shape of a subject — one reading of a form.
- **Shape vs outline:** shapes don't need outlines; contrast or negative space can define them.

## Variables

| Variable | Range | Typical effect | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Geometry** | geometric, organic, biomorphic, irregular | recognisability, precision, visual character | Avoid fixed personality claims |
| **Complexity** | simple → complex | recognition, detail, reproduction | Complex shapes fail small, cut or stitched |
| **Closure** | closed, open, implied | containment, figure-ground reading | Open shapes may be recognised more slowly |
| **Regularity / symmetry** | regular, symmetrical, irregular, asymmetric | predictability, rhythm, distinctiveness | Irregularity complicates systems |
| **Positive/negative relationship** | figure and surrounding or enclosed space | hidden forms, optical interplay | Check at small sizes and reversed out |
| **Edge character** | sharp, rounded, scalloped, rough, smooth | silhouette, tactility, production | Corner radii and gaps constrain cutting |
| **Proportion** | aspect ratio, relative dimensions | hierarchy, fit, stability | Test responsive and physical versions |
| **Overlap / intersection** | layered, merged, subtracted | depth, grouping, compound symbols | Boolean operations leave tiny slivers |
| **Repetition** | motif, tessellation, pattern | rhythm, identity | Test tiling, seams, crops |

## Types

- **Geometric** — measurable: circles, squares, triangles, polygons; icons, logos, UI, technical graphics.
- **Organic / biomorphic** — irregular, fluid, natural-feeling; surface design, illustration, branding.
- **Abstract** — non-literal shapes carrying meaning or structure; identity systems, data graphics.
- **Representational** — recognisable depictions; pictograms, illustrations.
- **Symbolic** — meaning set by convention or culture; signage, icons (e.g. a triangle warning sign).
- **Positive / negative** — occupied figure and surrounding ground; logos, layouts, interfaces.
- **Open / closed** — incomplete or complete boundary; marks, icons, composition.
- **Silhouette** — outer profile that carries recognition; brand marks, fashion, products.

## How shape works with the other elements

- **Line:** a closed line creates an outline shape; line weight changes perceived shape size. Shapes can also be implied by a series of lines stopping at a common edge.
- **Direction:** pointed, wedge and chevron shapes point; asymmetric shapes appear to face a way. Arrangement of shapes along a path creates direction.
- **Form:** shape is the 2D reading of form. Add value, overlap and perspective and a shape starts to read as volume; a garment silhouette is a shape reading of a 3D form.
- **Space:** figure-ground — the negative space between and inside shapes is itself a shape. Well-known logos hide a second shape in the negative space.
- **Size and scale:** detail inside a shape disappears as it shrinks; small versions of a mark often need simplified shapes and opened-up gaps.
- **Colour:** colour can define a shape without an outline; two shapes of equal area can feel unequal if one is more saturated.
- **Value:** value contrast is usually what separates a shape from its ground; low contrast weakens recognition.
- **Texture:** texture fill can define a shape (hatching, pattern) and edge texture changes its character.
- **Typography:** letterforms are shapes; counters (the enclosed spaces in a, e, o) are negative shapes that close up in embroidery or small print.
- **Layout and composition:** containers, cards and panels are shapes that group content; the overall arrangement of shapes sets balance and hierarchy.
- **Time and duration:** in motion, shapes morph, mask and reveal; recognition must survive the transition.

## How shape contributes to the principles

- **Unity and harmony:** a consistent shape language (same corner radius, same geometry) makes an icon set or brand feel like one family.
- **Contrast and variety:** a single organic shape among geometric ones, or one sharp shape among rounded ones, stands out.
- **Emphasis:** an unusual or larger shape becomes the focal point.
- **Balance:** a few large shapes can balance many small ones; asymmetric balance depends on shape weight and position.
- **Repetition, pattern and rhythm:** repeated motifs build pattern; varied spacing or size builds rhythm.
- **Proportion:** internal proportions of a shape, and the ratio between shapes.
- **Proximity and alignment:** shapes near each other group; shared edges align.
- **Hierarchy:** container shapes (cards, panels, badges) signal levels of information.

## Shape across fields

- **Branding:** shape can be a distinctive asset beyond the logo — ownable containers, crops, corner systems, frames, masks, icons, packaging windows and silhouettes. Define allowed transformations, clear space, corner logic, small-size simplifications and negative-space behaviour.
- **Graphic and communication design:** shapes organise layouts, containers, badges, infographics and icon systems. Ask what they group, separate or signal.
- **Photography, film and animation:** cropping and lighting create dominant shapes; title cards, lower thirds and masks are shape systems; morphing shapes act as transitions.
- **UI / UX:** buttons, cards, chips, fields, badges and icons rely on shape for affordance and state. Shape shouldn't be the only state signal, and the touch target is the interactive area, not just the visible shape.
- **Textiles and fashion:** motifs, appliqué, patchwork, pattern pieces and silhouette. Pattern-piece shapes have grain and construction consequences.
- **Product and industrial design:** profiles, openings, panels and graphics; shape affects nesting, cutting, tool paths, grip zones and brand recognition.
- **Interiors and architecture:** plan footprints, openings and furniture arrangements make shapes that must be read alongside volume and circulation.
- **Technical communication:** standard symbols are defined shapes; don't stylise them without a reason.

## Production and reproduction

| Process | Shape risks |
|---|---|
| Die cutting | Minimum bridge widths, internal corners and registration limit shapes |
| Laser, vinyl, CNC cutting | Kerf and tool width limit small internal details and acute corners |
| Embroidery | Tiny counters and enclosed negative spaces close up |
| Printing | Small knockouts and reversals fill in; test positive and negative |
| Responsive digital use | Complex shapes need simplified variants at small sizes |

## Common misconceptions

| Misconception | Better understanding |
|---|---|
| "Shapes need outlines." | Colour, value, texture or negative space can define a shape. Identify the boundary mechanism. |
| "Organic shapes are natural." | Organic describes irregular, non-geometric qualities; manufactured and digital shapes can be organic. |
| "Negative space is empty." | It shapes recognition, balance and meaning. Analyse figure and ground together. |
| "Circles are friendly, triangles are aggressive." | Associations exist but depend on context, colour, scale and use. Point to what supports the reading. |
| "Shape and form are interchangeable." | Shape is 2D area; form is 3D volume or implied volume. |

## Worked analysis (model for exemplars and annotations)

Scenario: a logo for a native plant nursery. A leaf shape is formed entirely from the negative space between two rounded hands facing each other. The mark is one flat colour.

- **Identify:** "The logo uses shape."
- **Describe:** "Two rounded, organic hand shapes face each other; the gap between them forms a pointed leaf. There is no outline — the shapes are defined by one flat colour against the background."
- **Explain:** "Because the leaf is formed from negative space, the viewer sees hands first and then 'finds' the leaf, which links care (hands) with plants (leaf) in one mark."
- **Analyse:** "Shape works with value contrast — the flat mark against a light ground makes the figure-ground reading clear — and with simplicity: two main shapes keep it recognisable. Rounded edges on the hands contrast with the leaf's pointed tip, which draws the eye to the leaf."
- **Evaluate:** "Effective as a memorable, meaningful mark at signage and website sizes. However, the leaf's narrow tip is a thin negative gap that may close up when embroidered on staff polo shirts or printed on small plant tags; a small-size version with a wider gap should be tested."

Each level should add a link in the chain, not simply more words.

## Justification examples (Year 11 app icon set)

- **Weak:** "I used rounded shapes because they look nice."
- **Developing:** "I used rounded squares for all icons so they look friendly and match."
- **Strong:** "I set every icon inside a rounded square with the same 8 px corner radius, so the set reads as one family and matches the rounded buttons in the app. That suits a study app for younger teens who need to recognise icons quickly."
- **Sophisticated:** "I tested sharp-cornered, 8 px and fully circular containers at 24 px. Circles cropped the detail in the calendar and calculator icons; sharp corners clashed with the app's buttons. The 8 px radius kept detail and matched the UI, so I set it as the rule. I gave up the extra distinctiveness of circles for clearer icons."

## Feedback patterns to look for in student work

- **Negative space ignored** — the student describes only the figure.
- **Shape and form confused** in written analysis.
- **Fixed personality claims** ("triangles are dangerous") without context.
- **Inconsistent shape language** across an icon set or brand (mixed corner radii, mixed geometry).
- **Detail that won't survive** small size, cutting or embroidery.
- **Boolean slivers** — tiny leftover fragments from overlapping shapes.

## Activity ideas for teaching resources

- **Figure-ground hunt:** find logos where the negative space forms a second shape; sketch both readings.
- **Silhouette test:** fill a product, character or logo in solid black; can it still be recognised?
- **Corner-radius experiment:** redraw one icon with 0, 4, 8 px and full radius; test at 24 px and 128 px.
- **Geometric vs organic sort:** sort 20 shapes from ads and discuss where the categories blur.
- **Die-cut / vinyl test (Graphics):** cut a detailed shape and a simplified one; record what survives.
- **Motif to pattern (Textiles):** repeat one motif in block, half-drop and mirror repeats; compare rhythm.
