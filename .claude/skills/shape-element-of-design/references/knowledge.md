# Shape: knowledge

The definition and variables are in `../SKILL.md`.

## Types

- **Geometric** — measurable: circles, squares, triangles, polygons; icons, logos, UI, technical graphics.
- **Organic / biomorphic** — irregular, fluid, natural-feeling; surface design, illustration, branding.
- **Abstract** — non-literal shapes carrying meaning or structure; identity systems, data graphics.
- **Representational** — recognisable depictions; pictograms, illustrations.
- **Symbolic** — meaning set by convention or culture; signage, icons (e.g. a triangle warning sign).
- **Positive / negative** — occupied figure and surrounding ground; logos, layouts, interfaces.
- **Open / closed** — incomplete or complete boundary; marks, icons, composition.
- **Silhouette** — outer profile that carries recognition; brand marks, fashion, products.

## How shape works with the other elements

- **Line:** a closed line creates an outline shape; line weight changes perceived shape size. Shapes can also be implied by a series of lines stopping at a common edge.
- **Direction:** pointed, wedge and chevron shapes point; asymmetric shapes appear to face a way. Arrangement of shapes along a path creates direction.
- **Form:** shape is the 2D reading of form. Add value, overlap and perspective and a shape starts to read as volume; a garment silhouette is a shape reading of a 3D form.
- **Space:** figure-ground — the negative space between and inside shapes is itself a shape. Well-known logos hide a second shape in the negative space.
- **Size and scale:** detail inside a shape disappears as it shrinks; small versions of a mark often need simplified shapes and opened-up gaps.
- **Colour:** colour can define a shape without an outline; two shapes of equal area can feel unequal if one is more saturated.
- **Value:** value contrast is usually what separates a shape from its ground; low contrast weakens recognition.
- **Texture:** texture fill can define a shape (hatching, pattern) and edge texture changes its character.
- **Typography:** letterforms are shapes; counters (the enclosed spaces in a, e, o) are negative shapes that close up in embroidery or small print.
- **Layout and composition:** containers, cards and panels are shapes that group content; the overall arrangement of shapes sets balance and hierarchy.
- **Time and duration:** in motion, shapes morph, mask and reveal; recognition must survive the transition.

## How shape contributes to the principles

- **Unity and harmony:** a consistent shape language (same corner radius, same geometry) makes an icon set or brand feel like one family.
- **Contrast and variety:** a single organic shape among geometric ones, or one sharp shape among rounded ones, stands out.
- **Emphasis:** an unusual or larger shape becomes the focal point.
- **Balance:** a few large shapes can balance many small ones; asymmetric balance depends on shape weight and position.
- **Repetition, pattern and rhythm:** repeated motifs build pattern; varied spacing or size builds rhythm.
- **Proportion:** internal proportions of a shape, and the ratio between shapes.
- **Proximity and alignment:** shapes near each other group; shared edges align.
- **Hierarchy:** container shapes (cards, panels, badges) signal levels of information.

## Shape across fields

- **Branding:** shape can be a distinctive asset beyond the logo — ownable containers, crops, corner systems, frames, masks, icons, packaging windows and silhouettes. Define allowed transformations, clear space, corner logic, small-size simplifications and negative-space behaviour.
- **Graphic and communication design:** shapes organise layouts, containers, badges, infographics and icon systems. Ask what they group, separate or signal.
- **Photography, film and animation:** cropping and lighting create dominant shapes; title cards, lower thirds and masks are shape systems; morphing shapes act as transitions.
- **UI / UX:** buttons, cards, chips, fields, badges and icons rely on shape for affordance and state. Shape shouldn't be the only state signal, and the touch target is the interactive area, not just the visible shape.
- **Textiles and fashion:** motifs, appliqué, patchwork, pattern pieces and silhouette. Pattern-piece shapes have grain and construction consequences.
- **Product and industrial design:** profiles, openings, panels and graphics; shape affects nesting, cutting, tool paths, grip zones and brand recognition.
- **Interiors and architecture:** plan footprints, openings and furniture arrangements make shapes that must be read alongside volume and circulation.
- **Technical communication:** standard symbols are defined shapes; don't stylise them without a reason.

## Production and reproduction

| Process | Shape risks |
|---|---|
| Die cutting | Minimum bridge widths, internal corners and registration limit shapes |
| Laser, vinyl, CNC cutting | Kerf and tool width limit small internal details and acute corners |
| Embroidery | Tiny counters and enclosed negative spaces close up |
| Printing | Small knockouts and reversals fill in; test positive and negative |
| Responsive digital use | Complex shapes need simplified variants at small sizes |

## Common misconceptions

| Misconception | Better understanding |
|---|---|
| "Shapes need outlines." | Colour, value, texture or negative space can define a shape. Identify the boundary mechanism. |
| "Organic shapes are natural." | Organic describes irregular, non-geometric qualities; manufactured and digital shapes can be organic. |
| "Negative space is empty." | It shapes recognition, balance and meaning. Analyse figure and ground together. |
| "Circles are friendly, triangles are aggressive." | Associations exist but depend on context, colour, scale and use. Point to what supports the reading. |
| "Shape and form are interchangeable." | Shape is 2D area; form is 3D volume or implied volume. |
