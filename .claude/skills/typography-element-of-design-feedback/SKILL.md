# Typography: feedback and marking

This skill handles **feedback and marking** for typography as an element of design. Everything known about typography (definition, variables, types, relationships with the other elements and the principles, fields, production, misconceptions and worked examples) is in `references/element-knowledge.md`. The shared method is in `references/design-method.md`. Read the sections the workflow points to.

Related skills: `typography-element-of-design-teaching`, `typography-element-of-design-analysis`, `typography-element-of-design-practice`; `typography-element-of-design` for general questions about typography; the `*-principle-of-design` skills for principles.

**Hand off to specialist skills when they fit and are available:**
- choosing, pairing or justifying typefaces → `typeface-selection-rationale`
- sizes, leading, type scales, paragraph or CSS styles → `type-system-builder`
- critiquing or marking the typography in a design → `typography-critique-audit`
- defining terms (kerning, x-height, point vs pixel) → `typography-fundamentals-explainer`
- typography lesson activities and videos → `typography-teaching-activities`

Apply the language rules in `references/design-method.md` §14: Australian English, describe before interpreting, conditional language for emotional or cultural associations, and name the criterion whenever something "works".

## Workflow

1. **Read the work against its brief.** Note the audience, purpose and every touchpoint or output named, such as cups, embroidery, a phone screen or signage. Reproduction problems usually hide there.
2. **Start from what the student actually did.** Quote their words or point to the part of the design, and name the typography variable involved (see Variables in `element-knowledge.md`).
3. **Check for the patterns below** and for the misconceptions in `element-knowledge.md`.
4. **Check production and accessibility.** Use the Production and reproduction table in `element-knowledge.md` and method §13.
5. **Place the reasoning on the justification ladder** (weak → developing → strong → sophisticated; method §4). Say what link in the chain is missing.
6. **If marking:** use the rubric supplied. If none is supplied, use `marking-rubric-builder` (if available) to generate NESA-aligned criteria first, then mark against them and say they were generated. Without that skill, give feedback without a grade (method §12).
7. **Write it up:**
   - **What's working:** specific, not generic praise.
   - **What's holding it back:** 2–4 points, each quoting the student.
   - **Next steps:** one or two, phrased as a test or a design decision, in priority order.
   - **Model structure** (optional): a fill-in-the-blanks version of a top-level justification, with a note not to copy the numbers.
   - **Teacher notes:** current level, the gap to the top, and a conferencing question.

Keep the tone warm, honest and specific.

## Feedback patterns to look for in student work

- **Too many typefaces or styles** — no clear system.
- **Hierarchy by size only,** or so many levels that none stands out.
- **Personality stereotypes** ("serif = old", "script = feminine") as the whole justification.
- **Long passages in caps, script or light weights.**
- **Poor contrast** — pale text, text over busy images.
- **Kerning problems** in logos and headlines.
- **Captions and titles on screen too briefly** to read.

## Justification examples (Year 11 magazine spread)

- **Weak:** "I used this font because it looks cool."
- **Developing:** "I used a bold sans for headings so they stand out."
- **Strong:** "I used a bold sans for headings and a serif at 10 pt with 14 pt leading for body text, so headings are easy to scan and the long article is comfortable to read. That suits a school magazine that people browse, then read."
- **Sophisticated:** "I printed test pages at 9, 10 and 11 pt with 12, 14 and 16 pt leading. At 9 pt, older readers in my test group struggled; at 11 pt the article ran to a third page. 10/14 was comfortable and fitted two pages, so I chose it. I gave up a larger size for a layout that doesn't split the article."

## Common misconceptions

| Misconception | Better understanding |
|---|---|
| "Typeface and font mean the same thing." | Typeface is the family; font is a specific style or file. |
| "Serif is more readable in print, sans on screen." | Readability depends on the specific typeface, size, spacing, medium and reader. |
| "Typography is just choosing a font." | It includes hierarchy, spacing, alignment, scale and system behaviour. |
| "A brand font creates brand consistency." | Consistency needs rules for hierarchy, weights, spacing and applications. |
| "Script fonts look elegant, so use them everywhere." | Script suits short display use; long text in script is hard to read. |
