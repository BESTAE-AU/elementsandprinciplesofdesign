# Time and duration — element of design

This skill gives Claude the time-specific knowledge for teaching, analysing and designing with time and duration. The shared method — analysis progression, justification levels, Factors Affecting Design, experimentation, differentiation, feedback approach and language rules — is in `references/design-method.md`. Read the relevant section of that file when a task calls for it.

The core habit: analyse the **temporal structure**, not just the total length. Move from **when things happen, for how long, in what order and how fast** → **effect** (comprehension, emphasis, tension, rhythm) → **why that matters here** → **how well it works and what it costs**.

## What time and duration are

Time and duration govern when visual, auditory or interactive information occurs, how long it stays available, how events are sequenced and how an experience unfolds. They are central to time-based, interactive and sequential media.

Keep the terms distinct:
- **Time** — the temporal dimension itself.
- **Duration** — how long something lasts (a shot, a hold, a transition).
- **Timing** — when an event happens relative to others.
- **Speed / tempo** — rate of change.
- **Pacing** — the overall felt rhythm of a sequence.
- **Sequence** — the order of events.
- **Movement** — change of position or state over time; it uses time but isn't time itself.

## Variables

| Variable | Range | Typical effect | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Duration** | short, long, fixed, user-controlled | comprehension, emphasis, dwell | Allow enough reading and processing time |
| **Timing** | early, late, synchronous, offset | causality, response, emphasis | Match timing to user action or story beat |
| **Sequence** | linear, branching, cyclical, layered | narrative and information structure | Order can change meaning |
| **Pacing** | rapid, moderate, slow, varied | attention, tension, cognitive load | Faster isn't automatically more engaging |
| **Tempo / speed** | rate of visual or sonic change | intensity, clarity | Separate speed from duration |
| **Pause / hold** | stillness or delay | processing time, emphasis | Holds should match content complexity |
| **Transition** | cut, fade, dissolve, wipe, morph, state change | continuity, hierarchy | Purposeful, not decorative |
| **Loop / frequency** | repetition over time | rhythm, persistence | Loops can fatigue or distract |
| **Easing** | linear, ease-in, ease-out, overshoot | naturalness, weight, emphasis | Respect reduced-motion settings |
| **Synchronisation** | aligned or offset events | audiovisual coherence, feedback | Don't make meaning depend on sync alone |

## Types

- **Linear time** — fixed beginning-to-end sequence; film, video, slideshows.
- **Interactive time** — user-controlled timing and order; UI, games, installations.
- **Cyclical / looped time** — repeating sequence; motion identities, GIFs, ambient displays.
- **Simultaneous time** — several events at once; split screen, dashboards, layered motion.
- **Asynchronous time** — independent events; notifications, distributed media.
- **Represented time** — time implied in static work; storyboards, comic panels, motion blur in a photo.

## How time and duration work with the other elements

- **Line:** lines draw on, trace and reveal; the speed of a line's growth sets pace.
- **Direction:** entry side, travel path, exit and consistent screen direction across cuts.
- **Shape:** shapes morph, mask and transition; recognition must survive the change.
- **Form:** turntables, camera moves round 3D forms, products that unfold.
- **Space:** motion needs room — entry and exit space; pauses are temporal "space" that let content breathe.
- **Size and scale:** zooms, scale-ups and shot-size changes over time.
- **Colour:** colour changes through a sequence (grade shifts, state colours); rapid flashing is an accessibility risk.
- **Value:** fades to and from black or white; light changes across a scene.
- **Texture:** grain and noise animate; compression can smear texture in fast motion.
- **Typography:** reading time is the hard constraint on titles, captions and kinetic type.
- **Layout and composition:** composition changes over time — hierarchy shifts as elements enter and leave.

## How time and duration contribute to the principles

- **Rhythm:** edit and animation rhythm — regular cuts, beats, repeated intervals.
- **Emphasis:** a hold, a slow-motion moment or a pause before a reveal.
- **Hierarchy:** order of appearance ranks information (what arrives first reads first).
- **Contrast:** a sudden change of pace (fast montage to a long still shot).
- **Unity:** consistent durations and easing across a motion system feel like one brand.
- **Movement:** timing and easing make movement read as heavy, light, mechanical or natural.
- **Tension:** delay, withholding and extended duration build suspense.
- **Balance:** alternating fast and slow sections balance a sequence.

## Time and duration across fields

- **Branding:** a motion identity specifies logo animation, entry and exit durations, easing families, transition behaviour, sequencing and tempo, with reduced-motion and static equivalents.
- **Graphic and communication design:** mostly static, but time appears in sequence (multi-panel posters, carousels, slide decks) and in how long a viewer has to read (billboards, bus sides).
- **Video, film and animation:** shot duration, edit pacing, montage, continuity, frame timing, keyframes, holds, spacing, anticipation, easing. Motion graphics coordinate entrance, hold, transition and exit so text is readable before it goes.
- **UI / UX:** response time, feedback delay, loading states, microinteraction duration, progressive disclosure and timeouts. Users should be able to pause, stop or extend time; avoid auto-advancing essential content (WCAG 2.2.2).
- **Textiles and fashion:** less central to a static textile, but relevant to wear, transformation, responsive textiles, performance costumes and production sequence.
- **Product and industrial design:** interaction feedback, cycle times, maintenance intervals, staged use. Latency changes perceived quality and safety.
- **Interiors, exhibitions and architecture:** visitor sequence, dwell time, queues, timed media, daylight change and programmed installations.
- **Technical communication:** storyboards, animatics, timelines, cue sheets and state diagrams communicate timing; a static drawing can't show duration without notation.

## Production and reproduction

| Process | Time risks |
|---|---|
| Video | Frame rate, platform length limits and compression constrain timing |
| Animation | Frame timing and render cost limit motion complexity |
| UI | Device performance and latency change intended transition timing |
| Exhibitions | Scheduling, dwell, reset time and accessibility shape timing |
| Social platforms | Autoplay without sound, loop behaviour and first-second hooks |

## Common misconceptions

| Misconception | Better understanding |
|---|---|
| "Time is just video length." | It includes sequence, timing, pauses, tempo, response and user control. |
| "Fast pacing is more engaging." | Pacing must match content, audience and processing demands. Test comprehension. |
| "Duration and speed are the same." | Duration is elapsed time; speed is rate of change. Describe both. |
| "Transitions make a video look professional." | Unmotivated transitions distract; a clean cut is often best. |
| "If I can read it, everyone can." | The maker knows the text already. Test with a first-time viewer. |

## Worked analysis (model for exemplars and annotations)

Scenario: a 30-second school open-day promo. It opens with rapid 0.5-second cuts of students in workshops, then holds for 4 seconds on a slow push-in of a student looking up from a sewing machine, then shows the date and time as kinetic type for 3 seconds before a 2-second end card.

- **Identify:** "The video uses time."
- **Describe:** "The first 12 seconds contain about 24 cuts of 0.5 s each; then a 4-second single shot with a slow push-in; then 3 seconds of animated date/time text; then a 2-second end card."
- **Explain:** "The fast montage builds energy and shows variety; the sudden long hold slows the pace so the viewer settles on one student, creating emphasis before the key information."
- **Analyse:** "Time works with contrast and hierarchy: the change from fast to slow makes the held shot feel important, and placing the date straight after the hold means it arrives when attention is highest. The push-in adds movement without breaking the stillness."
- **Evaluate:** "Effective for an energetic, emotional hook on social media. However, 3 seconds for animated date, time and address text is likely too short for parents to read — especially as the text is still moving. Holding the finished text for at least 2 more seconds, or putting the details on the end card, would fix this at the cost of 2 seconds of montage."

Each level should add a link in the chain, not simply more words.

## Justification examples (Year 10 logo animation)

- **Weak:** "I made the animation fast because it looks cool."
- **Developing:** "I made the logo animate in quickly so it feels energetic."
- **Strong:** "I animated the logo in over 0.8 s with an ease-out, so it arrives quickly and settles gently, then held it for 2 s so viewers can read the name. That suits a 6-second social ad for a skate shop where people scroll fast."
- **Sophisticated:** "I compared 0.4 s, 0.8 s and 1.5 s entries with five classmates. At 0.4 s two didn't notice the logo arrive; at 1.5 s it felt slow and ate into the hold. 0.8 s with an ease-out was noticed by all five and left 2 s to read. I also made a static version for users with reduced-motion settings. I gave up some drama for readability."

## Feedback patterns to look for in student work

- **Text on screen too briefly** to read, or moving while it should be read.
- **Every shot the same length** — no pacing variation.
- **Decorative transitions** (spins, page curls) with no purpose.
- **Linear easing everywhere,** so motion feels mechanical.
- **Audio and visual out of sync,** or meaning dependent on sound alone.
- **Auto-advancing or looping content** with no pause control, or no reduced-motion option.

## Activity ideas for teaching resources

- **Read-time test:** show a title for 1, 2 and 4 seconds to classmates; record who could read it.
- **Re-edit the pace:** recut the same footage fast and slow; compare the feeling and what is understood.
- **Easing comparison:** animate one ball with linear, ease-in, ease-out and overshoot; describe the "weight".
- **Timeline notation:** storyboard a 10-second sequence with durations and transitions marked.
- **Loading state redesign (UI):** design what a user sees at 0.1 s, 1 s and 10 s of waiting.
- **Pause for effect:** find a film scene with a deliberate hold and explain why it's there.
