# Form: analysis and model answers

This skill handles **analysis and model answers** for form as an element of design. Everything known about form (definition, variables, types, relationships with the other elements and the principles, fields, production, misconceptions and worked examples) is in `references/element-knowledge.md`. The shared method is in `references/design-method.md`. Read the sections the workflow points to.

Related skills: `form-element-of-design-teaching`, `form-element-of-design-feedback`, `form-element-of-design-practice`; `form-element-of-design` for general questions about form; the `*-principle-of-design` skills for principles.

Apply the language rules in `references/design-method.md` §14: Australian English, describe before interpreting, conditional language for emotional or cultural associations, and name the criterion whenever something "works".

## Workflow

1. **Fix the artefact.** If the user gives an image or description, work from it. If you're inventing one (for an exemplar), describe it briefly first so students can picture it, with the specific form characteristics the answers will cite.
2. **Work through the progression** (method §2): identify → describe → explain → analyse (connect to communication, purpose, audience) → evaluate against a named criterion, including a limitation or trade-off.
3. **For levelled exemplars** (e.g. developing / sound / high-range), make each level add a link in the reasoning chain, not just more words. Respect any word limits. Under each, add a short note on what lifts it to the next level.
4. **Analyse relationships.** Use the relationships with other elements and principles below to go beyond the element on its own.
5. **Language:** conditional wording for emotional or cultural readings, observation before interpretation, and no "it works" without a criterion.

## Worked analysis (model for exemplars and annotations)

Scenario: a Year 12 D&T student's bamboo bike-light housing, 3D printed. The body is a tapered cylinder with a flat underside; the front edge has a 3 mm fillet; a slot void runs through the rear for a strap.

- **Identify:** "The design uses form."
- **Describe:** "A tapered cylinder with a flat base, a 3 mm fillet round the front lip and a rectangular through-slot at the rear."
- **Explain:** "The flat base stops the light rolling on the handlebar; the taper narrows the form towards the lens; the fillet softens the front edge where hands and knees might hit it."
- **Analyse:** "Form works with direction — the taper points forward, matching the light's purpose — and with space: the slot is a functional void that lets the strap pass through without extra parts. The fillet also affects production: it removes a sharp overhang at the lip, which prints more cleanly."
- **Evaluate:** "Effective for a handlebar light: stable, safe to bump and printable without supports. However, the slot wall is thin; a load test on the strap tension is needed before final printing, and thickening it would add material and print time."

Each level should add a link in the chain, not simply more words.

## Justification examples (Year 9 timber phone stand)

- **Weak:** "I made it curvy because it looks cool."
- **Developing:** "I curved the back so it looks smooth and modern."
- **Strong:** "I shaped the back support as a 70° curved slope with a 5 mm lip, so a phone rests at a readable angle without sliding. The rounded edges make it comfortable to pick up and safer on a desk used by younger siblings."
- **Sophisticated:** "I tested 60°, 70° and 80° angles with three phone sizes. At 60° the screen reflected the ceiling light; at 80° larger phones tipped forward. 70° worked for all three, so I used it. Cutting the curve on the bandsaw took longer than a straight slope, but the curve removed a sharp edge and used less timber overall."

## How form works with the other elements

- **Line:** contour and cross-contour lines describe form on paper; in 3D, edges, creases, seams and part lines are where form shows up as line.
- **Direction:** form implies direction of use — spouts pour, handles pull, a wedge leads. It also directs movement through space.
- **Shape:** each view of a form is a shape (plan, elevation, silhouette). A strong silhouette helps a product or garment be recognised.
- **Space:** form and space define each other: solid and void, positive and negative volume, clearance around an object.
- **Size and scale:** the same form reads very differently at hand, furniture and building scale; ergonomics set many dimensions.
- **Colour:** colour can flatten or emphasise form; dark colours hide surface detail, contrasting colours can split a form into parts.
- **Value:** value gradation (highlight, mid-tone, core shadow, cast shadow) is how 2D media imply form.
- **Texture:** surface texture changes how light reveals form; gloss shows curvature, matte hides it.
- **Typography:** 3D lettering, embossed or extruded type, and signage mounted on forms.
- **Layout and composition:** in product and interior design, arrangement of forms in space; in images, overlap and placement build depth.
- **Time and duration:** rotating a 3D model, turntable renders, garments in motion, forms that transform or unfold.

## How form contributes to the principles

- **Balance:** visual and physical — a heavy base looks and is more stable; asymmetric forms need counterweight.
- **Proportion:** relationships between parts (lid to body, handle to vessel), and between object and user.
- **Emphasis:** a distinctive feature form (a sculpted handle, a cantilever) becomes the focal point.
- **Unity and harmony:** a consistent radius, draft angle or detail language across a product family.
- **Contrast:** solid against void, geometric against organic, sharp against soft edges.
- **Rhythm and repetition:** repeated ribs, pleats, fins or modules.
- **Movement:** flowing, twisting or cantilevered forms can suggest movement.
- **Scale:** monumental or miniature forms relative to the human body.
