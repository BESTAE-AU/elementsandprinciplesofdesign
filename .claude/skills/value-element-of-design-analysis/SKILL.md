# Value: analysis and model answers

This skill handles **analysis and model answers** for value as an element of design. Everything known about value (definition, variables, types, relationships with the other elements and the principles, fields, production, misconceptions and worked examples) is in `references/element-knowledge.md`. The shared method is in `references/design-method.md`. Read the sections the workflow points to.

Related skills: `value-element-of-design-teaching`, `value-element-of-design-feedback`, `value-element-of-design-practice`; `value-element-of-design` for general questions about value; the `*-principle-of-design` skills for principles.

For calculating WCAG contrast ratios or auditing a whole palette, use the `colour-contrast-audit` skill if it's available; this skill covers what value does and why.

Apply the language rules in `references/design-method.md` §14: Australian English, describe before interpreting, conditional language for emotional or cultural associations, and name the criterion whenever something "works".

## Workflow

1. **Fix the artefact.** If the user gives an image or description, work from it. If you're inventing one (for an exemplar), describe it briefly first so students can picture it, with the specific value characteristics the answers will cite.
2. **Work through the progression** (method §2): identify → describe → explain → analyse (connect to communication, purpose, audience) → evaluate against a named criterion, including a limitation or trade-off.
3. **For levelled exemplars** (e.g. developing / sound / high-range), make each level add a link in the reasoning chain, not just more words. Respect any word limits. Under each, add a short note on what lifts it to the next level.
4. **Analyse relationships.** Use the relationships with other elements and principles below to go beyond the element on its own.
5. **Language:** conditional wording for emotional or cultural readings, observation before interpretation, and no "it works" without a criterion.

## Worked analysis (model for exemplars and annotations)

Scenario: a low-key product photo for a candle brand. The background is near-black; the candle is lit from the left by its own flame and a small side light; the brand name sits in pale cream text in the lower right.

- **Identify:** "The photo uses value."
- **Describe:** "Most of the frame is very dark; the brightest values are the flame and the lit left side of the candle; the right side falls into shadow; the cream text is the second-lightest area."
- **Explain:** "Because almost everything is dark, the small bright flame becomes the focal point, and the gradient across the candle shows it's a rounded form."
- **Analyse:** "Value works with form and emphasis: the light-to-shadow transition models the cylinder, and the low-key range leaves only two light areas — flame then text — so the eye moves from product to brand name. The narrow bright range may suggest warmth and intimacy in this context."
- **Evaluate:** "Effective for a premium website hero image. However, the dark shadows may block up and lose the candle's texture when printed on uncoated stock for flyers; a print-adjusted version with lifted shadows should be proofed."

Each level should add a link in the chain, not simply more words.

## Justification examples (Year 9 poster)

- **Weak:** "I used a dark background because it looks dramatic."
- **Developing:** "I used a dark background with white text so the words stand out."
- **Strong:** "I used a near-black background with white headline text, giving very high value contrast, so the headline is readable from across the canteen. That suits a noticeboard poster that students pass quickly."
- **Sophisticated:** "I printed the poster in colour and in greyscale. In greyscale the orange subheading and red date merged into the same mid-grey, so I lightened the orange to create a clear value step. The headline's white-on-black contrast worked in both. The orange is now less saturated than I wanted, but the hierarchy survives photocopying, which the school office uses."

## How value works with the other elements

- **Line:** a line is only visible through value contrast with its ground; hatching density creates tone.
- **Direction:** a light-to-dark gradient creates a reading direction; the brightest point often ends the eye's path.
- **Shape:** value contrast is usually what separates a shape from its background.
- **Form:** highlight, mid-tone, core shadow, reflected light and cast shadow imply 3D form.
- **Space:** atmospheric perspective — distant things lighter and lower in contrast — builds depth.
- **Size and scale:** small elements (thin text, icons) need more value contrast than large ones.
- **Colour:** every colour has a value; palettes that look varied in hue can collapse in greyscale if the values match.
- **Texture:** texture is revealed by light and shadow on a surface; flat lighting flattens texture.
- **Typography:** text-to-background value contrast is the main driver of legibility.
- **Layout and composition:** the overall value pattern (where the big light and dark masses fall) structures the whole design before detail is seen.
- **Time and duration:** fades to black or white, lighting changes, and overlays moving over changing footage.

## How value contributes to the principles

- **Contrast:** value contrast is the strongest and most reliable contrast.
- **Emphasis:** the lightest light against the darkest dark usually becomes the focal point.
- **Hierarchy:** stepped values (dark headline, mid-grey body, light captions) rank information.
- **Balance:** dark masses feel heavier; a small dark area can balance a large light one.
- **Unity:** a limited tonal range ties an image or brand together.
- **Movement:** gradients and value paths move the eye.
- **Rhythm:** alternating light and dark bands.
- **Harmony:** close values feel calm and cohesive; big jumps feel dramatic — in context.
