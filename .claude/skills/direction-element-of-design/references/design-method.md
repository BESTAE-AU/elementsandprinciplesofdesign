## 1. Reasoning chain

Treat every use of an element as a decision someone made. The chain Claude follows, whether teaching, analysing or designing:

**observable characteristic → perceptual or functional effect → what it communicates → purpose it serves → fit with audience and context → how well it works, and what it costs**

Skipping links is the most common weakness in student writing ("the lines make it look modern") and in AI-generated analysis. Each claim of effect should be traceable to something you could point at. Move from observable evidence to interpretation, never the reverse.

Keep five kinds of statement apart, because they need different levels of certainty:

- **observable characteristics** — can be pointed at or measured ("the rule is 0.25 pt");
- **common perceptual associations** — often true, never guaranteed ("diagonals *can* suggest movement");
- **culturally or contextually dependent interpretations** — true for some audiences ("in this market, red *may* signal luck");
- **functional consequences** — what it lets people do or stops them doing;
- **technical or measurable properties** — what governs reproduction and performance.

The 12 elements — line, direction, shape, form, space, size and scale, time and duration, value, colour, texture, typography, layout and composition — work as one interacting system. Analyse the element in focus, but through its relationships with the others. Don't force an element into a discipline where it doesn't meaningfully apply (time in a static textile, for example); say so briefly and move on.

## 2. Analysis progression

NSW TAS command verbs build on each other. Use them in order and use them accurately.

1. **Identify** — name where the element is present (actual or implied).
2. **Describe** — state observable characteristics precisely, without interpretation: which variable, what value, where.
3. **Explain** — state the effect and the cause: *because* of this characteristic, this happens.
4. **Analyse** — show relationships: how the element works with other elements and principles, what would change if the variable changed.
   - *Connect to communication* — what it may communicate to the audience.
   - *Connect to purpose* — how it supports the intended function or message.
   - *Connect to audience and context* — why it may be appropriate here.
5. **Evaluate** — judge against the brief or explicit criteria, with evidence, and name what is gained and what is compromised.

Evaluation needs a criterion. "It works well" is not an evaluation; "it keeps the title legible at thumbnail size, which the social brief requires" is.

## 3. Characteristics, features, properties, attributes

The Multimedia syllabus uses four lenses that students often blur. Keep them distinct:

| Lens | Question it answers | Example (any element) |
|---|---|---|
| **Characteristics** | What can be observed or perceived? | weight, direction, hue, spacing, duration |
| **Features** | What does this use of the element let the design do? | separate, group, lead, identify, signal state |
| **Properties** | What measurable or technical qualities govern its behaviour? | 0.25 pt stroke, 4.5:1 contrast, 300 ms, kerf 0.2 mm |
| **Attributes** | How does it relate to audience, purpose, context and meaning? | suits young readers; matches a craft brand; readable in a moving vehicle |

## 4. Design justification

Basic chain: **decision → specific characteristic → effect → purpose → audience/context → suitability.**

Sophisticated chain adds: **evidence or testing → alternative considered → trade-off accepted → reason for the final choice.**

Quality levels (each element skill has its own worked examples; this is the pattern):

- **Weak** — preference or vague effect. "I chose it because it looks good."
- **Developing** — names a characteristic and an effect, but no purpose or audience.
- **Strong** — characteristic → effect → purpose → audience/context, specific to the brief.
- **Sophisticated** — as above, plus evidence from testing, a rejected alternative and an acknowledged trade-off.

Longer is not better. A sophisticated justification is often three or four sentences.

## 5. Factors Affecting Design

Apply only the factors that genuinely bear on the decision; forcing all fifteen into every answer produces padding.

| Factor | Question to ask about the element decision |
|---|---|
| Appropriateness | Does it suit the need, user, audience, context and environment? Would another treatment suit better? |
| Needs | What real need does it respond to? Is that need evidenced or assumed? |
| Function | What must the design do, and does this help or hinder it? |
| Aesthetics | What visual or sensory character does it create? Describe before interpreting. |
| Finance / cost | Does it change time, materials, tooling, licensing or reproduction cost? |
| Ergonomics | Does it affect comfort, reach, grip, visibility or cognitive effort? |
| WHS / safety | Could it create a hazard in making, using, maintaining or disposing? Safety outranks style. |
| Quality | Will it reproduce and perform consistently at the required standard? |
| Resources | What skills, software, equipment, materials or time does it need? |
| Environmental consequences | What are the short- and long-term waste, energy and material impacts? |
| Sustainability | Does it support durability, adaptability, repair or lower impact — without superficial claims like "minimal = sustainable"? |
| Obsolescence | Could it date quickly or become technically incompatible? |
| Life cycle | Where relevant: extraction, manufacture, distribution, use, end of life. |
| Accessibility | Can people with different sensory, physical, cognitive and technological needs use it? |
| Ethics | Could it mislead, stereotype, exclude or appropriate? |

## 6. Trade-offs

Strong work names what a decision costs as well as what it gains. Common tensions:

- simplicity ↔ expression
- visibility ↔ subtlety
- consistency ↔ variety
- novelty ↔ longevity
- quality ↔ cost
- impact ↔ accessibility
- precision ↔ hand-made character
- identical replication across media ↔ optical adjustment per medium

Derive the specific trade-off from the brief rather than listing these generically.

**Elements and principles.** Elements are the variables a designer manipulates. Principles are relationships and organisational effects: unity, harmony, balance, alignment, proximity, proportion, hierarchy, emphasis, contrast, repetition, pattern, rhythm, movement, tension, variety. An element *contributes to* a principle; it isn't the principle.

## 7. Controlled experimentation

Experiments are only informative when one thing changes at a time, and only useful when they answer a stated design question. Discourage random variation with no question behind it.

1. Make a baseline version.
2. Choose one variable from the element's variables table.
3. Make at least three clearly different versions of that variable.
4. Hold the other major variables constant.
5. Test in realistic conditions: actual size, actual medium, actual viewing distance or device.
6. Record only what visibly or functionally changed.
7. Compare against explicit criteria from the brief.
8. Get targeted feedback from users or peers if useful.
9. Select or combine, and record the trade-off.

Recording table:

| Variable changed | Version | What changed (observed) | Fit with brief | Next adjustment |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Useful methods: thumbnails, iterative sketches, mock-ups, prototypes, proofs, user testing, comparison tables, annotations. Thumbnails suit fast exploration, mock-ups suit context testing, prototypes suit functional or physical questions. Use a decision matrix only when criteria are clear enough to justify scores.

## 8. Brand-system testing

A brand decision is a rule, not a single artwork. When testing an element in a brand:

1. Keep the concept and content constant; vary only the element.
2. Test first in the mark or main graphic device.
3. Extend each surviving option to at least three touchpoints (for example packaging, social post, website header).
4. Test smallest and largest size, positive and reversed, monochrome.
5. Record what breaks when the system leaves the logo.
6. Write the result as a rule in the guidelines (permitted variants, minimum sizes, spacing, colour/value pairings, motion behaviour, examples).

## 9. Scaffolded questions

Use these as thinking prompts, adapted to the element:

- **Recognise:** Where is the element present, actual or implied? What type is it?
- **Describe:** Which characteristics can you name without interpreting? Which variable dominates?
- **Explain:** What effect does it have, and which characteristic causes it?
- **Analyse:** How does it interact with other elements and principles? What would change if you altered one variable?
- **Apply:** Which treatment best fits this brief, and what constraints shape it?
- **Justify:** What did you choose, what did you reject, and what evidence supports the choice?
- **Evaluate:** How well does it meet the criteria? Where does it perform differently across uses or users?
- **Refine:** What single change comes next, and what evidence points to it?

## 10. Building teaching resources

When producing a lesson, worksheet, workbooklet, Canvas page or scaffold, move learners through:

**knowledge → recognition → description → analysis → controlled experimentation → application → justification → evaluation → refinement**

Do not jump from a definition straight to "design your own". Keep resources **tight**, though: a single printed lesson should fit the lesson time without padding. Use one short modelled example rather than several, one stimulus to analyse (add a non-example only when the comparison is the point of the task), and cut anything a student won't actually write on or a teacher won't use. The answer key can be more generous than the student pages. In practice:

- Include examples **and non-examples**; comparison teaches faster than definition.
- Model a response before asking for one (worked example → guided → independent).
- Introduce vocabulary explicitly and reuse it.
- Connect to the student's own project where possible.
- Leave room for alternatives and testing, not just a single answer.
- Feedback prompts should point to the next design decision.

For a full workbooklet or unit, hand off to `content-booklet-builder`; for hands-on technique instruction, `practical-skill-builder`; for rubrics, `marking-rubric-builder`; for print layout, `workbooklet-style-system` — if those skills are available.

## 11. Differentiation

Same concept, different scaffolding; do not simplify the concept into something inaccurate.

- **Support:** visual identification, controlled two-way comparisons, labelled examples, sentence starters, fewer variables, partly completed tables, word bank.
- **Core:** independent analysis, controlled experiments, justified selection, application in at least two contexts.
- **Extension:** competing constraints, cross-platform systems, audience interpretation, production limits, cultural critique, trade-off analysis, evaluating alternatives with evidence.

## 12. Feedback on student work

Assessment logic runs **criteria → evidence → judgement → refinement**. Good feedback says what is working, what evidence supports that judgement, which design variable should change next, and why that change is likely to improve suitability.

1. Start from what the student actually did — quote or point to it.
2. Name the element variable involved (not just "your lines").
3. State the effect it has now, and whether that serves the brief.
4. Identify the gap in the reasoning chain (usually: no purpose, no audience, universal emotion claim, preference as reason, no testing).
5. Give **one or two** specific next actions, phrased as design decisions or tests.
6. If marking, use the rubric or marking criteria supplied and map the evidence to them. **If no rubric is supplied**, use the `marking-rubric-builder` skill (if available) to generate NESA-aligned criteria for the task first, then mark against those and say that the criteria were generated. If that skill isn't available, give the feedback without a grade and say what criteria are needed. Never invent band descriptors on the fly.

For a whole design (poster, slide, page, screen), a professional review also checks, where relevant: visual hierarchy, layout and spacing, clarity of message, consistency, readability and contrast, accessibility, fit for purpose, and issues specific to the format or platform. Finish with actionable refinements.

Keep the tone warm and specific. Praise should also be specific ("the taper on the lower stroke leads straight to the price"), not generic.

## 13. Checks that apply to every element

**Accessibility** — build it into the decision, not after it.
- Is essential information carried by more than one cue (not colour alone, not a hairline alone)?
- Does it survive zoom, reduction, reflow, greyscale printing and different screens?
- Does it add unnecessary visual or cognitive load?
- Where motion is involved, can it respect reduced-motion preferences?
- For physical work: grip, reach, tactile cues, sensory comfort, safe movement.

**Production and reproduction** — don't invent universal minimum sizes, weights or tolerances. They depend on equipment, substrate, process, final size, viewing distance, material behaviour, tooling, resolution and supplier specifications. Recommend proofs and tests wherever production could change the result. For type-heavy or cross-platform work, also consider licensing, language support, available weights and widths, and how assets resize across formats.

**Sustainability** — don't assume that minimal = sustainable, fewer colours = sustainable, digital = sustainable, or natural material = sustainable. Ask whether the treatment changes materials, inks, energy, set-up waste, rejects, maintenance or product life, and whether a trend-driven choice will force early redesign. Weigh environmental, social and economic effects.

**Culture and ethics** — separate common associations from culturally specific meanings; do not treat Western reading direction or symbolism as universal; where a design draws on a specific culture, recommend research with or about that community rather than guessing.

## 14. Language rules

- Australian English.
- Describe before interpreting.
- Use conditional language for emotional, cultural and perceptual associations: *may suggest*, *can be read as*, *in this context*.
- Never present preference as justification. Avoid "looks good", "makes it pop", unsupported aesthetic judgements and emotion claims stated as universal facts.
- Prefer precise, concise student language to inflated responses.
- Name the criterion when saying something "works".
- Keep elements (variables) and principles (relationships) distinct: an element *contributes to* a principle; it does not automatically create it.
- No body-value judgements or stereotypes.
- Preferred verbs: contributes to, reinforces, establishes, directs, emphasises, separates, groups, connects, differentiates, compromises, obscures.

## 15. Final check

Before handing over any resource, analysis or feedback:

- Claims of effect trace back to an observable characteristic.
- Variables are named precisely.
- Relevant (not all) Factors Affecting Design are used.
- Accessibility and production/reproduction are considered where they matter.
- Justifications include purpose, audience/context and suitability; sophisticated ones include evidence, an alternative and a trade-off.
- Branding is treated as a system across touchpoints; distinctive brand assets can exist beyond the logo.
- Sustainability and cultural claims are evidence-based and not universalised.
- Trade-offs are explicit and refinement actions are specific.
- Static and time-based uses are distinguished where relevant.
- Australian English throughout.
