# Colour: analysis and model answers

## Workflow

1. **Fix the artefact.** If the user gives an image or description, work from it. If you're inventing one (for an exemplar), describe it briefly first so students can picture it, with the specific colour characteristics the answers will cite.
2. **Work through the progression** (method §2): identify → describe → explain → analyse (connect to communication, purpose, audience) → evaluate against a named criterion, including a limitation or trade-off.
3. **For levelled exemplars** (e.g. developing / sound / high-range), make each level add a link in the reasoning chain, not just more words. Respect any word limits. Under each, add a short note on what lifts it to the next level.
4. **Analyse relationships.** Use the relationships with other elements and principles below to go beyond the element on its own.
5. **Language:** conditional wording for emotional or cultural readings, observation before interpretation, and no "it works" without a criterion.

## Worked analysis (model for exemplars and annotations)

Scenario: a poster for a climate-action rally. The background is a muted grey-blue; a large photo of a dry, cracked paddock is desaturated; the only saturated colour is a small orange sun that doubles as the "o" in "Now", and the date sits in the same orange.

- **Identify:** "The poster uses colour."
- **Describe:** "A muted grey-blue background, a desaturated photo, and one saturated orange used only for the 'o' in 'Now' and the date. Orange covers well under 5% of the poster."
- **Explain:** "Because everything else is muted, the small orange areas become the focal points; the eye goes from the sun-'o' to the date."
- **Analyse:** "Colour works with proportion and emphasis: a tiny accent dominates because of saturation contrast, not size. Repeating the orange links the headline and the date. In this context the orange sun against a drained landscape may suggest heat and urgency."
- **Evaluate:** "Effective for grabbing attention on a busy wall and leading to the key information. However, the orange date on grey-blue is close in value; in greyscale photocopies and for some viewers with colour-vision differences it may be hard to read. Darkening the background behind the date or bolding the date would help."

Each level should add a link in the chain, not simply more words.

## Justification examples (Year 11 café brand)

- **Weak:** "I chose green because it's my favourite colour."
- **Developing:** "I chose green because it looks natural and fresh."
- **Strong:** "I chose a deep sage green as the main colour and a warm cream background, with terracotta as a small accent for prices and buttons. The muted greens may suit a plant-based café's calm, natural positioning, and the accent makes key information easy to find."
- **Sophisticated:** "I tested three greens on the takeaway cup, website and embroidered apron. The bright green matched my mood board on screen but went dull printed on kraft cups; the dark green lost detail in embroidery. Sage held up across all three, so I made it primary. I checked sage text on cream and it failed contrast for body text, so sage is only for large headings, with charcoal for body. I gave up some colourfulness for consistency and readability."

## How colour works with the other elements

- **Line:** thin coloured lines lose contrast fast; colour-coded lines need a second cue.
- **Direction:** a saturated accent at the end of a path is where the eye lands; gradients suggest travel.
- **Shape:** colour can define a shape with no outline; a more saturated shape looks heavier than an equal-sized muted one.
- **Form:** colour can flatten or emphasise form; contrasting colours can split one form into parts.
- **Space:** colour fields divide space; in some contexts warm colours may appear to advance and cool ones recede.
- **Size and scale:** small areas look less saturated; large areas look more intense than the swatch.
- **Value:** every colour has a value; a varied palette can collapse in greyscale if values match.
- **Texture:** surface texture and finish change perceived colour (matte vs gloss, fibre vs vinyl).
- **Typography:** text colour must hold value contrast with the background; coloured type at small sizes loses legibility first.
- **Layout and composition:** proportion rules (e.g. a dominant, secondary and accent ratio) structure the page.
- **Time and duration:** colour shifts in grading, state changes, transitions; avoid rapid flashing.

## How colour contributes to the principles

- **Emphasis:** a single saturated accent in a muted field creates a focal point.
- **Contrast:** hue, value and saturation contrast — value contrast is usually the most reliable.
- **Hierarchy:** colour roles (primary action, secondary, text, background) rank information.
- **Unity and harmony:** a limited, consistent palette ties a brand or series together.
- **Balance:** small areas of intense colour balance large areas of muted colour.
- **Rhythm and repetition:** recurring colours link parts of a layout or a campaign.
- **Variety:** controlled accent colours within a consistent system.
- **Proportion:** the ratio of each colour sets the overall reading.
