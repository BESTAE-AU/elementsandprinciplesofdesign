# Descriptor quality, feedback and other rubric types

Extra guidance for writing, auditing and using rubrics. The school grade scale and the matrix-to-vertical workflow in `SKILL.md` always take priority.

## Writing descriptors

- A rubric describes **quality**; a checklist records completion. Completion counts can be requirements but don't measure sophistication.
- Each criterion assesses one coherent quality (or a justified group).
- Keep the same dimensions across every level (for example relevance, precision, development, evidence, independence, integration) and increase their precision, development and integration up the scale. Don't change what is being judged midway along a row.
- Replace bare adjectives with observable evidence. "Uses relevant testing evidence to justify a refinement" is assessable; "shows good evaluation" isn't.
- Weight presentation and language accuracy only as far as the outcome justifies.

**Worked audit (justification of equipment selection):** "poor / fair / good / very good / excellent" tells a marker nothing. Stable dimensions work better: relevance to the technique; explanation of capability; link to audience and purpose; evidence or comparison; awareness of limitation. A sound response explains why stable support enables the chosen shot. A more developed one compares feasible supports, uses trial evidence and explains the portability–stability trade-off.

## Avoiding overlap

In Textiles, appropriate design (aesthetic and functional suitability), creativity (meaningful originality) and manufacture (technical execution) are distinct. Don't award the same decorative choice three times unless distinct evidence supports each.

## Rubric audit checklist

Alignment to what was taught and asked; vague descriptors; uneven steps between levels; overlapping criteria; hidden expectations; missing evidence; bias and deficit language; language and cultural load; accessibility; whether presentation is overvalued; whether weighting matches importance; excessive complexity.

## Borderline decisions and moderation

Identify which evidence meets the higher level and what is still at the lower level, rather than averaging adjectives. Record the rationale. If the rubric can't handle a common type of response, clarify it before marking everything; don't invent a rule per student. Add marker notes on expected evidence, boundary decisions, assistance and missing components. A total must trace to criterion judgements.

## Feedback banks and student-facing versions

- Each comment is criterion-specific: the strength, the evidence, the gap, and a concrete next step that says what to develop **and how**.
- Example. Weak: "Add more detail." Stronger: "Your material selection gives a sound link to end use. Strengthen the justification by comparing the tested sample with one alternative, explaining the durability–weight trade-off and saying why your choice best meets your criteria."
- Triage: first fix a misunderstanding that blocks the task, then an evidence or process gap, then precision and presentation.
- Don't promise a higher mark for one change. If a current level is authorised and evidenced, state it with the school's grade word; otherwise call it provisional.
- Student-friendly versions use "I can" statements and success criteria without weakening the standard.
- AI-drafted comments must be checked against the actual work: no invented evidence, no claims about pages that weren't seen.

## Other rubric types

- **Analytic** for separate criteria, **holistic** for an overall judgement, **single-point** (expected standard plus space for strengths and next steps) for light formative feedback.
- **University work:** use High Distinction, Distinction, Credit, Pass, Fail when asked. Roughly: Pass meets basic requirements; Credit is clearer and better supported; Distinction is well developed and justified; High Distinction is precise, integrated, critical and evidence-based, showing synthesis or independent judgement. Use the institution's standards when supplied, and never guarantee a grade.
- **Reflective writing:** distinguish description, reflection (learning and future practice) and reflexivity (assumptions, positionality, effects on others). Reward analysis and responsibility, not confession alone.
- **Argument and critical thinking:** claim, evidence, reasoning, use of theory, alternatives, counterargument, justified conclusion.
- **Ethical reasoning:** issues, values and principles, perspectives, consequences, complexity, justified action. An unconventional conclusion isn't weak if the reasoning is sound and the task allows it.
- University theorists named in Britt's earlier GPT instructions (Kuhn for argumentation, Freire for dialogue and agency, Haynes for collaborative inquiry, Cam and Rachels for ethics) are orientations only. Check the actual texts before attributing a formal model.

## Academic integrity

Don't write submit-ready graded answers for students. Give planning support, guiding questions, structure options, feedback, checklists and non-submit-ready examples.
