# Fashion and costume design

Design variables, costume requirements, development, sustainability, assessment and justification.

## Fashion, costume and textile product design

Fashion design considers dress, identity, market, wear and production. Costume design supports character, performance, setting or concept. Textile product design can extend beyond garments to furnishings, accessories and functional products. Distinguish wearer, purchaser and audience: their needs may differ.

**Inspiration → concept → wearer/audience/end use → aesthetic and functional criteria → alternatives → fit/material trials → construction → evaluation.**

## Design variables

Silhouette is the overall outline. Proportion concerns relationships among parts. Line can direct attention or establish construction; colour, texture and pattern influence interpretation. Details include seams, openings, sleeves, collars, pockets, trims and fastenings. Each detail can serve aesthetic and functional purposes simultaneously.

Fit is suitability to body and intended silhouette; it is not necessarily tightness. Ease may support movement or a desired form. Anthropometric measurements are individual and task-dependent; avoid using one body size as a universal norm.

## Costume-specific requirements

Consider movement, visibility, dressing time, quick changes, attachment security, heat, weight, laundering, performance lighting and repeated use. A historically inspired garment need not claim historical accuracy without research. Stage lighting can alter colour and visibility; test when relevant.

Inspiration should be transformed through analysis rather than copied. Explain which visual or conceptual features were adapted and how they support the intended character or concept. Cultural references require authority, context and respect.

## Development and production

Use annotated alternatives, fabric trials, toiles or mock-ups, fitting records and comparison against criteria. A toile tests geometry and fit but may not predict the behaviour of a very different final fabric. Identify what the prototype can and cannot establish.

Production planning links pattern, fabric, construction and equipment. Consider seam accessibility, bulk, support, fastening and finishing early. Piping, boning, interfacing and decorative treatments may affect comfort or flexibility as well as appearance.

## Sustainability and ethics

Investigate durability, care, repair, adaptability, waste, sourcing and labour. Upcycling is meaningful when the resulting product is usable and avoids unnecessary additional impacts; novelty alone is insufficient. Inclusive design should consider size diversity, sensory preferences, mobility and dressing access without stereotypes.

## Assessment distinctions

Appropriate design assesses relevance to purpose and aesthetic/functional needs. Creativity concerns originality or meaningful adaptation. Manufacture proficiency concerns technical execution. Keep these criteria distinct to avoid double counting the same evidence.

A beautiful finish does not prove strong design reasoning; a well-justified concept can still have technical limitations. Assess each using observable evidence.

## Teaching and justification

Support with a wearer profile, small sample bank and annotated design template. Extend with conflicting performance needs, weighted comparisons and alternative construction systems.

Weak: "I chose satin because it looks fancy." Stronger: "The selected satin's surface sheen reinforces the formal character under the intended lighting, while sample trials showed the required drape; its tendency to fray requires an enclosed finish." Refer to the actual fabric rather than universal satin properties.
