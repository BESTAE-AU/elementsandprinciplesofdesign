# Textiles: materials and properties

Fibre to fabric, structure and properties, selection and testing, surface design, sustainability.

## Fibre → yarn → fabric → performance

Textile performance results from fibre properties, yarn construction, fabric structure, finishing and use conditions. Fibre identity alone does not determine suitability. Cotton jersey and cotton canvas share a fibre but differ substantially in stretch, drape and construction behaviour.

Natural fibres include plant and animal sources. Manufactured fibres include regenerated cellulosic and synthetic fibres. Blends combine fibres for particular properties; they may also complicate recycling. Continuous filaments and staple fibres influence yarn behaviour alongside twist and yarn size.

## Structure and properties

| Area | Variables | Likely relevance |
|---|---|---|
| Woven fabrics | Plain, twill, satin and derived structures | Stability, surface, wear and drape |
| Knitted fabrics | Loop structure, direction and density | Stretch, recovery, comfort and construction |
| Non-wovens | Fibre bonding or entanglement | Specialised stability, absorbency or filtration |
| Yarn | Fibre, twist, size, ply, texture | Strength, handle, surface and durability |
| Finishes | Mechanical or chemical treatments | Appearance, care, shrinkage, repellency and performance |
| Fabric properties | Mass, thickness, strength, stretch, recovery, absorbency, permeability | Suitability for intended use |

Stretch is extensibility; recovery is return after extension. Absorbency differs from wicking and water resistance. Breathability needs a defined meaning: air permeability and moisture-vapour transfer are not interchangeable. Drape concerns hanging behaviour, not softness alone.

Grain direction and bias affect shape and stretch. Nap and directional print affect layout, quantities and appearance. Colourfastness concerns resistance to specified exposure or transfer; wash, light and rubbing fastness can differ.

## Selection and testing

**End use → required performance → candidate fabric → evidence/test → construction implications → justified selection.**

Compare actual samples rather than generic fibre claims. Possible classroom trials include stretch/recovery, dimensional change after care, seam strength comparison, abrasion observation, drape, opacity, absorbency and print response. Keep sample size, direction and conditions consistent. Classroom trials demonstrate comparative evidence; do not label them as certified standard tests without the actual method and equipment.

Example: a costume sleeve must permit overhead movement. Test fabric stretch, pattern geometry and seam extensibility together. Stretch fabric alone cannot compensate for an unsuitable pattern or a seam that breaks under extension.

## Surface design and technology

Dyeing introduces colour into fibre/yarn/fabric; printing applies a pattern or image to selected areas. Embroidery adds stitched structure; appliqué attaches material shapes. Technique suitability depends on substrate, finish, stability, heat tolerance and care.

Digital textile technologies may include printing, cutting, embroidery and heat-transfer workflows. Smart textiles can incorporate sensing, conductive pathways or responsive materials. Claims of sustainability or innovation require evidence of benefit, durability and realistic use.

## Sustainability and pedagogy

Assess sourcing, production, dyeing, use, washing, durability, repair and end of life. Natural is not automatically sustainable, and recycled does not automatically mean recyclable.

Teach with labelled swatches, visible structure, handling and controlled tests. Support with a property bank and two candidates. Extend with conflicting requirements and comparison of finish effects. Assess **property → performance → end use → appropriateness**, including limitations and care consequences.
