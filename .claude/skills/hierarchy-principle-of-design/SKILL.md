# Hierarchy — principle of design

This skill covers **hierarchy** as a principle of design. Principles describe relationships and organisational effects; elements (line, direction, shape, form, space, size and scale, time and duration, value, colour, texture, typography, layout and composition) are the variables that create them. So always explain *which elements* produce hierarchy and *how*, and never treat an element and a principle as the same thing.

The shared method (analysis progression, justification levels, Factors Affecting Design, experimentation, teaching resources, feedback and marking, and language rules) is in `references/design-method.md`. ## What hierarchy is

Hierarchy is the ordering of elements by visual importance, so the audience meets information in the intended sequence: first, second, third.

Keep it distinct:
- **Hierarchy vs emphasis:** emphasis creates the top of the hierarchy; hierarchy orders everything.
- **Visual hierarchy vs reading order:** hierarchy is perceived importance; reading order is the path actually taken. They should agree.
- **Hierarchy vs size:** size is one lever, not the whole principle.

## Types and variations

- **Typographic hierarchy:** title, heading, subheading, body, caption.
- **Layout hierarchy:** dominant image or block, then supporting content.
- **Interface hierarchy:** primary, secondary and tertiary actions.
- **Spatial hierarchy:** in rooms and signage, what is seen first on entry.
- **Temporal hierarchy:** what appears first in a sequence.

## Levers: what a designer changes to create hierarchy

- Size and scale
- Weight
- Colour and value contrast
- Position
- Space around
- Grouping
- Order in time

- **Colour:** colour roles (primary action, secondary, text, background) rank information.
- **Direction:** a clear primary path, then secondary paths, sets the order information is met.
- **Layout and composition:** placement, scale and isolation create a clear first, second and third read.
- **Line:** a weight system (heavy section rules, light item dividers; thick visible outlines, thin hidden lines in technical drawing) ranks information.
- **Shape:** container shapes (cards, panels, badges) signal levels of information.
- **Size and scale:** the most direct link — relative size ranks information.
- **Space:** more space around an item raises its importance; tight clusters read as secondary detail.
- **Texture:** plain areas often carry key information, textured areas support mood.
- **Time and duration:** order of appearance ranks information (what arrives first reads first).
- **Typography:** size, weight, colour, case and spacing rank headings, body and captions — best with more than one cue.
- **Value:** stepped values (dark headline, mid-grey body, light captions) rank information.

## Common misconceptions

| Misconception | Better understanding |
|---|---|
| "Bigger = more important." | Size helps, but weight, contrast, position and space also rank information. |
| "More levels = more organised." | Too many levels blur together; three or four clear levels usually work better. |
| "Hierarchy is only for text." | Images, buttons, products and spaces all have hierarchy. |

## Worked analysis (model for exemplars and annotations)

Scenario: A school excursion permission note: the event name in a bold 24 pt heading, the date and cost in an 14 pt bold box, body text at 11 pt, and the return slip at the bottom separated by a dashed line.

- **Identify:** "The design uses hierarchy."
- **Describe:** "Three clear typographic levels plus a boxed 'key facts' block; the return slip is separated by a dashed line and space."
- **Explain:** "Parents see what the event is, then the date and cost, then the details, and the slip is clearly separate."
- **Analyse:** "Hierarchy uses size, weight, grouping (the box) and space. The box groups the facts parents most need, so they don't have to hunt through body text."
- **Evaluate:** "Effective for busy parents skimming. However, the due date for the slip is buried in body text; moving it into the key-facts box would put it at the right level."

Each level should add a link in the chain, not simply more words.

## Justification examples

- **Weak:** "I made the title big."
- **Developing:** "I made the heading bigger than the body text so people read it first."
- **Strong:** "I used three levels (bold 24 pt heading, a key-facts box, 11 pt body) so parents see the event, then the date and cost, then the details."
- **Sophisticated:** "In a skim test, 4 of 6 parents couldn't find the slip due date. I moved it into the key-facts box, which made the box longer but let 6 of 6 find it. I accepted a slightly busier box for a clearer priority."

## Feedback patterns to look for in student work

- **Flat hierarchy** — everything the same size and weight.
- **Too many levels** that blur together.
- **Priority mismatch** — the most important info isn't at the top level.
- **Hierarchy by size only.**
- **UI with several 'primary' buttons.**

## Activity ideas for teaching resources

- **Squint test:** blur a page and list the first three reads.
- **Rank then design:** list information by priority before laying it out.
- **One family, three levels:** create hierarchy with one typeface only.
- **Button audit (UI):** label each button primary, secondary or tertiary.
