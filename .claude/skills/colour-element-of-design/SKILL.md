# Colour — element of design

This skill gives Claude the colour knowledge needed to teach and analyse colour **as an element of design** — how it works with the other elements and principles and how to reason about it with NSW TAS students. The shared method — analysis progression, justification levels, Factors Affecting Design, experimentation, differentiation, feedback approach and language rules — is in `references/design-method.md`.

## What colour is

Colour is a perceptual and technical element described by hue, saturation (chroma) and value (lightness), produced through light, pigments, dyes, inks, materials and displays. Its appearance and meaning depend on context, surrounding colours, lighting, medium, culture and audience.

Keep the terms distinct:
- **Hue** — the colour family (red, blue…).
- **Saturation / chroma** — colourfulness, from muted to vivid.
- **Value / lightness** — how light or dark (see `value-element-of-design`).
- **Colour contrast** — a relationship between colours, not a property of one.
- **RGB** — additive light for screens; **CMYK** — subtractive process ink for print; **HEX** — a digital notation, not a guarantee of how it will look.

## Variables

| Variable | Range | Typical effect | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hue** | the colour wheel | differentiates and categorises | Meaning is contextual and cultural |
| **Saturation** | muted ↔ vivid | intensity, prominence | Vivid isn't automatically more accessible |
| **Value** | light ↔ dark | hierarchy, contrast | Test separately from hue |
| **Tint / shade / tone** | added white, black or grey | extends palette relationships | Terms vary; define them when teaching |
| **Temperature** | warmer ↔ cooler | relative colour interaction | Temperature is relative, not absolute |
| **Proportion / dominance** | how much of each colour | hierarchy, overall reading | A small accent can dominate |
| **Contrast** | of value, hue, saturation, complement | separation, emphasis | Check accessibility |
| **Interaction** | simultaneous contrast, surrounding colour | changes perceived colour | Judge colours in place, not as swatches |
| **Medium / lighting** | screen, ink, dye, material, light source | changes appearance and gamut | Proof and colour-manage |

## Types (colour relationships)

- **Monochromatic** — variations of one hue; controlled systems, tonal studies.
- **Analogous** — neighbouring hues; related, cohesive palettes.
- **Complementary** — opposite hues on a colour-wheel model; strong hue contrast (depends on value and saturation).
- **Split complementary** — one hue plus the two neighbours of its complement; contrast with more flexibility.
- **Triadic / tetradic** — three or four spaced hues; complex palettes that need a clear dominant.
- **Achromatic** — black, white and greys; value-driven systems.
- **Neutral + accent** — low-chroma base with one or two accents; common in brand and UI systems.

## How colour works with the other elements

- **Line:** thin coloured lines lose contrast fast; colour-coded lines need a second cue.
- **Direction:** a saturated accent at the end of a path is where the eye lands; gradients suggest travel.
- **Shape:** colour can define a shape with no outline; a more saturated shape looks heavier than an equal-sized muted one.
- **Form:** colour can flatten or emphasise form; contrasting colours can split one form into parts.
- **Space:** colour fields divide space; in some contexts warm colours may appear to advance and cool ones recede.
- **Size and scale:** small areas look less saturated; large areas look more intense than the swatch.
- **Value:** every colour has a value; a varied palette can collapse in greyscale if values match.
- **Texture:** surface texture and finish change perceived colour (matte vs gloss, fibre vs vinyl).
- **Typography:** text colour must hold value contrast with the background; coloured type at small sizes loses legibility first.
- **Layout and composition:** proportion rules (e.g. a dominant, secondary and accent ratio) structure the page.
- **Time and duration:** colour shifts in grading, state changes, transitions; avoid rapid flashing.

## How colour contributes to the principles

- **Emphasis:** a single saturated accent in a muted field creates a focal point.
- **Contrast:** hue, value and saturation contrast — value contrast is usually the most reliable.
- **Hierarchy:** colour roles (primary action, secondary, text, background) rank information.
- **Unity and harmony:** a limited, consistent palette ties a brand or series together.
- **Balance:** small areas of intense colour balance large areas of muted colour.
- **Rhythm and repetition:** recurring colours link parts of a layout or a campaign.
- **Variety:** controlled accent colours within a consistent system.
- **Proportion:** the ratio of each colour sets the overall reading.

## Colour across fields

- **Branding:** a brand colour system is more than one signature hue: primary, secondary, supporting and semantic colours; accessible pairings; logo colour versions; background rules; digital and print specs (RGB/HEX, CMYK, Pantone); material and finish references; and proportion across touchpoints.
- **Graphic and communication design:** colour organises hierarchy, grouping, navigation and data. Ask what information becomes easier or harder to find.
- **Photography, film and animation:** lighting, white balance, production design and grading create colour. Keep palette continuity across frames and displays; don't let colour changes carry essential meaning alone.
- **UI / UX:** semantic colour for status (error, success, warning) must be paired with text, icon or shape. Test contrast, light and dark modes, colour-vision differences, hover/focus/disabled states and charts.
- **Textiles and fashion:** colour depends on fibre, yarn, dye class, print method, surface and light; it shifts between materials and batches. Pattern scale changes colour proportion.
- **Product and industrial design:** material colour, coatings, gloss and texture change perceived colour. Colour can signal controls, hazards or status, subject to standards.
- **Interiors and architecture:** daylight, artificial light, reflectance and adjacency change colour; large areas look different from swatches.
- **Technical communication:** colour can code categories in diagrams and maps but must not be the only identifier; plan for greyscale printing.

## Production and reproduction

| Process | Colour risks |
|---|---|
| Screen (RGB) | Appearance varies with device, brightness, gamut and calibration |
| CMYK print | Some RGB colours are out of gamut; proof with the printer's profile |
| Spot / Pantone | Controlled matching, but stock and coating still change the result |
| Textile dye / print | Fibre chemistry, process and batch change the result |
| Embroidery / vinyl | Thread and film ranges limit exact matching |

## Common misconceptions

| Misconception | Better understanding |
|---|---|
| "Blue means calm." | Associations depend on culture, context, value, saturation and neighbours. Use conditional language. |
| "HEX guarantees the same colour everywhere." | Hardware, calibration and colour management change appearance. |
| "High saturation = high contrast." | Value contrast may still be poor. Check lightness. |
| "A brand palette is a list of colours." | It needs roles, proportions, pairings, accessibility and application rules. |
| "Colour alone can show status." | People with colour-vision differences need a second cue. |

## Worked analysis (model for exemplars and annotations)

Scenario: a poster for a climate-action rally. The background is a muted grey-blue; a large photo of a dry, cracked paddock is desaturated; the only saturated colour is a small orange sun that doubles as the "o" in "Now", and the date sits in the same orange.

- **Identify:** "The poster uses colour."
- **Describe:** "A muted grey-blue background, a desaturated photo, and one saturated orange used only for the 'o' in 'Now' and the date. Orange covers well under 5% of the poster."
- **Explain:** "Because everything else is muted, the small orange areas become the focal points; the eye goes from the sun-'o' to the date."
- **Analyse:** "Colour works with proportion and emphasis: a tiny accent dominates because of saturation contrast, not size. Repeating the orange links the headline and the date. In this context the orange sun against a drained landscape may suggest heat and urgency."
- **Evaluate:** "Effective for grabbing attention on a busy wall and leading to the key information. However, the orange date on grey-blue is close in value; in greyscale photocopies and for some viewers with colour-vision differences it may be hard to read. Darkening the background behind the date or bolding the date would help."

Each level should add a link in the chain, not simply more words.

## Justification examples (Year 11 café brand)

- **Weak:** "I chose green because it's my favourite colour."
- **Developing:** "I chose green because it looks natural and fresh."
- **Strong:** "I chose a deep sage green as the main colour and a warm cream background, with terracotta as a small accent for prices and buttons. The muted greens may suit a plant-based café's calm, natural positioning, and the accent makes key information easy to find."
- **Sophisticated:** "I tested three greens on the takeaway cup, website and embroidered apron. The bright green matched my mood board on screen but went dull printed on kraft cups; the dark green lost detail in embroidery. Sage held up across all three, so I made it primary. I checked sage text on cream and it failed contrast for body text, so sage is only for large headings, with charcoal for body. I gave up some colourfulness for consistency and readability."

## Feedback patterns to look for in student work

- **Colour meaning stated as universal** ("red means danger") without context or audience.
- **Favourite colour as the reason.**
- **No proportion plan** — every colour used equally.
- **Colour as the only cue** for status, categories or links.
- **Screen-only checking** for work that will be printed, dyed or stitched.
- **Hue and value confused** — "contrasting" colours that are the same lightness.

## Activity ideas for teaching resources

- **Same colour, different surround:** place one grey square on four backgrounds; describe how it changes.
- **Greyscale check:** convert a student palette to greyscale; does the hierarchy survive?
- **Proportion swap:** apply the same three colours at 60/30/10, 10/30/60 and equal amounts; compare.
- **Brand audit:** map a real brand's colour roles across five touchpoints.
- **Print vs screen:** print a screen palette on plain and kraft paper; record shifts.
- **Culture check:** research what one colour signals in three cultures relevant to the class.
