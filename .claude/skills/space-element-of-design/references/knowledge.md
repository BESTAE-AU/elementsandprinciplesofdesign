# Space: knowledge

The definition and variables are in `../SKILL.md`.

## Types

- **Positive space** — area occupied by content: figures, text, objects.
- **Negative space** — area around, between or within content; logos, layouts, interiors.
- **White space** — deliberately unoccupied layout space; editorial, UI, branding.
- **2D space** — compositional area on a flat surface; print, screens.
- **3D physical space** — volume people and objects occupy and move through; interiors, products, garments.
- **Implied space** — depth represented in 2D; photography, drawing, film.
- **Active / passive space** — space that shapes the composition vs background space that supports it.

## How space works with the other elements

- **Line:** rules and borders divide space; often extra space groups better than an extra line.
- **Direction:** open space ahead of a subject (lead room) lets implied movement continue; cramped space ahead creates tension.
- **Shape:** figure-ground — negative space between shapes forms shapes of its own.
- **Form:** solids and voids define each other; clearance round a form is designed space.
- **Size and scale:** a small element surrounded by generous space can dominate a larger crowded one; scale in depth creates implied space.
- **Colour:** colour fields can divide space without lines; warm/advancing and cool/receding readings can suggest depth in some contexts.
- **Value:** atmospheric perspective (lighter, lower-contrast distance) builds implied depth.
- **Texture:** detailed texture in the foreground and smoother texture behind adds depth.
- **Typography:** letter-spacing, word spacing, leading and margins are spatial decisions that control readability.
- **Layout and composition:** space is the raw material of layout — grids, margins and gutters are space systems.
- **Time and duration:** motion graphics need room for entry, exit and changing hierarchy; pauses are temporal "space".

## How space contributes to the principles

- **Proximity:** the most direct link — close spacing groups, larger gaps separate.
- **Hierarchy:** more space around an item raises its importance; tight clusters read as secondary detail.
- **Emphasis:** isolation in space creates a focal point.
- **Balance:** empty areas carry visual weight; asymmetric layouts balance filled and empty regions.
- **Unity:** a consistent spacing scale ties pages, screens and templates together.
- **Rhythm and repetition:** regular gaps create steady rhythm; changing gaps create pace.
- **Contrast:** dense against open areas.
- **Alignment:** consistent margins and gutters make alignment visible.

## Space across fields

- **Branding:** clear-space rules around logos and a repeatable spacing scale across templates, packaging and interfaces. Negative-space devices can become brand assets. Consistent spatial relationships create recognition even when colour or imagery changes.
- **Graphic and communication design:** margins, gutters, grouping, density and breathing room in posters, publications, packaging and infographics. Ask what the spacing groups, separates and prioritises.
- **Photography, film and animation:** foreground/background depth, blocking, headroom, lead room and negative space. Motion needs room to move across aspect ratios.
- **UI / UX:** spacing systems (e.g. 4 or 8 px steps), padding, touch-target separation, responsive gutters and density. More space isn't automatically better — too much separation breaks grouping and adds scrolling.
- **Textiles and fashion:** motif spacing, cut-outs, lace and open structures, ease, and volume around the body. Ease is functional 3D space, not visual white space.
- **Product and industrial design:** clearance for fingers, tools, moving parts, ventilation and maintenance; negative space can remove material or form handles but can weaken structure.
- **Interiors and architecture:** circulation, zoning, doorway clearance, furniture spacing and accessible paths. Evaluate dimensions, flow, occupancy and sensory load.
- **Technical communication:** dimension and annotation spacing, drawing margins and white space affect legibility; plans and sections show real spatial relationships at scale.

## Production and reproduction

| Process | Space risks |
|---|---|
| Print | Bleed, trim, safe area, margins and binding reduce usable space |
| Responsive UI | Spacing must reflow across breakpoints without collapsing hierarchy |
| Cutting / fabrication | Minimum gaps and bridges between cut features depend on the process |
| Textiles | Pattern layout, seam allowance and ease are spatial constraints with material cost |

## Common misconceptions

| Misconception | Better understanding |
|---|---|
| "White space must be white." | It means intentionally unoccupied space, whatever the colour. |
| "More space always looks better." | Too much separation weakens grouping or wastes limited space. Judge against task and medium. |
| "Negative space is unused." | It shapes recognition, hierarchy, access and function. |
| "Filling every gap makes it look finished." | Crowding raises cognitive load and flattens hierarchy. |
| "Spacing is just visual." | In products and rooms it's clearance, access and safety. |
