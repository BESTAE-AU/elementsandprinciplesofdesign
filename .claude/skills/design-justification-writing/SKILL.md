# Design justification writing

A justification explains **why a design decision suits its purpose, audience and context**, backed by evidence. This skill works for any element or principle. For element-specific examples, see the justification examples in each `<element>-element-of-design` skill. The shared method is in `references/design-method.md` (§4 and §6 matter most).

## The chains

**Basic:** decision → specific characteristic → effect → purpose → audience/context → suitability

**Sophisticated:** decision → evidence or testing → alternative considered → trade-off accepted → reason for the final choice

## Quality levels

| Level | What it contains | Example (school canteen poster) |
|---|---|---|
| **Weak** | Preference or vague effect | "I chose blue because I like it." |
| **Developing** | Characteristic and effect, no purpose or audience | "I used a big bold heading so it stands out." |
| **Strong** | Characteristic → effect → purpose → audience/context | "I set the special in 120 pt bold on a plain background so it's the first thing students see from the queue, which suits a busy canteen at lunch." |
| **Sophisticated** | Adds testing evidence, a rejected alternative and a trade-off | "At 80 pt, only 3 of 8 students could read the special from the back of the queue; at 120 pt all 8 could. I kept 120 pt and dropped the photo to fit it, giving up some appeal for readability." |

Longer isn't better. A sophisticated justification is often three or four sentences.

## Workflow

1. **List the decisions** worth justifying. Pick the few that matter most, not every choice.
2. **For each, name the specific characteristic** (e.g. "2 pt round-ended strokes", not "the lines").
3. **State the effect** and connect it to purpose and audience.
4. **Add evidence.** Use a test, a mock-up at real size, user feedback or a comparison. If the student hasn't tested, the next step is a test, not better wording.
5. **Name the alternative** and why it was rejected.
6. **Name the trade-off:** what was given up and why that's acceptable for this brief.
7. **Check language** (method §14): conditional wording for emotional or cultural claims, and no preference as a reason.

## Sentence frames

- "I chose ___ (specific characteristic) so that ___ (effect), because ___ (audience/context) need ___."
- "I tested ___, ___ and ___. ___ failed because ___; ___ worked because ___."
- "I rejected ___ because ___."
- "This means I gave up ___, but ___ mattered more for this brief."
- "This may be perceived as ___ by ___ because ___." (use for emotional or cultural readings)

## Turning test results into justification

| Test result | Justification move |
|---|---|
| Option failed at small size | "At ___ size, ___ disappeared, so I set a minimum of ___." |
| Users misread something | "___ of ___ users thought ___, so I changed ___." |
| Production limit found | "The supplier's minimum is ___, so I ___." |
| Two options both worked | "Both passed ___; I chose ___ because it also ___." |

## Common weaknesses

- "It looks good", "it pops" or "professional" as the whole reason.
- Universal meanings ("green = eco").
- No audience named.
- Justifying every tiny choice instead of the important ones.
- Claims of testing with no results given.
