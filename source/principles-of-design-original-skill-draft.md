# Principles of Design — Claude Code Skill

## Purpose

This skill provides Claude with a comprehensive knowledge and reasoning framework for the **Principles of Design**.

It is not simply a glossary and must not be used as a set of rigid visual rules. It is a design-thinking system that helps Claude:

- teach and explain Principles of Design
- analyse existing design work
- compare alternative solutions
- generate design concepts
- critique student and professional work
- scaffold design thinking
- support experimentation and iteration
- provide actionable feedback
- support design justification
- evaluate effectiveness
- recommend evidence-based refinement
- connect visual decisions to audience, purpose, context, function, accessibility and implementation
- reason across multiple design disciplines
- explain causal relationships between Elements and Principles of Design

The framework synthesises Technology and Applied Sciences design decision-making with Multimedia and Visual Arts approaches to visual communication, project application, visual literacy, Characteristics, Features, Properties and Attributes, HSC-style command verbs, audience, purpose, meaning, practice, experimentation, analysis and refinement.

Claude should treat Principles of Design as **relational and organisational concepts** that explain how design elements interact, are structured and are perceived.

---

# 1. Core Distinction: Elements vs Principles

Claude must maintain a clear distinction between **Elements of Design** and **Principles of Design**.

## Elements of Design

Elements are the visual, spatial, material and temporal variables a designer manipulates.

The 12 Elements in this framework are:

1. Line
2. Direction
3. Shape
4. Form
5. Space
6. Size and Scale
7. Time and Duration
8. Value
9. Colour
10. Texture
11. Typography
12. Layout and Composition

## Principles of Design

Principles describe **how Elements are organised, related, controlled, differentiated, repeated, grouped, sequenced or perceived** within a design.

The 15 Principles in this framework are:

1. Unity
2. Harmony
3. Balance
4. Alignment
5. Proximity
6. Proportion
7. Hierarchy
8. Emphasis
9. Contrast
10. Repetition
11. Pattern
12. Rhythm
13. Movement
14. Tension
15. Variety

Claude must not treat a Principle as if it were a physical object or isolated visual feature.

Prefer:

> Repeated colour, typography and spacing conventions establish unity across the brand system.

Avoid:

> Unity is an element in the poster.

---

# 2. Core Design Reasoning Model

When analysing or explaining a Principle, Claude should use this underlying sequence:

**design intention → design choices → Elements manipulated → relationship between Elements → Principle created → interaction with other Principles → perceptual effect → communication/function → audience or user experience → suitability → trade-offs → evaluation → refinement**

The model should be adapted to the specific Principle rather than applied mechanically.

Principles concern different types of relationships, including:

- similarity
- difference
- grouping
- visual weight
- spacing
- scale relationships
- dominance
- order
- sequencing
- repetition
- interval
- directional flow
- stability
- instability
- variation
- consistency
- coherence

Claude must first identify the intrinsic nature of the Principle before explaining it.

---

# 3. Principle Interaction and Causal Reasoning

Principles rarely operate independently.

Claude should reason through causal chains where evidence supports them.

Use:

**Elements manipulated → Primary Principle → Secondary Principle(s) → Perceptual effect → Communication or functional outcome**

Examples:

- **Value + size + typography → Contrast → Emphasis → Hierarchy**
- **Shape + colour + typography repetition → Repetition → Rhythm + Unity → stronger brand consistency**
- **Space + alignment + proximity → Grouping → Hierarchy → easier information processing**
- **Asymmetrical placement + opposing direction + strong scale differences → Tension → Movement + Emphasis → dramatic communication**
- **Repeated timing + recurring motion → Repetition → Rhythm → Unity → coherent motion identity**

Claude should distinguish:

### Primary Principle
The Principle most directly established by the design decision.

### Secondary Principle
A Principle strengthened, weakened, disrupted or created as a consequence.

Do not merely stack terminology.

Avoid:

> The design uses contrast, hierarchy, emphasis, balance and unity.

Prefer:

> The strong value and scale difference establishes contrast. This makes the headline the primary point of emphasis and places it at the highest level of the information hierarchy. Repeated left alignment then reconnects the contrasting information levels into a unified structure.

Not every relationship is linear. Claude should recognise **principle networks**.

For example, contrast may strengthen emphasis and hierarchy while, if overused, reducing unity or harmony.

Causal relationships are conditional, not universal.

- similarity

- figure-ground
- common region
- connectedness
- visual weight
- salience
- grouping
- focal attention
- perceptual hierarchy
- expectation
- predictability
- contrast

# 5. The 15 Principles of Design

## 5.1 Unity

### Definition

Unity is the perception that the parts of a design belong together as a coherent whole.

Unity does not require every component to be identical. It arises when sufficient relationships connect otherwise distinct elements into a recognisable system.

### Core perceptual logic

Unity relies strongly on:

- similarity
- recurrence
- consistency
- alignment
- shared structure
- visual relationships
- continuity
- controlled variation

### Why designers use it

Unity can:

- make a design feel coherent
- strengthen brand consistency
- connect related information
- support recognition
- reduce apparent fragmentation
- help multiple components behave as a system

### How Unity can be created

Common strategies include:

- repeating colour
- repeating shape
- recurring typography
- consistent image treatment
- shared grid systems
- consistent spacing
- recurring graphic devices
- alignment systems
- consistent motion behaviour
- controlled stylistic variation
- common material or finish
- coherent proportions

### Important Element relationships

| Element | Relationship to Unity |
|---|---|
| Line | Recurring line weight, direction or style can connect components. |
| Direction | Consistent directional logic can unify visual flow. |
| Shape | Recurring shapes create family resemblance. |
| Form | Related forms can establish product or spatial families. |
| Space | Consistent spacing systems strengthen structural coherence. |
| Size and Scale | Repeated scale relationships can create system consistency. |
| Time and Duration | Repeated timing conventions can unify motion systems. |
| Value | Controlled tonal relationships can connect components. |
| Colour | Consistent palettes strongly support unity and recognition. |
| Texture | Repeated material or surface treatment can create cohesion. |
| Typography | Shared type families, styles and scales are major unifying devices. |
| Layout and Composition | Grids, alignment and recurring structures strongly support unity. |

### Relationship to other Principles

- repetition frequently strengthens unity
- alignment can strengthen unity
- harmony often supports unity
- variety can coexist with unity when variation occurs within a system
- excessive contrast may weaken unity if relationships are lost
- pattern can support unity through organised recurrence
- hierarchy can differentiate content while remaining unified

### Unity vs Harmony

**Unity** asks whether the design forms a coherent whole.

**Harmony** asks whether different components have compatible visual relationships.

A design may be unified through a highly consistent system while deliberately containing strong contrast or tension.

### Branding

Unity is central to visual identity systems.

Claude should analyse unity across:

- logo use
- colour palette
- type systems
- graphic devices
- imagery
- templates
- packaging
- advertising
- social media
- websites
- signage
- merchandise
- motion behaviour

Brand guidelines often establish unity through:

- clear-space rules
- grid systems
- colour specifications
- typographic hierarchy
- imagery treatment
- icon systems
- recurring shapes
- tone of motion
- spacing tokens
- component rules

### Multimedia and time-based design

Unity can be temporal as well as spatial.

It may be created through:

- recurring transitions
- consistent motion curves
- repeated title structures
- stable colour systems
- consistent editing style
- recurring audio motifs
- coherent pacing
- shared animation behaviour

### UI/UX

Unity can improve predictability through:

- repeated components
- consistent spacing
- stable navigation
- shared interaction patterns
- consistent states
- recurring icon logic

Visual unity should not override usability. Components that perform different functions may need sufficient differentiation.

### Textiles and fashion

Unity may arise through:

- repeated motif
- consistent palette
- related textures
- coherent silhouette
- recurring seam language
- coordinated trims
- repeated proportion relationships

### Product, spatial and interior design

Unity may come from:

- product-family form language
- consistent materials
- repeated radii
- shared control logic
- repeated architectural details
- coherent lighting or material systems

### Accessibility

Unity can reduce cognitive load by making patterns predictable. However, excessive similarity can make controls or information states too difficult to distinguish.

### Trade-offs

**Possible advantage ↔ possible disadvantage**

- coherence ↔ reduced differentiation
- consistency ↔ monotony
- recognition ↔ rigidity
- predictable system ↔ insufficient flexibility

### Failure modes

- **Too little unity:** components may feel unrelated or fragmented.
- **Excessive unity:** everything may become visually equivalent.
- **Inconsistent unity:** a system may appear accidental.
- **Deliberate disruption:** can create emphasis or tension if controlled.

### Misconception

**Misconception:** Unity means everything must look the same.  
**Correction:** Unity requires coherent relationships, not complete uniformity.

## 5.2 Harmony

### Definition

Harmony is the compatible and visually coherent relationship between different elements or components.

Harmony concerns how differences coexist without producing unnecessary conflict.

### Core perceptual logic

Harmony may arise through:

- related colour
- compatible proportions
- consistent stylistic language
- related materials
- controlled contrast
- visual resonance
- repeated characteristics

### Why designers use it

Harmony can:

- make complex compositions feel considered
- support aesthetic coherence
- connect varied components
- create visual comfort
- reinforce mood or brand character

### Creation strategies

- analogous or related colour relationships
- compatible typeface pairing
- recurring form language
- material compatibility
- related proportions
- controlled image treatment
- consistent stylistic conventions
- moderated contrast

### Elements

Harmony commonly involves colour, value, shape, form, texture, typography, space and composition. Time-based harmony may also involve compatible timing, sound and motion behaviour.

### Relationships

- harmony can support unity
- contrast can exist within harmony
- variety can enrich harmony
- excessive harmony may weaken emphasis
- tension may deliberately interrupt harmony

### Harmony vs Unity

Unity concerns whole-system coherence. Harmony concerns compatibility between parts.

### Applications

In branding, harmony connects multiple brand assets without requiring identical execution.

In multimedia, visual and audio components may harmonise through pacing, tone, rhythm or mood.

In textiles, colours, materials, surfaces and silhouette may relate harmoniously.

In interiors, material palettes, lighting, furniture and spatial proportions can be compatible without being identical.

### Accessibility

Harmonious low-contrast schemes must not compromise legibility. Compatibility is not more important than functional differentiation.

### Trade-offs

- visual comfort ↔ insufficient distinction
- compatibility ↔ sameness
- subtlety ↔ reduced hierarchy
- coherence ↔ lower dramatic impact

### Failure modes

- incompatible components may create unintended conflict
- excessive harmony may become visually undifferentiated
- deliberate disharmony may create tension, urgency or disruption

### Misconception

**Misconception:** Harmony means avoiding contrast.  
**Correction:** Contrast can occur within a harmonious system when differences remain purposefully related.

## 5.3 Balance

### Definition

Balance is the distribution of visual, spatial or perceptual weight within a composition.

Balance does not require equal size or symmetry. Elements have perceived weight based on attributes such as:

- size
- value
- colour intensity
- complexity
- texture
- position
- isolation
- density
- motion

### Types

#### Symmetrical balance
Visual weight is mirrored or closely matched across an axis.

#### Asymmetrical balance
Different elements create an overall equilibrium without mirroring.

#### Radial balance
Elements distribute around a central point.

#### Approximate balance
Near-symmetry with controlled differences.

#### Dynamic balance
An active equilibrium created through unequal but counteracting forces.

### Creation

Balance may be adjusted through:

- position
- scale
- density
- negative space
- colour intensity
- tonal weight
- image complexity
- directional force
- movement
- number of components

### Visual weight

Claude should explicitly analyse *why* something appears visually heavy or light.

For example:

> The small red element carries more visual weight than its physical size suggests because of its saturation, isolation and position near the edge of the composition.

### Relationships

- symmetry is a method, not a synonym for balance
- asymmetry can strengthen movement
- reduced balance may create tension
- contrast can change perceived weight
- hierarchy may intentionally create unequal visual weight

### Applications

Branding: stable or dynamic composition across touchpoints.  
Photography: distribution of subjects, negative space and tonal mass.  
Film: changing balance as subjects enter or leave frame.  
UI: balancing information density without sacrificing hierarchy.  
Fashion: distribution of volume, colour, details and surface treatment.  
Product design: visual mass, controls and form relationships.  
Interiors: furniture, lighting, architectural mass and circulation.

### Accessibility and function

Visual balance does not necessarily equal functional balance. A visually balanced interface may still have poor navigation.

### Trade-offs

- stability ↔ predictability
- asymmetrical energy ↔ possible instability
- visual equilibrium ↔ reduced focal dominance
- strong imbalance ↔ possible confusion, or intentional tension

### Failure modes

- weak control of visual weight may make a design feel unintentionally unstable
- excessive symmetry may reduce energy
- deliberate imbalance may be effective for tension or movement

### Misconception

**Misconception:** Balance means symmetry.  
**Correction:** Symmetry is only one strategy for distributing visual weight.

---

## 5.4 Alignment

### Definition

Alignment is the deliberate positioning of elements in relation to shared edges, centres, axes, baselines, grids or spatial structures.

### Core logic

Alignment creates visible or implied relationships.

It can:

- organise information
- create continuity
- connect distant elements
- improve scanning
- reinforce hierarchy
- strengthen unity

### Types and forms

- edge alignment
- centre alignment
- baseline alignment
- grid alignment
- axis alignment
- modular alignment
- optical alignment
- deliberate misalignment

### Optical alignment

Mathematical alignment does not always appear visually aligned. Curves, pointed shapes, circular icons and letterforms may require optical adjustment.

### Elements

Alignment strongly affects typography, space, layout, line, shape, form and composition.

In motion, elements may align temporarily as part of a transition or sequence.

### Relationships

- alignment often supports unity
- alignment + proximity can establish grouping
- alignment helps clarify hierarchy
- deliberate misalignment may create emphasis, tension or movement

### Applications

Graphic design: text columns, grids, image systems.  
Branding: consistent templates and lock-ups.  
UI: component grids, labels, controls and responsive structure.  
Textiles: seam placement, motif alignment and repeat matching.  
Product design: controls, interfaces, joins and component relationships.  
Spatial design: sightlines, furniture, architecture and wayfinding.

### Accessibility

Consistent alignment can aid scanning and comprehension. Overly centred long text may reduce readability.

### Trade-offs

- order ↔ rigidity
- consistency ↔ reduced spontaneity
- precise alignment ↔ potential optical awkwardness
- disruption ↔ emphasis but possible confusion

### Misconception

**Misconception:** All elements should align to the same edge.  
**Correction:** Designs may use multiple intentional alignment systems within a coherent grid.

## 5.5 Proximity

### Definition

Proximity is the use of spatial distance to suggest relationships between elements.

Elements placed near one another are often perceived as belonging together, while greater separation can suggest different groups or functions.

### Core perceptual logic

Proximity is strongly related to perceptual grouping.

Claude should distinguish **physical distance** from **perceived grouping**, which may also be influenced by:

- colour
- enclosure
- alignment
- similarity
- connecting lines
- background regions

### Creation

- reduced spacing within a group
- increased spacing between groups
- common region
- section spacing
- clustering
- card structures
- whitespace
- separation zones

### Elements

Space is the dominant Element, but typography, layout, size, colour and line can reinforce proximity relationships.

### Relationships

- proximity supports grouping
- grouping can strengthen hierarchy
- proximity + alignment can improve information organisation
- excessive proximity can reduce clarity
- excessive separation can weaken relationships

### Applications

Editorial design: headings with related body copy.  
Branding: logo lock-ups and information groups.  
UI: labels near fields, related controls, card content.  
Infographics: grouped data.  
Packaging: related information blocks.  
Spatial design: functional zoning.  
Product design: grouping controls by function.

### Accessibility

Clear proximity can reduce cognitive load. Important label-control relationships should not depend on proximity alone when other cues are needed.

### Trade-offs

- grouping ↔ crowding
- separation ↔ fragmentation
- compactness ↔ reduced readability
- whitespace ↔ inefficient use of constrained space

### Misconception

**Misconception:** Proximity simply means putting things close together.  
**Correction:** Proximity is about using distance intentionally to communicate relationships.

## 5.6 Proportion

### Definition

Proportion is the relationship between the sizes, dimensions or quantities of parts within a whole, or between components.

Proportion differs from scale. **Scale** concerns size relative to a reference, context or expected size; **proportion** concerns size relationships among parts.

### Core logic

Proportion influences:

- visual relationships
- hierarchy
- perceived stability
- usability
- ergonomics
- emphasis
- character
- realism or stylisation

### Forms

- equal proportion
- dominant/subordinate proportion
- modular proportion
- geometric ratio systems
- human proportion
- exaggerated proportion
- progressive proportion

Claude should not imply that any mathematical ratio is automatically superior.

### Elements

Size and Scale are central. Proportion also interacts with shape, form, space, typography and composition.

### Relationships

- proportion can establish hierarchy
- exaggerated proportion may create emphasis
- disproportion can create tension
- consistent proportions can support unity
- varying proportion can create variety

### Applications

Typography: heading/body relationships.  
Branding: mark-to-type relationships.  
Fashion: silhouette, panel divisions and detail scale.  
Product design: component sizing and ergonomics.  
Interiors: furniture-to-room relationships.  
Architecture: openings, façade divisions and human scale.  
UI: control size, type scale and content relationships.

### Accessibility

Functional proportions can affect legibility, touch targets, control comprehension and ergonomics.

### Trade-offs

- dramatic scale relationships ↔ reduced balance
- consistent proportion ↔ predictability
- compact components ↔ accessibility limitations
- oversized emphasis ↔ loss of supporting information

### Misconception

**Misconception:** Good proportion means using the golden ratio.  
**Correction:** Proportion is contextual; no single ratio guarantees effective design.

## 5.7 Hierarchy

### Definition

Hierarchy is the organisation of information or components into distinguishable levels of importance, priority or sequence.

### Core perceptual logic

Hierarchy allows users to determine:

- what to notice first
- what is primary
- what is secondary
- what belongs together
- what action to take
- how to navigate information

### Hierarchy can be created through

- scale
- position
- value
- colour
- typography
- contrast
- spacing
- proximity
- alignment
- isolation
- image size
- motion
- timing
- order

### Typography and hierarchy

Claude should understand that hierarchy may be manipulated through:

- size
- weight
- width
- case
- style
- colour
- value
- line spacing
- letter spacing
- position
- indentation
- typeface
- whitespace

### Relationships

- contrast frequently establishes hierarchy
- emphasis identifies particularly important points within hierarchy
- proximity groups levels
- alignment structures levels
- proportion influences relative importance
- repetition can make hierarchy consistent across a system

### Hierarchy vs Emphasis

Hierarchy creates **multiple levels of priority**.

Emphasis makes a particular element or area especially prominent.

### Multimedia

Hierarchy can change over time. Motion, sound, timing and editing can shift the viewer's attention from one element to another.

### UI/UX

Hierarchy is critical for:

- navigation
- headings
- dashboards
- progressive disclosure
- calls to action
- form structure
- scanning
- decision pathways

### Accessibility

Clear hierarchy supports comprehension. Hierarchy should not rely on colour alone. Heading structures and semantic organisation should reinforce visual organisation where possible.

### Trade-offs

- strong priority ↔ secondary content may be overlooked
- subtle hierarchy ↔ slower comprehension
- many levels ↔ complexity
- few levels ↔ insufficient differentiation

### Failure modes

- too little hierarchy: everything competes equally
- too much hierarchy: supporting content may become insignificant
- inconsistent hierarchy: users cannot predict importance
- deliberately flattened hierarchy: may communicate equality, but can reduce navigation clarity

### Misconception

**Misconception:** Hierarchy means making the heading bigger.  
**Correction:** Scale is only one of many variables that establish hierarchy.

## 5.8 Emphasis

### Definition

Emphasis is the intentional creation of prominence so that a particular element, area, message or moment receives increased attention.

### Creation strategies

- contrast
- isolation
- scale
- colour
- value
- unusual shape
- texture
- directional cues
- position
- movement
- timing
- sound
- interruption of repetition
- negative space

### Core logic

Emphasis depends on difference relative to context.

An element is not inherently emphasised; it becomes emphasised because it differs from surrounding conditions or occupies a strategically important position.

### Relationships

- contrast often creates emphasis
- emphasis can strengthen hierarchy
- disruption of repetition can create emphasis
- isolation can create emphasis through proximity and space
- movement can create temporal emphasis

### Applications

Branding: campaign message, product feature, call to action.  
Film: subject placement, lighting, focus, sound and editing.  
Motion graphics: timed entry, scale change, motion contrast.  
UI: primary buttons, warnings, selected states.  
Fashion: focal details, colour accents, silhouette features.  
Spatial design: focal walls, lighting, architectural features.

### Accessibility

Emphasis should not rely exclusively on colour, motion or subtle contrast. Urgent information must remain perceivable.

### Trade-offs

- strong focal point ↔ supporting information may be suppressed
- multiple emphases ↔ competition
- animation emphasis ↔ distraction or motion sensitivity
- visual dominance ↔ reduced balance

### Misconception

**Misconception:** Emphasis means making something large.  
**Correction:** Emphasis can be produced through many forms of difference, including isolation, contrast, movement or interruption.

## 5.9 Contrast

### Definition

Contrast is the perceptible difference between elements or conditions.

Contrast allows components to be distinguished and can establish emphasis, hierarchy, separation, energy and clarity.

### Types

Contrast may occur through:

- value
- colour
- scale
- size
- shape
- form
- texture
- typography
- direction
- space
- density
- motion
- speed
- timing
- sound
- conceptual meaning

### Core logic

Contrast is relational.

A colour, size or typeface is not "contrasting" by itself. It contrasts **with another condition**.

### Relationships

- contrast can create emphasis
- contrast can strengthen hierarchy
- contrast can increase variety
- strong directional contrast can create tension
- excessive unrelated contrast can reduce unity

### Contrast and accessibility

Claude must distinguish **aesthetic contrast** from **functional contrast**.

Functional contrast can affect:

- text legibility
- focus states
- interface controls
- visual differentiation
- low-vision access
- colour vision accessibility

Colour difference alone may not provide sufficient luminance contrast.

### Time-based contrast

Contrast may occur between:

- stillness and movement
- slow and fast motion
- loud and quiet audio
- long and short duration
- dense and sparse sequences
- abrupt and gradual transitions

### Trade-offs

- differentiation ↔ fragmentation
- visibility ↔ visual aggression
- drama ↔ reduced harmony
- strong hierarchy ↔ weaker secondary content

### Misconception

**Misconception:** Contrast means complementary colours.  
**Correction:** Colour opposition is only one form of contrast.

## 5.10 Repetition

### Definition

Repetition is the recurrence of an element, feature, relationship or behaviour.

Repeated components can create consistency, recognition, continuity, rhythm, pattern and unity.

### Forms

- exact repetition
- varied repetition
- progressive repetition
- modular repetition
- temporal repetition

### Creation

Designers may repeat:

- shape
- colour
- line
- spacing
- type treatment
- image style
- layout structure
- material
- motif
- motion
- timing
- sound
- interface component

### Relationships

- repetition can create rhythm
- organised repetition can create pattern
- repetition can strengthen unity
- repetition supports brand consistency
- variation within repetition can create variety

### Repetition vs Pattern

Repetition is recurrence.

Pattern is an organised repeated arrangement that forms a recognisable system.

### Repetition vs Rhythm

Repetition creates recurrence. Rhythm emerges through recurrence, interval, sequence, timing and variation.

### Applications

Branding: visual identity systems.  
UI: reusable components.  
Textiles: motifs and repeats.  
Architecture: windows, bays, columns.  
Product families: recurring features.  
Motion graphics: repeated transitions or animation behaviour.

### Trade-offs

- consistency ↔ monotony
- recognition ↔ predictability
- efficiency ↔ sameness
- recurring structure ↔ insufficient emphasis

### Misconception

**Misconception:** Repetition and pattern are interchangeable.  
**Correction:** Repetition may occur without forming a recognisable pattern system.

---

## 5.11 Pattern

### Definition

Pattern is an organised system of repeated elements or relationships that produces a recognisable visual, structural or temporal arrangement.

### Core logic

Pattern requires recurrence **plus organisation**.

### Forms

- regular
- alternating
- modular
- geometric
- organic
- tessellated
- directional
- progressive
- randomised within constraints
- temporal

### Elements

Shape, line, colour, value, texture, space and time are particularly important.

### Relationships

- repetition is a foundation for pattern
- pattern can create rhythm
- pattern can strengthen unity
- variation can interrupt pattern
- a pattern break can create emphasis

### Applications

Textiles: repeat design, placement prints, engineered patterns.  
Branding: proprietary graphic systems.  
Packaging: surface identity.  
Architecture: façade modules, screens and structure.  
UI: repeated layout modules and content structures.  
Motion: looping patterns and repeated behaviours.

### Accessibility

Dense patterns may interfere with text readability or create visual discomfort. Moving repetitive patterns may affect motion-sensitive users.

### Trade-offs

- recognition ↔ visual dominance
- decorative richness ↔ clutter
- consistency ↔ monotony
- dense repetition ↔ legibility issues

### Misconception

**Misconception:** Any repeated element is a pattern.  
**Correction:** Pattern involves organised recurrence.

---

## 5.12 Rhythm

### Definition

Rhythm is the perceived beat, cadence or visual/temporal flow created by repetition, interval, sequence and variation.

### Types

- regular rhythm
- alternating rhythm
- progressive rhythm
- flowing rhythm
- random or irregular rhythm

### Core logic

Rhythm requires attention to:

- recurrence
- spacing
- interval
- timing
- progression
- variation
- sequence

### Elements

Rhythm may be created through repeated line, direction, shape, space, scale, colour, value, typography, motion, duration or audio.

### Rhythm vs Repetition

Repetition provides recurring material.

Rhythm concerns the **cadence or flow generated by that recurrence**.

### Multimedia

Rhythm is particularly important in:

- editing
- animation timing
- motion graphics
- music-image relationships
- title sequences
- pacing
- transitions

Visual rhythm may reinforce, complement or deliberately conflict with audio rhythm.

### Applications

Textiles: motif spacing and repeat cadence.  
Architecture: columns, openings, structural bays.  
Graphic design: repeated modules and spacing.  
UI: recurring content cadence.  
Film: shot duration and editing pattern.

### Trade-offs

- predictability ↔ monotony
- variation ↔ disrupted cadence
- rapid rhythm ↔ cognitive overload
- slow rhythm ↔ reduced energy

### Misconception

**Misconception:** Rhythm is simply repetition.  
**Correction:** Rhythm depends on recurrence plus interval, sequence or variation.

## 5.13 Movement

### Definition

Movement is the actual or implied progression of attention, form, subject or information through space or time.

Movement may be:

- implied in static design
- actual in animation or film
- navigational in interfaces
- physical in products or spaces
- perceptual as the eye moves through a composition

### Creation

- directional line
- diagonal structure
- scale progression
- repetition
- rhythm
- blur
- cropping
- gesture
- perspective
- directional typography
- sequencing
- animation
- transitions
- camera movement
- editing
- scrolling
- interaction

### Movement vs Direction

Direction describes orientation or pathway.

Movement describes perceived or actual progression along or through a pathway.

### Relationships

- rhythm can produce movement
- direction guides movement
- contrast can redirect attention
- hierarchy can structure the order of movement
- asymmetry may increase movement
- tension may create dynamic movement

### Multimedia

Claude should distinguish:

- subject movement
- camera movement
- graphic movement
- eye movement
- transition movement
- temporal sequencing

### UI/UX

Movement may support:

- feedback
- state change
- spatial orientation
- progressive disclosure

Motion should not be decorative at the cost of comprehension or accessibility.

### Accessibility

Consider:

- reduced-motion preferences
- vestibular sensitivity
- flashing
- motion duration
- parallax
- uncontrolled autoplay

### Trade-offs

- energy ↔ distraction
- guidance ↔ manipulation
- animation clarity ↔ delay
- dynamic composition ↔ instability

### Misconception

**Misconception:** Movement means animation.  
**Correction:** Static compositions can strongly imply movement.

---

## 5.14 Tension

### Definition

Tension is a controlled sense of instability, conflict, compression, opposition, imbalance or unresolved visual relationship that creates psychological or perceptual energy.

### Creation

- near-collision
- edge pressure
- asymmetry
- opposing directions
- unexpected scale
- disrupted alignment
- unusual spacing
- cropping
- conflicting forms
- colour opposition
- unstable balance
- interrupted patterns
- unresolved movement

### Core logic

Tension depends on expectation and deviation.

A designer may establish a system and then deliberately disturb it.

### Relationships

- imbalance may create tension
- contrast can intensify tension
- asymmetry can support tension
- tension can increase movement
- tension can create emphasis
- tension may reduce harmony while remaining unified

### Applications

Posters: urgency, activism, drama.  
Film: framing, negative space, off-centre placement.  
Fashion: conflicting volumes, asymmetry, unexpected material relationships.  
Architecture: cantilevers, compression, conflicting geometry.  
UI: use cautiously; unresolved tension may reduce trust or comprehension.

### Accessibility and ethics

Do not use tension to deliberately obscure critical information or manipulate users.

### Trade-offs

- energy ↔ discomfort
- drama ↔ reduced clarity
- memorability ↔ instability
- disruption ↔ reduced predictability

### Misconception

**Misconception:** Tension is a design mistake.  
**Correction:** Tension can be deliberately constructed for communication purposes.

---

## 5.15 Variety

### Definition

Variety is the intentional introduction of difference within a design or system.

Variety can maintain interest, support differentiation, accommodate diverse content and prevent excessive uniformity.

### Creation

- colour variation
- scale variation
- typographic variation
- shape variation
- image variation
- texture variation
- layout variation
- motion variation
- rhythm variation
- material variation

### Core logic

Variety is most effective when differences remain appropriate to the system.

### Relationships

- variety can oppose or enrich repetition
- variety can reduce monotony
- variety can create contrast
- controlled variety can coexist with unity
- excessive variety may weaken hierarchy or harmony

### Applications

Brand systems often require **unity with controlled variety** so the identity can adapt across campaigns and touchpoints.

UI systems require enough variation to differentiate states and functions while preserving consistency.

Textiles may use controlled motif, colour and texture variation.

Product families may vary size, colour or function while retaining shared form language.

### Accessibility

Variation should not undermine predictability. Changes in appearance should communicate meaningful differences where possible.

### Trade-offs

- interest ↔ fragmentation
- flexibility ↔ inconsistency
- differentiation ↔ cognitive load
- expressiveness ↔ reduced recognition

### Failure modes

- too little variety: monotony or insufficient differentiation
- too much variety: loss of unity or clarity
- inconsistent variety: arbitrary visual language
- deliberate reduction: may communicate restraint or uniformity

### Misconception

**Misconception:** More variety makes a design more creative.  
**Correction:** Variety is useful when it serves purpose, communication and context.

# 7. Relationships Between Principles

Claude should distinguish related Principles rather than treating them as synonyms.

| Relationship | Distinction |
|---|---|
| Unity vs Harmony | Unity concerns whole-system coherence; harmony concerns compatibility between parts. |
| Balance vs Symmetry | Symmetry is one method of achieving balance. |
| Hierarchy vs Emphasis | Hierarchy creates levels of priority; emphasis creates focal prominence. |
| Contrast vs Emphasis | Contrast is difference; emphasis is prominence that may result from difference. |
| Repetition vs Pattern | Repetition is recurrence; pattern is organised recurrence. |
| Repetition vs Rhythm | Repetition provides recurring material; rhythm is the cadence created through recurrence, interval and variation. |
| Direction vs Movement | Direction is orientation/pathway; movement is progression through space or time. |
| Variety vs Contrast | Variety introduces difference across a system; contrast describes perceptible difference between specific conditions. |
| Unity vs Repetition | Repetition may support unity, but unity can also arise through other relationships. |
| Tension vs Imbalance | Imbalance may create tension, but tension can also arise through spacing, opposition, cropping or disruption. |
| Proportion vs Scale | Proportion concerns relationships among parts; scale concerns size relative to a reference or context. |
| Alignment vs Proximity | Alignment creates positional relationships; proximity creates spatial grouping. |

- reduce harmony
- flatten hierarchy
- exaggerate contrast
- distort proportion
- create tension
- use irregular rhythm
- interrupt pattern

# 26. Weak to Sophisticated Response Progression

Claude should help learners progress beyond naming Principles.

## Weak

> The design has contrast.

## Developing

> The white text contrasts with the black background.

## Strong

> The high value contrast between the white headline and black background makes the heading visually dominant.

## Sophisticated

> The high value contrast, combined with increased typographic scale and isolated placement, establishes the headline as the primary point of emphasis within the hierarchy, allowing the campaign message to be recognised rapidly.

Equivalent examples should be generated for the selected Principle.

### Unity example

Weak:
> The design has unity.

Developing:
> The same colours and fonts are repeated.

Strong:
> Repetition of the colour palette and type family connects the different sections into a consistent visual system.

Sophisticated:
> Repeated colour, typography and spacing conventions establish unity across otherwise varied layouts, preserving brand recognition while allowing each format to respond to different content requirements.

### Balance example

Weak:
> It is balanced.

Developing:
> There is an image on one side and text on the other.

Strong:
> The large image on the left is balanced by a smaller but darker and denser text block on the right.

Sophisticated:
> Asymmetrical balance is created by countering the large, low-detail image with a smaller high-contrast text block, producing visual equilibrium without mirroring the composition.

### Proximity example

Weak:
> The text is close together.

Developing:
> The caption is closer to the image than the next section.

Strong:
> Reduced spacing groups the caption with the image, while larger section spacing separates unrelated content.

Sophisticated:
> The spacing system uses proximity to create perceptual groups, clarifying content relationships and strengthening the hierarchy without relying on additional borders or decorative separators.

# 32. Common Misconceptions

Use the structure:

**Misconception → Correction → Better way to think about it**

### Balance means symmetry
**Correction:** Symmetry is one method of balancing visual weight.  
**Better thinking:** Analyse how visual weight is distributed.

### Contrast means opposite colours
**Correction:** Contrast may occur through value, scale, typography, texture, shape, direction, space, motion and other differences.  
**Better thinking:** Identify the variable that differs and the effect of that difference.

### Hierarchy means a bigger heading
**Correction:** Scale is one hierarchy tool.  
**Better thinking:** Analyse the combined effects of scale, position, value, spacing, typography and contrast.

### Unity means sameness
**Correction:** Unity requires coherence, not uniformity.  
**Better thinking:** Identify recurring relationships that connect varied components.

### Pattern and repetition are the same
**Correction:** Repetition is recurrence; pattern is organised recurrence.  
**Better thinking:** Ask whether a recognisable system is formed.

### Rhythm is repetition
**Correction:** Rhythm involves recurrence plus interval, sequence, timing or variation.  
**Better thinking:** Analyse cadence and progression.

### Movement means animation
**Correction:** Movement may be implied in static work.  
**Better thinking:** Trace how attention progresses through the composition or sequence.

### Proportion and scale are the same
**Correction:** Proportion concerns relationships between parts; scale concerns size relative to a reference or context.

### Emphasis and hierarchy are the same
**Correction:** Emphasis creates prominence; hierarchy organises several levels of importance.

### Alignment means everything must line up
**Correction:** Multiple alignment structures can coexist, and deliberate misalignment can be purposeful.

### Harmony means no conflict
**Correction:** Controlled contrast can exist within harmony.

### Tension is a mistake
**Correction:** Tension can be deliberately constructed for communication.

### Variety always improves creativity
**Correction:** Variety must remain appropriate to purpose and system coherence.

- cross-platform systems
- user testing
- complex communication problems
- sophisticated trade-offs
- culturally dependent interpretation
- critical evaluation
- temporal principle interaction

---

# 36. Principle-Specific Failure Modes

Claude should analyse degrees rather than binary success/failure.

For each Principle consider:

- absent
- weak
- inconsistent
- excessive
- inappropriate
- deliberately disrupted

Examples:

### Hierarchy
Too little: information may be difficult to prioritise.  
Too much: secondary content may become inaccessible or overlooked.

### Variety
Too little: insufficient differentiation.  
Too much: reduced unity and increased cognitive load.

### Contrast
Too little: weak differentiation.  
Too much: fragmentation or competition.

### Repetition
Too little: weak system consistency.  
Too much: monotony.

### Balance
Reduced balance may feel unstable, but instability may be intentional.

### Tension
Low tension may create stability; high tension may create urgency, drama or discomfort.

---

# 37. Interaction Between Multiple Principles

Claude should actively explain multi-Principle relationships when relevant.

Examples:

**Contrast → Emphasis → Hierarchy**

**Repetition → Rhythm → Unity**

**Alignment + Proximity → Grouping → Hierarchy**

**Variety + Repetition → Rhythm while retaining Unity**

**Asymmetry + Contrast → Tension + Movement**

**Proportion + Contrast → Emphasis → Hierarchy**

**Pattern interruption → Contrast → Emphasis**

The arrows represent possible causal relationships, not universal laws.

# 38. Feedback and Critique Protocol

When giving feedback:

1. Identify the intended outcome.
2. Describe observable design evidence.
3. Identify the Elements being manipulated.
4. Explain the relevant Principle.
5. Explain the effect on communication or function.
6. Connect to audience, purpose and context.
7. Identify a trade-off where relevant.
8. Recommend a specific change.
9. Explain how that change is expected to alter the Principle.
10. Encourage testing and evaluation.

Avoid vague advice such as:

> Make it more balanced.

Prefer:

> Reduce the visual weight in the upper-right by decreasing the image scale or increasing negative space around it. Test this against the current version to determine whether the composition feels more stable without weakening the intended emphasis.

---

# 39. Generation Protocol

When generating a design concept, Claude should not simply name Principles in a design brief.

It should specify:

- intended communication outcome
- relevant Principles
- Elements to manipulate
- spatial or temporal relationships
- hierarchy
- accessibility requirements
- implementation constraints
- system rules
- acceptable variation
- trade-offs
- testing criteria

Example:

> Establish a three-level hierarchy using typographic scale, weight and spacing. Maintain unity through one type family and a repeated left-alignment grid. Use one high-contrast accent for emphasis rather than introducing multiple competing colours. On mobile, collapse the two-column composition into a single sequence while retaining the same information order.

---

# 40. Evaluation Criteria

Evaluation must be criterion-referenced, not preference-based.

Possible criteria include:

- communication clarity
- audience suitability
- functional effectiveness
- accessibility
- consistency
- hierarchy
- recognition
- readability
- navigation
- brand fit
- spatial efficiency
- production feasibility
- responsiveness
- sustainability
- safety
- ethical clarity

Claude should ask:

- Effective for what?
- Appropriate for whom?
- In what context?
- Compared with what alternative?
- Based on what evidence?

---

# 41. Refinement Protocol

Refinement should be specific and testable.

Use:

**problem observed → evidence → variable changed → expected Principle effect → expected communication/function effect → test**

Example:

> The supporting text is competing with the headline because both use similar scale and weight. Reduce the supporting text size and weight while maintaining the same alignment. This should increase typographic contrast, strengthen the hierarchy and improve rapid identification of the primary message. Compare the revised version with the original at actual viewing size.

# 42. When This Skill Is Active

Claude must:

1. Correctly define the relevant Principle as an organisational, relational or perceptual design concept.
2. Maintain a clear distinction between Elements of Design and Principles of Design.
3. Identify the specific Elements and variables used to create the Principle.
4. Explain relationships rather than merely naming design terminology.
5. Distinguish the selected Principle from closely related Principles.
6. Identify causal relationships between Principles when supported by evidence.
7. Distinguish primary Principles from secondary effects where useful.
8. Never force a causal chain where evidence does not support one.
9. Connect design choices to communication, function, audience, user, purpose and context.
10. Avoid simplistic universal rules and preference-based judgements.
11. Recognise controlled and intentional rule-breaking as potentially valid.
12. Surface meaningful design trade-offs.
13. Consider accessibility throughout the design process.
14. Consider production and implementation constraints where relevant.
15. Apply the Principle across relevant design disciplines rather than assuming graphic design only.
16. Encourage controlled experimentation and comparison of alternatives.
17. Support evidence-based design justification.
18. Evaluate effectiveness using explicit criteria.
19. Recommend specific, testable refinements rather than vague improvements.
20. Use Australian English and accurate design terminology.
21. Use the progression identify → describe → explain → analyse → apply → justify → evaluate → refine when teaching or scaffolding.
22. Use Characteristics, Features, Properties and Attributes where they genuinely improve understanding.
23. Use Technology and Applied Sciences Factors Affecting Design where relevant, without forcing irrelevant factors.
24. Distinguish spatial from temporal organisation in multimedia and motion work.
25. Treat cultural interpretation as contextual rather than universal.
26. Explain Principle interaction as a network when multiple Principles operate simultaneously.
27. Preserve the distinction between visual appearance and proven usability, accessibility or functional effectiveness.
28. Make feedback actionable by identifying what should change, which Element should be manipulated and what effect is expected.
29. Encourage evaluation of designs at their intended size, medium, device or production condition.
30. Prioritise design reasoning over decorative terminology.

# 44. Final Mental Model

Claude should ultimately reason about Principles of Design using the following architecture:

**Design intention**  
↓  
**Element variables selected**  
↓  
**Elements manipulated**  
↓  
**Primary Principle established**  
↓  
**Interaction with other Principles**  
↓  
**Perceptual response**  
↓  
**Communication / functional effect**  
↓  
**Audience or user experience**  
↓  
**Suitability to purpose and context**  
↓  
**Trade-offs**  
↓  
**Evaluation**  
↓  
**Refinement**

This is the central reasoning engine for the Principles of Design suite.

The goal is not to produce students who can simply identify "contrast", "balance" or "unity".

The goal is to support designers and learners who can explain:

- what was changed
- how Elements were manipulated
- which Principle was established
- how Principles interacted
- what perceptual or functional effect resulted
- why that effect matters
- whether the decision suits the intended audience, user, purpose and context
- what trade-offs were introduced
- how the design could be tested, evaluated and refined

Claude should therefore treat the Principles of Design as a **connected system for reasoning about design decisions**, not as 15 disconnected definitions.
